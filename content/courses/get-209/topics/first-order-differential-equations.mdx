## Overview

A wide range of physical laws in mechanics, electrical circuits, heat transfer, and chemical kinetics express relationships between a quantity and its rate of change. This topic develops the complete theory and solution methods for **differential equations of the first order and first degree**: definitions, order, degree, formation of differential equations, variables separable, homogeneous and reducible-to-homogeneous equations, linear and Bernoulli equations, exact equations, and five systematic rules for finding integrating factors of non-exact equations.

### 3.1 Definition

An equation that contains one or more differential coefficients (derivatives) of a dependent variable with respect to one or more independent variables is called a **differential equation**. Representative examples from include:

1. $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{1+x^2}{1-y^2}$
2. $\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2} - 2\dfrac{dy}{dx} - 8y = 0$
3. $\left[1 + \left(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\right)^2\right]^{3/2} = k\,\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}$
4. $x\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x} + y\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial y} = nu$
5. $\dfrac{\partial^2 z}{\partial x\,\partial y} = \dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}$

Equations (1)–(3) involve derivatives with respect to a single independent variable $x$, whereas equations (4)–(5) involve partial derivatives with respect to two independent variables $x$ and $y$. This leads to the fundamental division of the subject.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="Ordinary and Partial Differential Equations">

- **Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE):** A differential equation involving derivatives with respect to a **single** independent variable (e.g. $y$ as a function of $x$ alone).
- **Partial Differential Equation (PDE):** A differential equation involving partial derivatives with respect to **more than one** independent variable (e.g. $u(x,y)$ or $z(x,y)$).

</RuleBox>

### 3.2 Order and Degree of a Differential Equation

Every differential equation is characterized by two positive integers: its **order** and its **degree**.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="Order and Degree">

- **Order:** The order of a differential equation is the order of the **highest differential coefficient (derivative)** present in the equation.
- **Degree:** The degree of a differential equation is the **power (exponent) of the highest-order derivative**, *after the equation has been made free of radicals and fractions* as far as derivatives are concerned.

</RuleBox>

Consider the following three equations:

1. $L\dfrac{d^2q}{dt^2} + R\dfrac{dq}{dt} + \dfrac{q}{C} = E\sin \omega t$ — highest derivative is $\dfrac{d^2q}{dt^2}$ raised to power $1$: **order $2$, degree $1$**.
2. $\cos x\,\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2} + \sin x\left(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\right)^2 + 8y = \tan x$ — highest derivative is $\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}$ raised to power $1$ (the square on $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ does not control the degree): **order $2$, degree $1$**.
3. $\left[1 + \left(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\right)^2\right]^{3} = \left(\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}\right)^{2}$ — obtained by squaring $\left[1 + \left(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\right)^2\right]^{3/2} = \dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}$ to remove the fractional exponent $\tfrac{3}{2}$. Highest derivative is $\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}$ raised to power $2$: **order $2$, degree $2$**.

### 3.3 Formation of Differential Equations

A differential equation can be formed from an ordinary algebraic relation (the *primitive*) containing arbitrary constants by differentiating successively with respect to the independent variable and eliminating all arbitrary constants between the original equation and its derivatives.

<RuleBox kind="working-rule" title="Forming an ODE by Eliminating Arbitrary Constants">

1. Count the number $n$ of independent arbitrary constants in the given relation $F(x, y, c_1, c_2, \dots, c_n) = 0$.
2. Differentiate the relation $n$ times with respect to $x$, producing $n+1$ equations in all.
3. Eliminate the $n$ arbitrary constants from these $n+1$ equations.
4. The resulting differential equation has **order $n$** — equal to the number of arbitrary constants eliminated.

</RuleBox>

### 3.4 Solution of a Differential Equation

In Example 1(b), eliminating the two constants $A$ and $B$ from $y = A\cos x + B\sin x$ gave the second-order equation $\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2} + y = 0$. Conversely, $y = A\cos x + B\sin x$ is called a **solution** (or **primitive**) of $\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2} + y = 0$. Notice that the number of independent arbitrary constants ($2$) equals the order of the differential equation ($2$).

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="Solution (Primitive) of a Differential Equation">

A relation between the dependent variable $y$ and the independent variable $x$, free from derivatives, which satisfies the differential equation identically is called its **solution** or **primitive**.

- The **general (complete) solution** of an $n$-th order ODE contains exactly **$n$ independent arbitrary constants**.
- A **particular solution** is obtained from the general solution by assigning specific numerical values to those constants (for example, via an initial condition such as $y(x_0) = y_0$).

</RuleBox>

### 3.5 Differential Equations of the First Order and First Degree

A differential equation of the first order and first degree has the general form

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = F(x,y) \qquad \text{or equivalently} \qquad M(x,y)\,dx + N(x,y)\,dy = 0.
$$

Because no single universal formula integrates every first-order ODE, solvable equations are grouped into four standard families (together with equations reducible to them):

1. **Equations solvable by separation of variables**
2. **Homogeneous equations** and **equations reducible to homogeneous form**
3. **Linear equations of the first order** and **Bernoulli equations**
4. **Exact differential equations** and **equations reducible to exact form**

### 3.6 Variables Separable

If a first-order differential equation can be rearranged so that all $y$-terms appear solely with $dy$ on one side and all $x$-terms appear solely with $dx$ on the other side,

$$
f(y)\,dy = \phi(x)\,dx,
$$

the variables are said to be **separable**, and the equation is solved directly by integrating both sides.

<RuleBox kind="working-rule" title="Working Rule for Variables Separable">

- **Step 1.** Separate the variables into the form $f(y)\,dy = \phi(x)\,dx$.
- **Step 2.** Integrate both sides:

$$
\int f(y)\,dy = \int \phi(x)\,dx + C
$$

- **Step 3.** Add an arbitrary constant of integration $C$ on the right-hand side (which may be written as $\log C$ when all integrated terms are logarithms).

*Note:* Many equations that are not immediately separable become separable after an algebraic substitution such as $z = ax + by + c$ or $v = xy$ (see Examples 3–5 below).

</RuleBox>

### 3.7 Homogeneous Differential Equations

A differential equation of the form

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{f(x,y)}{\phi(x,y)}
$$

is called **homogeneous** if $f(x,y)$ and $\phi(x,y)$ are homogeneous functions of the **same degree** in $x$ and $y$ — that is, every term in both numerator and denominator has the same total degree (for instance, $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{3xy + y^2}{3x^2 + xy}$, where every term has degree $2$). Equivalently, the right-hand side can be written purely as a function of the ratio $\dfrac{y}{x}$.

<RuleBox kind="working-rule" title="Working Rule for Homogeneous Differential Equations">

To solve a homogeneous equation $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{f(x,y)}{\phi(x,y)}$:

- **Step 1.** Put $y = vx$, so that

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = v + x\frac{dv}{dx}.
$$

- **Step 2.** Substitute $y = vx$ and $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = v + x\dfrac{dv}{dx}$ into the differential equation. All powers of $x$ cancel in the ratio, leaving a separable equation in $v$ and $x$.
- **Step 3.** Separate the variables $v$ and $x$, and integrate both sides.
- **Step 4.** Replace $v$ by $\dfrac{y}{x}$ and simplify.

*(If the equation involves $\dfrac{x}{y}$ more naturally, put $x = vy$ and $\dfrac{dx}{dy} = v + y\dfrac{dv}{dy}$ instead.)*

</RuleBox>

### 3.8 Equations Reducible to Homogeneous Form

Consider a first-order equation with linear numerator and linear denominator containing constant terms:

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{ax + by + c}{Ax + By + C}.
$$

Because of the constants $c$ and $C$, the equation is not homogeneous as written. Its reduction depends on whether the two straight lines $ax + by + c = 0$ and $Ax + By + C = 0$ intersect or are parallel.

<RuleBox kind="working-rule" title="Two Cases for Reducible-to-Homogeneous Equations">

Given $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{ax + by + c}{Ax + By + C}$:

- **Case I ($\dfrac{a}{A} \neq \dfrac{b}{B}$ — intersecting lines):** Shift the origin to the point of intersection $(h, k)$ by setting
  $$
  x = X + h, \qquad y = Y + k \qquad \left(\text{so } \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dY}{dX}\right).
  $$
  Choose the constants $h$ and $k$ to satisfy the simultaneous equations
  $$
  ah + bk + c = 0, \qquad Ah + Bk + C = 0.
  $$
  The constants vanish and the equation becomes homogeneous:
  $$
  \frac{dY}{dX} = \frac{aX + bY}{AX + BY},
  $$
  which is solved by putting $Y = vX$, then substituting back $X = x - h$ and $Y = y - k$.

- **Case II ($\dfrac{a}{A} = \dfrac{b}{B} = \dfrac{1}{m}$ — parallel lines / case of failure):** Here $h$ and $k$ are not finite. Write $A = am$, $B = bm$, so that
  $$
  \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{ax + by + c}{m(ax + by) + C}.
  $$
  Substitute $z = ax + by$ (so $a + b\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{dz}{dx}$). The resulting equation is immediately **variables separable** in $z$ and $x$.

</RuleBox>

### 3.9 Linear Differential Equations

A differential equation is **linear of the first order** (also called *Leibniz's linear equation*) if the dependent variable $y$ and its derivative $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ occur only to the first degree and are never multiplied together. Its standard form is

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} + P y = Q,
$$

where $P$ and $Q$ are functions of $x$ alone (not of $y$) or constants.

To derive its general solution, multiply both sides by the **integrating factor** $e^{\int P\,dx}$:

$$
e^{\int P\,dx}\left(\frac{dy}{dx} + Py\right) = Q\,e^{\int P\,dx}.
$$

By the product rule, the left-hand side is the exact derivative $\dfrac{d}{dx}\!\left(y\,e^{\int P\,dx}\right)$. Integrating both sides with respect to $x$ immediately gives the closed-form solution.

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="Standard Linear ODE, Integrating Factor & Working Rule">

**Standard Form (linear in $y$):**
$$
\frac{dy}{dx} + P(x)\,y = Q(x)
$$

- **Step 1.** Divide through by the coefficient of $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ to bring the equation into the standard form $\dfrac{dy}{dx} + Py = Q$, and identify $P$ and $Q$ (watching signs carefully).
- **Step 2.** Compute the **Integrating Factor**:
  $$
  \text{I.F.} = e^{\int P\,dx}
  $$
- **Step 3.** Write the general solution:
  $$
  y \cdot (\text{I.F.}) = \int Q \cdot (\text{I.F.})\,dx + C
  $$

**Symmetric Form (linear in $x$):** When an equation is not linear in $y$, check whether it is linear in $x$:
$$
\frac{dx}{dy} + P(y)\,x = Q(y) \implies \text{I.F.} = e^{\int P\,dy}, \qquad x \cdot (\text{I.F.}) = \int Q \cdot (\text{I.F.})\,dy + C.
$$

</RuleBox>

### 3.10 Equations Reducible to the Linear Form (Bernoulli Equation)

Many nonlinear first-order equations can be transformed into Leibniz's linear form by a clean change of dependent variable. The archetype is **Bernoulli's equation**:

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} + P y = Q y^n,
$$

where $P$ and $Q$ are functions of $x$ alone (or constants) and $n \neq 0, 1$.

Dividing both sides by $y^n$ produces

$$
y^{-n}\frac{dy}{dx} + P\,y^{1-n} = Q.
$$

Putting $z = y^{1-n} = \dfrac{1}{y^{n-1}}$, we have $\dfrac{dz}{dx} = (1-n)y^{-n}\dfrac{dy}{dx}$, so the equation becomes linear in $z$:

$$
\frac{dz}{dx} + (1 - n)P\,z = (1 - n)Q.
$$

<RuleBox kind="working-rule" title="Reduction of Bernoulli and Generalized Equations to Linear Form">

1. **Bernoulli Equation:** $\dfrac{dy}{dx} + Py = Qy^n$.
   - Divide through by $y^n$: $y^{-n}\dfrac{dy}{dx} + P\,y^{1-n} = Q$.
   - Substitute $z = y^{1-n}$, so $y^{-n}\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{1}{1-n}\dfrac{dz}{dx}$.
   - Solve the resulting linear ODE $\dfrac{dz}{dx} + (1-n)Pz = (1-n)Q$ then restore $y^{1-n}$.
2. **Generalized Form:** $f'(y)\dfrac{dy}{dx} + P(x)\,f(y) = Q(x)$.
   - Substitute $z = f(y)$, so $\dfrac{dz}{dx} = f'(y)\dfrac{dy}{dx}$, giving the linear ODE $\dfrac{dz}{dx} + P(x)\,z = Q(x)$ (see Examples 14–16).
3. **Interchanging Variables:** If an equation is nonlinear in $y$ (or has $x$ appearing only to the first power), rewrite it as $\dfrac{dx}{dy} + P(y)\,x = Q(y)$ (see Examples 18–19).

</RuleBox>

### 3.11 Exact Differential Equation

A differential equation $M(x,y)\,dx + N(x,y)\,dy = 0$ is called **exact** if it can be obtained directly by taking the total differential $du = \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x}dx + \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial y}dy = 0$ of some primitive $u(x,y) = C$ without any further multiplication or algebraic elimination. By symmetry of mixed partial derivatives ($\dfrac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y\,\partial x} = \dfrac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x\,\partial y}$), exactness is governed by a simple partial-derivative test.

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="Exactness Criterion & Method for Solving Exact ODEs">

**Necessary and Sufficient Condition:** The equation $M\,dx + N\,dy = 0$ is **exact** if and only if

$$
\frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial N}{\partial x},
$$

where $\dfrac{\partial M}{\partial y}$ is differentiated holding $x$ constant and $\dfrac{\partial N}{\partial x}$ is differentiated holding $y$ constant.

**Method for Solving Exact Differential Equations:**

- **Step I.** Integrate $M$ with respect to $x$, treating $y$ as a constant.
- **Step II.** Integrate with respect to $y$ **only those terms of $N$ that do not contain $x$**.
- **Step III.** Add the two results and equate to an arbitrary constant:

$$
\int_{(y\text{ const.})} M\,dx + \int (\text{terms of } N \text{ not containing } x)\,dy = C.
$$

</RuleBox>

### 3.12 Equations Reducible to the Exact Equations

When $M\,dx + N\,dy = 0$ fails the exactness test $\left(\dfrac{\partial M}{\partial y} \neq \dfrac{\partial N}{\partial x}\right)$, it can often be made exact by multiplying through by a suitable nonzero function $\mu(x,y)$ called an **integrating factor (I.F.)**. Once multiplied by the integrating factor, the transformed equation satisfies $\dfrac{\partial (\mu M)}{\partial y} = \dfrac{\partial (\mu N)}{\partial x}$ and is solved as an exact equation. Below are **five systematic rules** for finding integrating factors.

#### Rule I — Integrating Factor Depending on $x$ Alone

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="Rule I (I.F. a function of x alone)">

If the quotient

$$
\frac{\dfrac{\partial M}{\partial y} - \dfrac{\partial N}{\partial x}}{N} = f(x)
$$

is a function of $x$ alone (or a constant), then

$$
\text{I.F.} = e^{\int f(x)\,dx}.
$$

</RuleBox>

#### Rule IV — Integrating Factor of the Form $x^h y^k$

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="Rule IV (I.F. of the form xʰ yᵏ)">

$$

$$

$$
\frac{m + h + 1}{a} = \frac{n + k + 1}{b} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \frac{m' + h + 1}{a'} = \frac{n' + k + 1}{b'}.
$$

## Common Exam Pitfalls

<Callout variant="warning" title="Key Pitfalls to Avoid in First-Order ODE Exams">

- **Determining degree before clearing radicals:** Never read off the degree of an ODE while fractional exponents or radicals surround derivatives. For example, $\left[1 + \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2\right]^{3/2} = \frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$ has degree **2** (not 1), because squaring both sides yields $\left(\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}\right)^2$.
- **Forgetting to normalize $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ in linear ODEs:** Before computing $\text{I.F.} = e^{\int P\,dx}$, the coefficient of $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ **must be $+1$**. Dividing $(x+1)\frac{dy}{dx} - y = e^x(x+1)^2$ by $(x+1)$ first is mandatory.
- **Sign errors in Rules I and II:** Rule I uses $\dfrac{M_y - N_x}{N} = f(x)$, while Rule II reverses the numerator to $\dfrac{N_x - M_y}{M} = f(y)$. Flipping the sign produces the reciprocal of the true integrating factor, which fails to make the equation exact.
- **Double-counting $x$-dependent terms in exact integration:** In $\int M\,dx + \int (\text{terms of } N \text{ free of } x)\,dy = C$, omit **every** term of $N$ that contains $x$ — those cross-terms are already accounted for inside $\int M\,dx$.
- **Missing linear-in-$x$ equations:** If an ODE looks hopeless as $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$, check $\dfrac{dx}{dy}$ (like Examples 18 and 19) before trying complicated substitutions.

</Callout>

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| — | Variables separable | $f(y)\,dy = \phi(x)\,dx$ | $\displaystyle \int f(y)\,dy = \int \phi(x)\,dx + C$ |
| — | Homogeneous | $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{f(x,y)}{\phi(x,y)}$ (same degree) | $y = vx,\quad \dfrac{dy}{dx} = v + x\dfrac{dv}{dx}$ |
| — | Reducible to homogeneous ($\frac{a}{A} \neq \frac{b}{B}$) | $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{ax+by+c}{Ax+By+C}$ | $x = X+h,\; y = Y+k$ with $ah+bk+c=0,\; Ah+Bk+C=0$ |
| — | Reducible to homogeneous ($\frac{a}{A} = \frac{b}{B}$) | $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{ax+by+c}{m(ax+by)+C}$ | $z = ax + by$ (becomes separable) |
| — | Linear in $y$ | $\dfrac{dy}{dx} + Py = Q$ | $\text{I.F.} = e^{\int P\,dx},\quad y(\text{I.F.}) = \displaystyle \int Q(\text{I.F.})\,dx + C$ |
| — | Linear in $x$ | $\dfrac{dx}{dy} + Px = Q$ | $\text{I.F.} = e^{\int P\,dy},\quad x(\text{I.F.}) = \displaystyle \int Q(\text{I.F.})\,dy + C$ |
| — | Bernoulli | $\dfrac{dy}{dx} + Py = Qy^n$ | Divide by $y^n$, put $z = y^{1-n}$ |
| — | Exact | $M\,dx + N\,dy = 0,\; \dfrac{\partial M}{\partial y} = \dfrac{\partial N}{\partial x}$ | $\displaystyle \int_{(y\text{ const.})} M\,dx + \int (N\text{ free of } x)\,dy = C$ |
| — | Non-exact (I.F. in $x$) | $\dfrac{M_y - N_x}{N} = f(x)$ | $\text{I.F.} = e^{\int f(x)\,dx}$ |
| — | Non-exact (I.F. in $y$) | $\dfrac{N_x - M_y}{M} = f(y)$ | $\text{I.F.} = e^{\int f(y)\,dy}$ |
| — | Non-exact ($y f_1(xy), x f_2(xy)$) | $y f_1(xy)\,dx + x f_2(xy)\,dy = 0$ | $\text{I.F.} = \dfrac{1}{Mx - Ny}$ ($Mx - Ny \neq 0$) |
