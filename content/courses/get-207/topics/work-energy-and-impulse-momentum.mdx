## Overview

Previously, every kinetics problem went through $\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$: find the acceleration, then integrate with kinematics to get velocities and positions. This topic integrates Newton's second law *once and for all* to obtain two new methods that skip the acceleration entirely:

- the **method of work and energy**, which relates **force, mass, velocity and displacement**, and
- the **method of impulse and momentum**, which relates **force, mass, velocity and time**, and is the only practical tool for **impact**.

Section 13.15 then shows how to choose among $\sum \mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$, work–energy and impulse–momentum, and how to chain them (energy → impact → energy) in a single problem. Section 13.9 (motion under a conservative central force, space mechanics) is outside the scope of these notes and is only summarized.

---

### 13.1 Introduction

The advantage of the two new methods is that **the acceleration never has to be determined**. Work and energy is a *scalar* method and is ideal when forces are known as functions of position; impulse and momentum is a *vector* method and is ideal when forces are known as functions of time or act for very short times (impulsive forces).

The chapter is organized as follows: work of a force, kinetic energy and the principle of work and energy, power and efficiency, potential energy and conservative forces, conservation of energy, the principle of impulse and momentum, impulsive motion, impact, and problems combining energy and momentum.

### 13.2 Work of a Force

Let a particle move from $A$ to a neighboring point $A'$; the small vector $d\mathbf{r}$ joining them is the **displacement**. The work of a force $\mathbf{F}$ during this displacement is the scalar product of the force and the displacement.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="Work of a force">

$$
dU = \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} \tag{13.1}
$$

With $F$ and $ds$ the magnitudes of the force and displacement and $\alpha$ the angle between $\mathbf{F}$ and $d\mathbf{r}$:

$$
dU = F\,ds\cos\alpha \tag{13.1$'$}
$$

$$
dU = F_x\,dx + F_y\,dy + F_z\,dz \tag{13.1$''$}
$$

- Work is a **scalar** — it has magnitude and sign but no direction. $dU > 0$ if $\alpha$ is acute, $dU < 0$ if $\alpha$ is obtuse, $dU = 0$ if $\mathbf{F} \perp d\mathbf{r}$.
- Units: $\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$ = **joule (J)** in SI; $\text{ft}\cdot\text{lb}$ or $\text{in}\cdot\text{lb}$ in U.S. units. $1\,\text{ft}\cdot\text{lb} = (0.3048\,\text{m})(4.448\,\text{N}) = 1.356\,\text{J}$.
- The *moment* of a force is also expressed in $\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$, but must **not** be written in joules — a moment is not a form of energy.

</RuleBox>

For a **finite** displacement from $A_1$ to $A_2$, integrate along the path:

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="Work during a finite displacement">

$$
U_{1\to 2} = \int_{A_1}^{A_2} \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} \tag{13.2}
$$

$$
U_{1\to 2} = \int_{s_1}^{s_2} (F\cos\alpha)\,ds = \int_{s_1}^{s_2} F_t\,ds \tag{13.2$'$}
$$

$$
U_{1\to 2} = \int_{A_1}^{A_2} (F_x\,dx + F_y\,dy + F_z\,dz) \tag{13.2$''$}
$$

The work $U_{1\to2}$ equals the **area under the curve** of $F_t = F\cos\alpha$ plotted against the path coordinate $s$).

</RuleBox>

The following special cases cover almost every problem in the chapter.

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="Work of common forces">

**Constant force in rectilinear motion**), $\alpha$ = angle between the force and the direction of motion, $\Delta x$ = displacement from $A_1$ to $A_2$:

$$
U_{1\to 2} = (F\cos\alpha)\,\Delta x \tag{13.3}
$$

**Force of gravity** (y axis upward, $F_x = 0$, $F_y = -W$, $F_z = 0$):

$$
U_{1\to 2} = -\int_{y_1}^{y_2} W\,dy = W y_1 - W y_2 \tag{13.4}
$$

$$
U_{1\to 2} = -W(y_2 - y_1) = -W\,\Delta y \tag{13.4$'$}
$$

The work of the weight is the product of $W$ and the vertical displacement of the center of gravity; it is **positive when the body moves down** ($\Delta y < 0$) and is independent of the path.

**Force exerted by a spring** (deflection $x$ measured from the undeformed position, spring constant $k$ in N/m, kN/m, lb/ft or lb/in.):

$$
F = kx \tag{13.5}
$$

$$
U_{1\to 2} = -\int_{x_1}^{x_2} kx\,dx = \tfrac12 k x_1^2 - \tfrac12 k x_2^2 \tag{13.6}
$$

$$
U_{1\to 2} = -\tfrac12 (F_1 + F_2)\,\Delta x \tag{13.6$'$}
$$

The spring's work is positive when $x_2 < x_1$, i.e., when the spring is **returning** to its undeformed position. Eq. (13.6$'$) is the area of the trapezoid under the $F$–$x$ line and is usually less error-prone with units.

**Gravitational force** (particle $m$ moving from $r_1$ to $r_2$ relative to a fixed mass $M$):

$$
U_{1\to 2} = \frac{GMm}{r_2} - \frac{GMm}{r_1} \tag{13.7}
$$

For the earth, $GMm = WR^2$ with $R = 6.37\times 10^6\,\text{m}$ (3960 mi) and $W$ the weight at the earth's surface.

</RuleBox>

<RuleBox kind="note" title="Forces which do no work">

Forces applied to **fixed points** ($ds = 0$) or acting **perpendicular to the displacement** ($\cos\alpha = 0$) do no work. Examples: the reaction at a frictionless pin when the supported body rotates about the pin; the reaction at a frictionless surface when the body moves along it; the reaction at a roller moving along its track; and the weight of a body whose center of gravity moves horizontally.

The relation $F = kx$ is strictly a static relation; using it in kinetics is accurate as long as the mass of the spring is small compared with the other masses in motion.

</RuleBox>

### 13.3 Kinetic Energy of a Particle. Principle of Work and Energy

Write Newton's second law in tangential components, $F_t = ma_t = m\,dv/dt$, and use $v = ds/dt$:

$$
F_t = m\frac{dv}{ds}\frac{ds}{dt} = mv\frac{dv}{ds} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad F_t\,ds = mv\,dv
$$

Integrating from $A_1$ ($s_1$, $v_1$) to $A_2$ ($s_2$, $v_2$):

$$
\int_{s_1}^{s_2} F_t\,ds = m\int_{v_1}^{v_2} v\,dv = \tfrac12 m v_2^2 - \tfrac12 m v_1^2 \tag{13.8}
$$

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="Kinetic energy and the principle of work and energy">

The **kinetic energy** of a particle of mass $m$ and speed $v$ is the scalar

$$
T = \tfrac12 m v^2 \tag{13.9}
$$

The **principle of work and energy**: when a particle moves from $A_1$ to $A_2$ under a force $\mathbf{F}$, the work of $\mathbf{F}$ equals the change in kinetic energy,

$$
U_{1\to 2} = T_2 - T_1 \tag{13.10}
$$

or, rearranged,

$$
T_1 + U_{1\to 2} = T_2 \tag{13.11}
$$

- $U_{1\to2}$ is the **algebraic sum** of the work of **all** forces acting on the particle.
- Like $\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$, the principle holds only in a **newtonian frame**; $v$ must be measured in such a frame.
- $T$ is always **positive**, whatever the direction of motion. $T$ equals the work needed to bring the particle from rest to speed $v$, and also its capacity to do work as it is brought to rest.
- Units: $\text{kg}(\text{m/s})^2 = \text{N}\cdot\text{m} = \text{J}$; $(\text{lb}\cdot\text{s}^2/\text{ft})(\text{ft/s})^2 = \text{ft}\cdot\text{lb}$.

</RuleBox>

### 13.4 Applications of the Principle of Work and Energy

**Pendulum example).** A bob of weight $W$ on a cord of length $l$ is released from rest at the horizontal position $A_1$. On the free-body diagram the cord force $\mathbf{P}$ is normal to the path and does no work; only $W$ works, through a vertical drop $l$: $U_{1\to2} = Wl$. Then

$$
T_1 + U_{1\to2} = T_2:\qquad 0 + Wl = \tfrac12\frac{W}{g}v_2^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_2 = \sqrt{2gl}
$$

— the speed of a body falling freely through $l$.

<DiagramFigure
  src="/diagrams/get-207/work-energy-and-impulse-momentum-fig-13-8.png"
  caption="(a) Pendulum released from A₁; (b) free-body diagram of the bob: only W does work."
/>

The example shows the three **advantages** of the method: (1) no need to find the acceleration at an intermediate position and integrate; (2) all quantities are scalars and add directly; (3) forces which do no work drop out.

The same features are **limitations**: work–energy cannot give an acceleration directly, and a force normal to the path (which does no work) must be found with Newton's second law. For the cord tension at $A_2$), $\sum F_n = ma_n$ gives

$$
P - W = ma_n = \frac{W}{g}\frac{v_2^2}{l} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad P = W + \frac{W}{g}\frac{2gl}{l} = 3W
$$

<DiagramFigure
  src="/diagrams/get-207/work-energy-and-impulse-momentum-fig-13-9.png"
  caption="Free-body diagram and kinetic diagram of the bob at A₂ (used with ΣFₙ = maₙ)."
/>

<RuleBox kind="working-rule" title="Systems of particles and friction">

1. **Several particles:** Eq. (13.11) may be written for each particle separately, or as a **single equation** $T_1 + U_{1\to2} = T_2$ in which $T$ is the arithmetic sum of all kinetic energies (all positive) and $U_{1\to2}$ is the work of all forces, including action–reaction pairs between the particles.
2. **Inextensible cords and links:** the work of the forces a cord or link exerts on the two bodies it connects **cancels**, because their points of application move through equal distances.
3. **Friction:** friction forces always oppose the displacement of the body on which they act, so their work is **always negative**. This work is energy dissipated into heat.

</RuleBox>

<RuleBox kind="working-rule" title="Procedure — method of work and energy">

1. **Compute the work of each force** with Eqs. (13.2), (13.3), (13.4$'$), (13.6) or (13.7). Zero for forces perpendicular to the displacement; negative for friction.
2. **Compute the kinetic energies** $T_1$ and $T_2$ from Eq. (13.9).
3. **Substitute into $T_1 + U_{1\to2} = T_2$** and solve for the single unknown.
4. If a **normal force** is required where the speed is now known, use $a_n = v^2/\rho$ and $\sum F_n = ma_n$.

</RuleBox>

### 13.5 Power and Efficiency

**Power** is the time rate at which work is done. In choosing a motor, power matters far more than the total work: a small motor and a power plant can do the same work, but at vastly different rates.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="Power">

Average power over $\Delta t$ is $\Delta U/\Delta t$; letting $\Delta t \to 0$,

$$
\text{Power} = \frac{dU}{dt} \tag{13.12}
$$

Substituting $dU = \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}$ and $d\mathbf{r}/dt = \mathbf{v}$ (velocity of the point of application of $\mathbf{F}$):

$$
\text{Power} = \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{v} \tag{13.13}
$$

Units: $1\,\text{W} = 1\,\text{J/s} = 1\,\text{N}\cdot\text{m/s}$. U.S.: $\text{ft}\cdot\text{lb/s}$ or horsepower, $1\,\text{hp} = 550\,\text{ft}\cdot\text{lb/s}$. Since $1\,\text{ft}\cdot\text{lb/s} = 1.356\,\text{W}$, $\;1\,\text{hp} = 550(1.356\,\text{W}) = 746\,\text{W} = 0.746\,\text{kW}$.

</RuleBox>

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="Mechanical efficiency">

$$
\eta = \frac{\text{output work}}{\text{input work}} \tag{13.14}
$$

If work is done at a constant rate, the same ratio applies to rates:

$$
\eta = \frac{\text{power output}}{\text{power input}} \tag{13.15}
$$

Because of friction losses, $\eta < 1$ always. For machines converting electric or thermal energy into mechanical energy, Eq. (13.15) gives the **overall efficiency**, which accounts for all losses. Power output and power input must be expressed in the **same units**.

</RuleBox>

### 13.6 Potential Energy

The work of the weight, Eq. (13.4), depends only on the initial and final values of the function $Wy$ — not on the path. This function is called the **potential energy with respect to gravity**.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="Gravitational potential energy (constant W)">

$$
U_{1\to 2} = (V_g)_1 - (V_g)_2 \qquad\text{with}\qquad V_g = Wy \tag{13.16}
$$

- If $V_g$ **increases** (body rises), the work of $W$ is **negative**; if the work of $W$ is positive, $V_g$ decreases.
- Only **changes** in $V_g$ matter, so the **datum** from which $y$ is measured is arbitrary.
- Valid only while $W$ is constant, i.e., for displacements small compared with the earth's radius.

</RuleBox>

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="Potential energy for a variable gravitational force and for a spring">

From Eq. (13.7), when the variation of gravity with distance $r$ from the earth's center cannot be neglected (space vehicles):

$$
V_g = -\frac{GMm}{r} \tag{13.17}
$$

$$
V_g = -\frac{WR^2}{r} \tag{13.17$'$}
$$

with $R$ the earth's radius and $W$ the weight at the surface; $r$ is measured from the center of the earth ($r \ge R$). $V_g$ is always negative and approaches zero as $r \to \infty$.

For a spring, from Eq. (13.6):

$$
U_{1\to 2} = (V_e)_1 - (V_e)_2 \qquad\text{with}\qquad V_e = \tfrac12 k x^2 \tag{13.18}
$$

The deflection $x$ **must be measured from the undeformed position** of the spring. Eq. (13.18) remains valid even when the spring rotates about its fixed end): only the initial and final deflections matter.

</RuleBox>

The concept extends to any force whose work is **independent of the path** of its point of application; such forces are called **conservative forces**.

---

### 13.7 Conservative Forces

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="Properties of a conservative force">

A force $\mathbf{F}$ is **conservative** if its work $U_{1\to2}$ is independent of the path from $A_1$ to $A_2$. Then a **potential function** $V(x,y,z)$ exists with

$$
U_{1\to 2} = V(x_1, y_1, z_1) - V(x_2, y_2, z_2) \tag{13.19}
$$

$$
U_{1\to 2} = V_1 - V_2 \tag{13.19$'$}
$$

Over any **closed path** the work is zero:

$$
\oint \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = 0 \tag{13.20}
$$

The elementary work is an **exact differential**:

$$
dU = -dV(x, y, z) \tag{13.21}
$$

so that

$$
F_x = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial x} \qquad F_y = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial y} \qquad F_z = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial z} \tag{13.22}
$$

$$
\mathbf{F} = -\operatorname{grad} V \tag{13.23}
$$

A necessary condition is that $\mathbf{F}$ depends **only on the position** of its point of application. Conversely, if $\mathbf{F}$ satisfies any one of Eqs. (13.19)–(13.23), it is conservative.

</RuleBox>

### 13.8 Conservation of Energy

When a particle moves under conservative forces only, substitute $U_{1\to2} = V_1 - V_2$ (Eq. 13.19$'$) into $U_{1\to2} = T_2 - T_1$:

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="Principle of conservation of energy">

$$
T_1 + V_1 = T_2 + V_2 \tag{13.24}
$$

When a particle moves under the action of conservative forces, the sum $E = T + V$, its **total mechanical energy**, remains constant.

- **Pendulum):** with the datum at $A_2$, $T_1 + V_1 = 0 + Wl$ and $T_2 + V_2 = \tfrac12 \dfrac{W}{g}(2gl) + 0 = Wl$. The energy is all potential at $A_1$, all kinetic at $A_2$, all potential again at $A_3$.
- If the only forces are the **weight and a normal reaction**, the speed is the same at all points at the same level, whatever the shape of the path.
- **Friction forces are nonconservative**: their work depends on the path and is always negative, so the total mechanical energy of a system with friction **decreases** (it is converted into heat). Considering all forms of energy, the total energy is always conserved.

</RuleBox>

<RuleBox kind="working-rule" title="Procedure — conservation of energy">

1. **Check that all forces are conservative.** If friction or another nonconservative force does work, use $T_1 + U_{1\to2} = T_2$ instead.
2. **Kinetic energy** $T = \tfrac12 mv^2$ at each end of the path.
3. **Potential energy** of every force at each end: $V_g = Wy$ (choose a datum), $V_g = -GMm/r$ for space problems, $V_e = \tfrac12 kx^2$ with $x$ from the **undeformed** length.
4. **Substitute into $T_1 + V_1 = T_2 + V_2$.** For a normal force (e.g., contact with a loop), add $\sum F_n = ma_n$.

</RuleBox>

### 13.9 Motion Under a Conservative Central Force. Application to Space Mechanics

*(Outside the scope of these notes.)* When a particle moves under a force that is both **central** and **conservative** (e.g., a space vehicle under the earth's gravity), both its angular momentum about the center of force and its total energy are conserved. standard mechanics combine $r_0 m v_0 \sin\phi_0 = r m v \sin\phi$ (13.25) with $\tfrac12 mv_0^2 - GMm/r_0 = \tfrac12 mv^2 - GMm/r$ (13.26) to find speeds and the maximum and minimum distances of orbits (Sample Probs. 13.8 and 13.9).

### 13.10 Principle of Impulse and Momentum

Newton's second law in terms of the linear momentum $m\mathbf{v}$ is

$$
\mathbf{F} = \frac{d}{dt}(m\mathbf{v}) \tag{13.27}
$$

Multiplying by $dt$ and integrating from $t_1$ to $t_2$: $\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\mathbf{F}\,dt = m\mathbf{v}_2 - m\mathbf{v}_1$.

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="Principle of impulse and momentum">

$$
m\mathbf{v}_1 + \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \mathbf{F}\,dt = m\mathbf{v}_2 \tag{13.28}
$$

The integral is the **linear impulse** of $\mathbf{F}$:

$$
\mathbf{Imp}_{1\to2} = \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\mathbf{F}\,dt = \mathbf{i}\int_{t_1}^{t_2} F_x\,dt + \mathbf{j}\int_{t_1}^{t_2} F_y\,dt + \mathbf{k}\int_{t_1}^{t_2} F_z\,dt \tag{13.29}
$$

Each component of the impulse equals the **area under the $F$–$t$ curve**). For a constant force, $\mathbf{Imp} = \mathbf{F}(t_2 - t_1)$. Units: $\text{N}\cdot\text{s} = \text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$ (SI) and $\text{lb}\cdot\text{s}$ (U.S.) — the same units as momentum.

$$
m\mathbf{v}_1 + \mathbf{Imp}_{1\to2} = m\mathbf{v}_2 \tag{13.30}
$$

Momentum and impulse are **vectors** (unlike work and kinetic energy), so in practice Eq. (13.30) is replaced by its components:

$$
(mv_x)_1 + \int_{t_1}^{t_2}F_x\,dt = (mv_x)_2 \qquad (mv_y)_1 + \int_{t_1}^{t_2}F_y\,dt = (mv_y)_2 \qquad (mv_z)_1 + \int_{t_1}^{t_2}F_z\,dt = (mv_z)_2 \tag{13.31}
$$

With several forces:

$$
m\mathbf{v}_1 + \sum \mathbf{Imp}_{1\to2} = m\mathbf{v}_2 \tag{13.32}
$$

</RuleBox>

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="Systems of particles — conservation of momentum">

For two or more particles, add the momenta and impulses of all particles:

$$
\sum m\mathbf{v}_1 + \sum \mathbf{Imp}_{1\to2} = \sum m\mathbf{v}_2 \tag{13.33}
$$

The impulses of the action–reaction forces between the particles **always cancel** (equal, opposite, same time interval), so only **external** impulses appear. If the sum of the external forces is zero:

$$
\sum m\mathbf{v}_1 = \sum m\mathbf{v}_2 \tag{13.34}
$$

— the **total momentum is conserved**. Example: two boats initially at rest pulled together (water resistance neglected): $0 = m_A\mathbf{v}'_A + m_B\mathbf{v}'_B$, so they move toward each other with speeds inversely proportional to their masses.

Note the contrast: the sum of the **impulses** of internal forces is always zero, but the sum of their **work** is zero only in special cases (e.g., inextensible cords or links).

</RuleBox>

### 13.11 Impulsive Motion

A force acting during a very short interval but large enough to produce a definite change in momentum is an **impulsive force**; the resulting motion is **impulsive motion** (e.g., a bat striking a ball, the diagram).

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="Impulsive motion">

$$
m\mathbf{v}_1 + \sum \mathbf{F}\,\Delta t = m\mathbf{v}_2 \tag{13.35}
$$

- **Nonimpulsive forces may be neglected**: the weight, the force of a spring, or any force known to be small compared with the impulsive forces ($W\Delta t \approx 0$).
- **Unknown reactions** may or may not be impulsive — include their impulses unless proved negligible (e.g., the reactions of a batter's hands are not negligible if the ball is hit incorrectly).

For several particles:

$$
\sum m\mathbf{v}_1 + \sum \mathbf{F}\,\Delta t = \sum m\mathbf{v}_2 \tag{13.36}
$$

where only **impulsive external** forces appear. If all external forces are nonimpulsive, Eq. (13.36) reduces to Eq. (13.34): the total momentum is conserved — e.g., when two freely moving particles collide. **Total energy is generally not conserved** in such collisions.

</RuleBox>

<RuleBox kind="working-rule" title="Procedure — impulse and momentum">

1. Draw an **impulse–momentum diagram**: initial momentum $m\mathbf{v}_1$ + impulses of all forces = final momentum $m\mathbf{v}_2$.
2. Compute each impulse: constant force over finite time, $\mathbf{F}(t_2 - t_1)$; variable force, $\int \mathbf{F}(t)\,dt$; impulsive force, $\mathbf{F}\,\Delta t$ (zero for nonimpulsive forces).
3. Write the **component equations** (two in plane problems) and solve for the two unknowns — a time, a velocity and an impulse, or an average impulsive force.
4. For several particles, draw a diagram for each particle; a diagram including **all** particles eliminates internal impulses.

</RuleBox>

### 13.12 Impact

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="Classification of impacts">

- **Impact:** a collision between two bodies occurring in a very small interval of time, during which they exert relatively large forces on each other.
- **Line of impact:** the common normal to the surfaces in contact during the impact.
- **Central impact:** the mass centers of both bodies lie on the line of impact; otherwise the impact is **eccentric** (rigid bodies, Sec. 17.12).
- **Direct impact:** the velocities of both particles are directed along the line of impact).
- **Oblique impact:** either or both particles move along a line other than the line of impact).

</RuleBox>

### 13.13 Direct Central Impact

Particles $A$ and $B$ move along the same line with $v_A > v_B$. During the **period of deformation** they deform until they share a common velocity $u$; then, during the **period of restitution**, they either regain their shape or stay permanently deformed). No external impulsive force acts on the pair, so total momentum is conserved:

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="Conservation of momentum and the coefficient of restitution">

$$
m_A v_A + m_B v_B = m_A v'_A + m_B v'_B \tag{13.37}
$$

(scalars; positive to the right). Applying impulse–momentum to $A$ alone with $P$ = force during deformation and $R$ = force during restitution:

$$
m_A v_A - \int P\,dt = m_A u \tag{13.38}
$$

$$
m_A u - \int R\,dt = m_A v'_A \tag{13.39}
$$

The **coefficient of restitution** is the ratio of the restitution impulse to the deformation impulse:

$$
e = \frac{\int R\,dt}{\int P\,dt} \tag{13.40}
$$

$0 \le e \le 1$; it depends on the materials and also on impact velocity, shape and size. Eliminating the impulses:

$$
e = \frac{u - v'_A}{v_A - u} \tag{13.41}
$$

$$
e = \frac{v'_B - u}{u - v_B} \tag{13.42}
$$

Adding numerators and denominators gives the working relation:

$$
v'_B - v'_A = e(v_A - v_B) \tag{13.43}
$$

The **relative velocity after impact** equals $e$ times the relative velocity before impact. Solve (13.37) and (13.43) simultaneously. A negative value means the velocity is directed to the left.

</RuleBox>

<DiagramFigure
  src="/diagrams/get-207/work-energy-and-impulse-momentum-fig-13-21.png"
  caption="Direct central impact: (a) before impact; (b) at maximum deformation (common velocity u); (c) after impact."
/>

<RuleBox kind="note" title="Two particular cases">

1. **$e = 0$, perfectly plastic impact:** $v'_B = v'_A = v'$; there is no restitution and the particles stay together:

$$
m_A v_A + m_B v_B = (m_A + m_B)v' \tag{13.44}
$$

2. **$e = 1$, perfectly elastic impact:**

$$
v'_B - v'_A = v_A - v_B \tag{13.45}
$$

The particles separate with the same relative speed with which they approached. Writing (13.37) and (13.45) as $m_A(v_A - v'_A) = m_B(v'_B - v_B)$ (13.37$'$) and $v_A + v'_A = v_B + v'_B$ (13.45$'$) and multiplying gives

$$
\tfrac12 m_A v_A^2 + \tfrac12 m_B v_B^2 = \tfrac12 m_A (v'_A)^2 + \tfrac12 m_B (v'_B)^2 \tag{13.46}
$$

— **kinetic energy is conserved only when $e = 1$**. For $e \ne 1$ some kinetic energy is lost (to heat and elastic waves).

</RuleBox>

### 13.14 Oblique Central Impact

When the velocities are not along the line of impact), $\mathbf{v}'_A$ and $\mathbf{v}'_B$ are unknown in both magnitude and direction: **four** equations are needed. Take the **$n$ axis along the line of impact** (common normal) and the **$t$ axis along the common tangent**. For perfectly smooth, frictionless particles the only impulses are the internal forces along $n$).

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="Oblique central impact of two free particles">

1. The $t$ component of the momentum of **each particle separately** is conserved, so its $t$ velocity component is unchanged:

$$
(v_A)_t = (v'_A)_t \qquad (v_B)_t = (v'_B)_t \tag{13.47}
$$

2. The $n$ component of the **total** momentum is conserved:

$$
m_A(v_A)_n + m_B(v_B)_n = m_A(v'_A)_n + m_B(v'_B)_n \tag{13.48}
$$

3. The $n$ component of the relative velocity after impact equals $e$ times its value before impact:

$$
(v'_B)_n - (v'_A)_n = e\left[(v_A)_n - (v_B)_n\right] \tag{13.49}
$$

</RuleBox>

<DiagramFigure
  src="/diagrams/get-207/work-energy-and-impulse-momentum-fig-13-24.png"
  caption="Oblique central impact: the impulses FΔt and −FΔt act only along the line of impact n."
/>

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="One particle constrained">

If block $A$ is constrained to move on a horizontal frictionless surface and ball $B$ is free, the surface exerts an external impulse $\mathbf{F}_{\text{ext}}\Delta t$ on $A$ (vertical). There are three unknowns ($v'_A$, which is horizontal, and the magnitude and direction of $\mathbf{v}'_B$) and three equations:

1. The $t$ component of the momentum of ball $B$ is conserved:

$$
(v_B)_t = (v'_B)_t \tag{13.50}
$$

2. The **horizontal ($x$) component of the total momentum** of $A$ and $B$ is conserved:

$$
m_A v_A + m_B(v_B)_x = m_A v'_A + m_B(v'_B)_x \tag{13.51}
$$

3. The relative-velocity relation (13.49) along $n$ **still holds**. standard mechanics prove this by applying impulse–momentum to $A$ during deformation and restitution, $m_A v_A - (\int P\,dt)\cos\theta = m_A u$ (13.52) and $m_A u - (\int R\,dt)\cos\theta = m_A v'_A$ (13.53), which with (13.40) give

$$
e = \frac{u_n - (v'_A)_n}{(v_A)_n - u_n} \tag{13.54}
$$

identical in form to (13.41). The result extends to the case where both particles are constrained.

</RuleBox>

### 13.15 Problems Involving Energy and Momentum

There are now three methods: $\sum\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$, work and energy, and impulse and momentum. Choose the one best suited to each **part** of a problem.

<RuleBox kind="working-rule" title="Choosing the method">

1. **Work and energy** is usually faster than $\sum\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$ when forces depend on position — but it cannot give an **acceleration** or a **normal force**; supplement it with $\sum\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$ for those.
2. With **no impulsive forces**, $\sum\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$ is about as fast as impulse–momentum, and work–energy (if it applies) is faster still.
3. For **impact**, impulse–momentum is the **only practicable** method; work–energy cannot be used because an impact (unless perfectly elastic) loses mechanical energy.
4. **Multi-phase problems** with conservative forces except for a short impact: split into parts — energy before the impact, momentum plus the relative-velocity relation during the impact, energy after the impact. Use $\sum F_n = ma_n$ if a cord tension or normal force is required.

</RuleBox>

**Two-pendulum example).** Pendulum $A$ released from rest at $A_1$ strikes pendulum $B$ at rest; find the angle $\theta$ through which $B$ swings:

1. $A_1 \to A_2$: conservation of energy gives $(v_A)_2$.
2. Impact: conservation of total momentum and $(v_B)_3 - (v_A)_3 = e[(v_A)_2 - 0]$ give $(v_A)_3$ and $(v_B)_3$.
3. $B_3 \to B_4$: conservation of energy gives the rise $y_4$, then $\theta$ by trigonometry.

## Common Exam Pitfalls

<Callout variant="warning" title="Frequent Mistakes in Energy and Momentum Problems">

1. **Spring deflection from the wrong reference.** $V_e = \tfrac12kx^2$ and $U = \tfrac12kx_1^2 - \tfrac12kx_2^2$ require $x$ measured from the **undeformed** length. In Sample Prob. 13.6 the spring is already stretched $4\,\text{in.}$ in position 1; in Sample Prob. 13.17 the pan already compresses the spring by $x_3 = W_B/k$.
2. **Sign of friction work.** Friction always does **negative** work — on the way back the friction work is still negative even though the gravity or spring work changes sign.
3. **Using conservation of energy across an impact.** Mechanical energy is lost in any impact with $e < 1$. Use momentum + restitution for the impact, energy only before and after.
4. **Forgetting that work–energy cannot give normal forces.** For tension in a cord or contact force on a loop, add $\sum F_n = mv^2/\rho$ (Sample Probs. 13.4 and 13.7).
5. **Writing $e$ with the wrong velocity order.** $e = \dfrac{v'_B - v'_A}{v_A - v_B}$ with all velocities as **signed** components along the same axis (or along $n$ in oblique impact); a left-moving particle has a negative velocity.
6. **Applying restitution to tangential components.** In oblique impact $e$ relates only the **normal ($n$)** components; the tangential components of each smooth particle are unchanged.
7. **Neglecting an unknown reaction's impulse.** Weights and springs are nonimpulsive, but unknown reactions (cord, floor, hand) must be included unless proved negligible — e.g., the cord impulse $T\Delta t$ in Sample Prob. 13.16 kills conservation of momentum in the vertical direction.
8. **Unit slips.** $60\,\text{mi/h} = 88\,\text{ft/s}$; $1\,\text{hp} = 550\,\text{ft}\cdot\text{lb/s} = 746\,\text{W}$; in U.S. units, $m = W/g$ with $g = 32.2\,\text{ft/s}^2$; mix $k$ in lb/in. with $x$ in in.

</Callout>

## Summary of Formulas

|---|---|---|
| Work of a force | $U_{1\to2} = \int \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int F_t\,ds$ | (13.2), (13.2$'$) |
| Constant force, straight line | $U_{1\to2} = (F\cos\alpha)\,\Delta x$ | (13.3) |
| Work of weight | $U_{1\to2} = -W\,\Delta y$ | (13.4$'$) |
| Work of spring | $U_{1\to2} = \tfrac12kx_1^2 - \tfrac12kx_2^2 = -\tfrac12(F_1+F_2)\Delta x$ | (13.6), (13.6$'$) |
| Work of gravitational force | $U_{1\to2} = GMm/r_2 - GMm/r_1$ | (13.7) |
| Kinetic energy | $T = \tfrac12mv^2$ | (13.9) |
| Work and energy | $T_1 + U_{1\to2} = T_2$ | (13.11) |
| Power | $\text{Power} = dU/dt = \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{v}$; $1\,\text{hp} = 550\,\text{ft}\cdot\text{lb/s} = 746\,\text{W}$ | (13.12), (13.13) |
| Efficiency | $\eta = \dfrac{\text{output work}}{\text{input work}} = \dfrac{\text{power output}}{\text{power input}}$ | (13.14), (13.15) |
| Potential energies | $V_g = Wy$; $\;V_g = -GMm/r = -WR^2/r$; $\;V_e = \tfrac12kx^2$ | (13.16)–(13.18) |
| Conservative force | $U_{1\to2} = V_1 - V_2$, $\oint\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = 0$, $\mathbf{F} = -\operatorname{grad}V$ | (13.19)–(13.23) |
| Conservation of energy | $T_1 + V_1 = T_2 + V_2$ | (13.24) |
| Impulse and momentum | $m\mathbf{v}_1 + \sum\mathbf{Imp}_{1\to2} = m\mathbf{v}_2$ | (13.32) |
| Conservation of momentum | $\sum m\mathbf{v}_1 = \sum m\mathbf{v}_2$ | (13.34) |
| Impulsive motion | $m\mathbf{v}_1 + \sum\mathbf{F}\,\Delta t = m\mathbf{v}_2$ | (13.35) |
