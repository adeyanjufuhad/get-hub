## 1. Overview

Why are structural steel beams shaped like an **"I"** or **"T"** instead of a solid square bar? Because resistance to bending depends directly on the **second moment of area ($I$)** about the cross-section's **centroidal axis**—pushing material farther away from the neutral axis increases stiffness dramatically without adding weight.

Mastering the centroid table and the **Parallel-Axis Theorem** here pays off immediately in both GET 207 exams and 300L Strength of Materials.

---

## 2. Key Concepts

### Centroid of Composite Areas

Break any complex shape into simple sub-shapes $i = 1, 2, \dots, n$ (treating holes/cutouts as **negative** areas $A_i < 0$):

$$\bar{x} = \frac{\sum A_i x_i}{\sum A_i}, \qquad \bar{y} = \frac{\sum A_i y_i}{\sum A_i}$$

### Standard Centroidal Second Moments of Area

| Shape | Area $A$ | Centroidal $I_{xc}$ |
|---|---|---|
| Rectangle (width $b$, height $h$) | $bh$ | $\dfrac{bh^3}{12}$ |
| Right triangle (base $b$, height $h$) | $\dfrac{1}{2}bh$ | $\dfrac{bh^3}{36}$ |
| Circle (radius $r$) | $\pi r^2$ | $\dfrac{\pi r^4}{4}$ |

### The Parallel-Axis Theorem (Steiner's Theorem)

To transfer a sub-shape's second moment of area from its own centroidal axis $c$ to any parallel axis (such as the composite centroidal axis) separated by perpendicular distance $d$:

$$I = I_c + A d^2$$

where $d = |y_i - \bar{y}|$ for $I_x$ (or $d = |x_i - \bar{x}|$ for $I_y$).

The **radius of gyration** $k_x$ summarizes how spread out the area is from the $x$-axis:

$$k_x = \sqrt{\frac{I_x}{A}}$$

<Callout variant="tip" title="Always draw a 5-column table">
In exams, make a clean table with columns **Part | $A_i$ | $y_i$ | $A_i y_i$ | $I_{ci} + A_i d_i^2$**. Even if you slip on arithmetic at the end, the organized table secures almost full method marks.
</Callout>

## 4. Common Exam Pitfalls

<Callout variant="warning" title="Parallel-Axis Theorem traps">
1. **Transferring between two non-centroidal axes directly:** $I = I_c + Ad^2$ ONLY works when $I_c$ is the **centroidal** axis! Never jump from one edge directly to another edge without passing through the centroid first.
2. **Swapping $b$ and $h$:** The dimension **perpendicular** to the reference axis is ALWAYS the one that gets cubed!
</Callout>
