## Overview

Engineering fields such as temperature distribution $T(x, y, t)$, wave displacement $y(x, t)$, and potential fields $v(x, y, z)$ depend on **two or more independent variables**. To measure how such a quantity responds when **only one independent variable changes** while every other variable is held fixed, we use **partial differentiation**.

Following **H. K. Dass, *Advanced Engineering Mathematics*, Chapter 1 (§1.7–1.11)**, this folio develops first-order partial derivatives (§1.7), higher-order and mixed partial derivatives (§1.8), the crucial convention for which variable is kept constant (§1.9), homogeneous functions (§1.10), and **Euler's theorem** together with its first- and second-order deductions (§1.11). Every theorem, worked example (Examples 7–29) and exercise set (Exercises 1.3 and 1.4) follows Dass's exact numbering and answers.

### 1.7 Partial Derivatives

Consider a function $z = f(x, y)$ of two independent variables $x$ and $y$. If $y$ is held fixed at a constant value and only $x$ varies, then $z$ behaves as a function of the single variable $x$. Differentiating $z$ with respect to $x$ under this condition produces the **partial derivative** (or partial differential co-efficient) of $z$ with respect to $x$. In practice, all standard rules of ordinary differentiation apply unchanged—we simply treat every independent variable other than the differentiating variable as a constant.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§1.7 First-order partial derivatives of z = f(x, y)">

The **partial derivative of $z$ with respect to $x$** (keeping $y$ constant), denoted by $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}$, $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}$, or $f_x(x, y)$, is defined by

$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = \lim_{\delta x \to 0} \frac{f(x + \delta x,\, y) - f(x, y)}{\delta x}.$$

Similarly, the **partial derivative of $z$ with respect to $y$** (keeping $x$ constant), denoted by $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}$, $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}$, or $f_y(x, y)$, is defined by

$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = \lim_{\delta y \to 0} \frac{f(x,\, y + \delta y) - f(x, y)}{\delta y}.$$

</RuleBox>

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§1.7 Standard Monge notation for partial derivatives">

In engineering mathematics and partial differential equations, the first- and second-order partial derivatives of $z = f(x, y)$ are frequently written in compact notation:

$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = p, \qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = q, \qquad \frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial x^2} = r, \qquad \frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial x\,\partial y} = s, \qquad \frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial y^2} = t.$$

</RuleBox>

Geometrically, the equation $z = f(x, y)$ represents a surface in three-dimensional space. Slicing this surface with the vertical plane $y = y_0$ yields a space curve $C_1$ along which $y$ is constant; the partial derivative $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}$ at $(x_0, y_0)$ is the slope of the tangent line $T_1$ to $C_1$ at $P(x_0, y_0, z_0)$. Likewise, slicing with the vertical plane $x = x_0$ produces a trace curve $C_2$ whose tangent line $T_2$ at $P$ has slope $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}$.

### 1.8 Partial Derivatives of Higher Orders

Because the first-order partial derivatives $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}$ and $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}$ are themselves functions of $x$ and $y$, they can be differentiated partially again with respect to $x$ or $y$ to produce second-, third-, and higher-order partial derivatives.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§1.8 Second-order partial derivatives and symmetry of mixed partials">

For $z = f(x, y)$, the four second-order partial derivatives are defined symbolically as:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\!\left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\right) = \frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial x^2} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} = f_{xx}, \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\!\left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\right) = \frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial y^2} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2} = f_{yy},$$

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\!\left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\right) = \frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial y\,\partial x} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\,\partial x} = f_{yx}, \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\!\left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\right) = \frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial x\,\partial y} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\,\partial y} = f_{xy}.$$

**Commutativity Note:** For all smooth engineering functions (where the mixed partial derivatives are continuous), the order of differentiation does not affect the result:

$$\frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial y\,\partial x} = \frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial x\,\partial y}.$$

</RuleBox>

### 1.9 Which Variable Is to Be Treated as Constant

When a problem relates more than two variables—such as Cartesian coordinates $(x, y)$ and polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$ connected by $x = r\cos\theta$ and $y = r\sin\theta$—a bare partial derivative such as $\dfrac{\partial r}{\partial x}$ is **ambiguous** unless we specify which remaining variable is held fixed.

Consider the two ways to express $r$ in terms of $x$:

1. From $x = r\cos\theta$, we have $r = x\sec\theta$. Differentiating with respect to $x$ while keeping $\theta$ constant gives $\dfrac{\partial r}{\partial x} = \sec\theta$.
2. From $r^2 = x^2 + y^2$, differentiating with respect to $x$ while keeping $y$ constant gives $2r\dfrac{\partial r}{\partial x} = 2x$, hence $\dfrac{\partial r}{\partial x} = \dfrac{x}{r} = \cos\theta$.

The two results ($\sec\theta$ versus $\cos\theta$) differ because holding $\theta$ fixed moves the point along a radial ray, whereas holding $y$ fixed moves the point horizontally.

<DiagramFigure
  src="/diagrams/get-209/partial-differentiation-and-eulers-theorem-polar-constant-variable.svg"
  caption="Geometric contrast in §1.9 for x = r cos θ, y = r sin θ: (i) holding θ constant yields (∂r/∂x)_θ = sec θ; (ii) holding y constant yields (∂r/∂x)_y = cos θ."
/>

<RuleBox kind="working-rule" title="§1.9 Subscript convention for held variables">

To eliminate ambiguity when multiple coordinate systems appear:

1. $\left(\dfrac{\partial r}{\partial x}\right)_\theta$ denotes the partial derivative of $r$ with respect to $x$, **keeping $\theta$ constant** (so $\left(\dfrac{\partial r}{\partial x}\right)_\theta = \sec\theta$).
2. $\left(\dfrac{\partial r}{\partial x}\right)_y$ denotes the partial derivative of $r$ with respect to $x$, **keeping $y$ constant** (so $\left(\dfrac{\partial r}{\partial x}\right)_y = \cos\theta$).
3. **Default convention** when no subscript is written: variables are paired by their natural coordinate set—$(x, y)$ together and $(r, \theta)$ together:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial x} \text{ means } \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)_y, \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \text{ means } \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right)_x, \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial r} \text{ means } \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\right)_\theta, \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} \text{ means } \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta}\right)_r.$$

</RuleBox>

### 1.10 Homogeneous Function

Before stating Euler's theorem, we formalize what it means for a function of two or more variables to be **homogeneous**. Intuitively, a polynomial in $x$ and $y$ is homogeneous of degree (or order) $n$ if every single term has the exact same total degree $n$.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§1.10 Homogeneous function of order (degree) n">

A polynomial function $f(x, y)$ is a **homogeneous function of order $n$** if the degree of every term in $x$ and $y$ equals $n$:

$$f(x, y) = a_0 x^n + a_1 x^{n-1} y + a_2 x^{n-2} y^2 + \dots + a_{n-1} x y^{n-1} + a_n y^n.$$

Factoring out $x^n$ yields the general form valid for **any** real degree $n$ (positive, negative, zero, or fractional):

$$f(x, y) = x^n \left[ a_0 + a_1\left(\frac{y}{x}\right) + a_2\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)^2 + \dots + a_n\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)^n \right] = x^n \varphi\!\left(\frac{y}{x}\right).$$

Equivalently, $f(tx, ty) = t^n f(x, y)$ for a scaling parameter $t > 0$. For three variables, $u(x, y, z) = x^n \varphi\!\left(\dfrac{y}{x}, \dfrac{z}{x}\right)$.

</RuleBox>

Dass highlights three diagnostic cases to fix the idea:

1. $x^3\left[1 + \dfrac{y}{x} + 3\left(\dfrac{y}{x}\right)^2 + 5\left(\dfrac{y}{x}\right)^3\right]$ is a homogeneous function of **order $3$**.
2. $\dfrac{\sqrt{x} + \sqrt{y}}{x^2 + y^2} = \dfrac{\sqrt{x}\left[1 + \sqrt{\dfrac{y}{x}}\right]}{x^2\left[1 + \left(\dfrac{y}{x}\right)^2\right]} = x^{-3/2} \cdot \dfrac{1 + \sqrt{y/x}}{1 + (y/x)^2}$ is a homogeneous function of **order $-3/2$**.
3. $\sin^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{x} + \sqrt{y}}{x^2 + y^2}\right)$ is **not** a homogeneous function, because it cannot be factored into $x^n f(y/x)$ so that a degree $n$ can be assigned directly; rather, it is a *function of a homogeneous expression*. (Such functions are handled via Euler's Deduction I below.)

### 1.11 Euler's Theorem on Homogeneous Function

Euler's theorem provides an immediate algebraic shortcut linking the first-order partial derivatives of any homogeneous function to the function itself—often eliminating pages of quotient-rule differentiation.

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="§1.11 Euler's Theorem on Homogeneous Functions (U.P., I Semester, Dec. 2006)">

**Statement.** If $z$ is a homogeneous function of $x$ and $y$ of order (degree) $n$, then

$$x\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} + y\frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = n z.$$

**Extension to three variables.** If $u$ is a homogeneous function of $x, y, z$ of degree $n$, then

$$x\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + y\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} + z\frac{\partial u}{\partial z} = n u.$$

</RuleBox>

**Proof (two variables).** Since $z$ is homogeneous of order $n$ in $x$ and $y$, we can write

$$z = x^n f\!\left(\frac{y}{x}\right). \tag{1}$$

Differentiating (1) partially with respect to $x$ by the product and chain rules:

$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = n x^{n-1} f\!\left(\frac{y}{x}\right) + x^n f'\!\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)\!\left(-\frac{y}{x^2}\right) = n x^{n-1} f\!\left(\frac{y}{x}\right) - x^{n-2} y\, f'\!\left(\frac{y}{x}\right).$$

Multiplying both sides by $x$:

$$x\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = n x^n f\!\left(\frac{y}{x}\right) - x^{n-1} y\, f'\!\left(\frac{y}{x}\right). \tag{2}$$

Now differentiating (1) partially with respect to $y$:

$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = x^n f'\!\left(\frac{y}{x}\right) \cdot \frac{1}{x} = x^{n-1} f'\!\left(\frac{y}{x}\right).$$

Multiplying both sides by $y$:

$$y\frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = x^{n-1} y\, f'\!\left(\frac{y}{x}\right). \tag{3}$$

Adding (2) and (3), the $f'(y/x)$ terms cancel identically:

$$x\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} + y\frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = n x^n f\!\left(\frac{y}{x}\right) = n z. \qquad \blacksquare$$

#### I. Deduction from Euler's Theorem

In many university exam problems, the given function $u$ is *not* homogeneous itself (for instance, $u = \sin^{-1}(\dots)$, $u = \tan^{-1}(\dots)$, or $u = \log(\dots)$), yet applying an invertible function $z = f(u)$ reveals a homogeneous expression of degree $n$.

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="§1.11 Deduction I from Euler's Theorem (Nagpur University, Winter 2003)">

If $z$ is a homogeneous function of $x, y$ of degree $n$ and $z = f(u)$, then

$$x\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + y\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = n\frac{f(u)}{f'(u)}.$$

**Three-variable form:** If $v = f(u)$ is a homogeneous function of $x, y, z$ of degree $n$, then

$$x\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + y\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} + z\frac{\partial u}{\partial z} = n\frac{f(u)}{f'(u)}.$$

</RuleBox>

**Proof.** Since $z$ is homogeneous of degree $n$, Euler's theorem gives $x\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x} + y\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y} = nz$. Substituting $z = f(u)$, $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x} = f'(u)\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x}$, and $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y} = f'(u)\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial y}$ yields

$$x f'(u)\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + y f'(u)\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = n f(u) \implies x\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + y\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = n\frac{f(u)}{f'(u)}. \qquad \blacksquare$$

#### II. Second-Order Deduction from Euler's Theorem

When $z = f(u)$ is homogeneous of degree $n$, Deduction I yields $x\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x} + y\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial y} = g(u)$, where $g(u) = n\dfrac{f(u)}{f'(u)}$. Differentiating that identity produces the second-order composite formula—one of the highest-frequency formulas in GET 209 examinations.

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="§1.11 Deduction II from Euler's Theorem (Nagpur University, Winter 2003)">

If $z = f(u)$ is a homogeneous function of $x, y$ of degree $n$ and $g(u) = n\dfrac{f(u)}{f'(u)}$ (so that $x\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x} + y\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial y} = g(u)$), then

$$x^2\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} + 2xy\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x\,\partial y} + y^2\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2} = g(u)\,[g'(u) - 1].$$

</RuleBox>

**Proof.** Starting from Deduction I,

$$x\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + y\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = g(u). \tag{1}$$

Differentiating (1) partially with respect to $x$:

$$\left(x\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\right) + y\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x\,\partial y} = g'(u)\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} \implies x\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} + y\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x\,\partial y} = [g'(u) - 1]\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}. \tag{2}$$

Similarly, differentiating (1) partially with respect to $y$:

$$x\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y\,\partial x} + y\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2} = [g'(u) - 1]\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}. \tag{3}$$

Multiplying (2) by $x$, multiplying (3) by $y$, and adding (using $\dfrac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y\,\partial x} = \dfrac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x\,\partial y}$):

$$x^2\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} + 2xy\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x\,\partial y} + y^2\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2} = [g'(u) - 1]\!\left(x\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + y\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right) = g(u)\,[g'(u) - 1]. \qquad \blacksquare$$

## Common Exam Pitfalls

<Callout variant="warning" title="Key mistakes to avoid in §1.7–1.11">

- **Assuming $\left(\dfrac{\partial r}{\partial x}\right)_\theta = \left(\dfrac{\partial r}{\partial x}\right)_y$:** Never invert a partial derivative blindly as if it were an ordinary derivative! For polar coordinates, $\left(\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial r}\right)_\theta = \cos\theta$, so $\left(\dfrac{\partial r}{\partial x}\right)_\theta = \sec\theta$, whereas $\left(\dfrac{\partial r}{\partial x}\right)_y = \cos\theta$. Always check which variable is held constant (§1.9).
- **Applying Euler's theorem directly to a non-homogeneous composite function:** Expressions like $u = \sin^{-1}\!\left(\dfrac{x+y}{\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y}}\right)$ or $u = \log\!\left(\dfrac{x^4+y^4}{x+y}\right)$ are **not** homogeneous. Always invert first to $z = f(u) = \sin u$ or $z = e^u$, find the degree $n$ of $z$, and then use **Deduction I** ($n\dfrac{f(u)}{f'(u)}$) and **Deduction II** ($g(u)[g'(u)-1]$).
- **Forgetting to split sums of different degrees:** A function like $u = f(y/x) + \sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ or $z = x^n f(y/x) + y^{-n}\phi(x/y)$ is *not* a single homogeneous function—split it into $u_1 + u_2$ of separate degrees $n_1, n_2$ and apply Euler's theorem to each part independently (Examples 26 & 27).
- **Forgetting the $-1$ inside Deduction II:** The second-order composite identity is $g(u)[g'(u) - 1]$, **not** $g(u)g'(u)$. When $z$ itself is homogeneous of degree $n$, $g(z) = nz$, so $g(z)[g'(z) - 1] = nz(n - 1) = n(n - 1)z$, which immediately recovers Example 25(iii).

</Callout>

## Summary of Formulas

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="Master formula sheet — H. K. Dass, Ch. 1 (§1.7–1.11)">

- **First-order partial derivatives (§1.7):**
  $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = \lim_{\delta x \to 0}\frac{f(x+\delta x, y) - f(x, y)}{\delta x}, \qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = \lim_{\delta y \to 0}\frac{f(x, y+\delta y) - f(x, y)}{\delta y}$$
- **Monge notation (§1.7):** $p = z_x$, $q = z_y$, $r = z_{xx}$, $s = z_{xy}$, $t = z_{yy}$.
- **Mixed partial symmetry (§1.8):** $\dfrac{\partial^2 z}{\partial y\,\partial x} = \dfrac{\partial^2 z}{\partial x\,\partial y}$.
- **Polar coordinate derivatives (§1.9):** For $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$,
  $$\left(\frac{\partial x}{\partial r}\right)_\theta = \cos\theta, \qquad \left(\frac{\partial r}{\partial x}\right)_\theta = \sec\theta, \qquad \left(\frac{\partial r}{\partial x}\right)_y = \frac{x}{r} = \cos\theta, \qquad \left(\frac{\partial \theta}{\partial y}\right)_x = \frac{x}{x^2 + y^2}.$$
- **Homogeneous function of degree $n$ (§1.10):** $f(x, y) = x^n \varphi\!\left(\dfrac{y}{x}\right)$ (or $f(tx, ty) = t^n f(x, y)$).
- **Euler's Theorem (§1.11):**
  $$x\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} + y\frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = n z \qquad \text{(and } x\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + y\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} + z\frac{\partial u}{\partial z} = nu \text{ in 3 variables)}$$
- **Second-order Euler identity (§1.11, Ex. 25):**
  $$x^2\frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial x^2} + 2xy\frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial x\,\partial y} + y^2\frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial y^2} = n(n - 1) z$$
- **Deduction I for $z = f(u)$ homogeneous of degree $n$ (§1.11):**
  $$x\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + y\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = n\frac{f(u)}{f'(u)} = g(u)$$
- **Deduction II for $z = f(u)$ homogeneous of degree $n$ (§1.11):**
  $$x^2\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} + 2xy\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x\,\partial y} + y^2\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2} = g(u)\,[g'(u) - 1]$$

</RuleBox>
