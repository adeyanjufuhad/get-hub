## Overview

In §1.7–1.11 only *one* variable at a time was allowed to change. Here every variable of a function is allowed to change together. That single idea produces the **total differential** $dz=\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,dx+\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}\,dy$, and from it the whole toolkit of this part of Chapter 1: the chain rule for composite functions, change of variables (including polar co-ordinates), implicit differentiation, geometrical meaning of partial derivatives, tangent planes and normal lines, error estimation, and the Jacobian determinant (including its properties, functional dependence, and implicit systems). Each section below follows Dass's numbering (§1.12 to §1.25) and uses his solved examples and exercises.

### 1.12 Total Differential

When we differentiate partially, only one variable varies while the others are frozen. In total differentiation the increments are given to **all** the variables simultaneously. The total differential is the first-order change of the function produced by those simultaneous small changes.

### 1.13 Total Differential Co-efficient

Let $z=f(x,y)$. Give $x$ and $y$ small increments $\delta x,\delta y$, and let $\delta z$ be the resulting increment in $z$. Then

$$\delta z=f(x+\delta x,\,y+\delta y)-f(x,y).$$

Adding and subtracting $f(x,y+\delta y)$ splits this into a change in $x$ (with $y+\delta y$ held) and a change in $y$ (with $x$ held):

$$\delta z=\frac{f(x+\delta x,y+\delta y)-f(x,y+\delta y)}{\delta x}\,\delta x+\frac{f(x,y+\delta y)-f(x,y)}{\delta y}\,\delta y .$$

Letting $\delta x\to0$ and $\delta y\to0$, each quotient becomes a partial derivative.

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§1.13 Total differential of z = f(x, y)">

$$dz=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,dx+\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\,dy$$

Here $dz$ is called the **total differential** of $z$. For $u=f(x,y,z)$ the same pattern gives $du=\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x}\,dx+\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial y}\,dy+\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial z}\,dz$.

</RuleBox>

### 1.14 Change of Two Independent Variables x and y by Any Other Variable t

Suppose $z=f(x,y)$ where $x=\varphi(t)$ and $y=\psi(t)$. Then $z$ is a **composite function of $t$** alone. Dividing the total differential by $dt$ gives the rule.

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§1.14 Differentiation of a composite function (total differential co-efficient)">

$$\frac{dz}{dt}=\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt}+\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt}$$

Here $\dfrac{dz}{dt}$ is called the **total differential co-efficient** of $z$ with respect to $t$.

</RuleBox>

### 1.15 Change in the Independent Variables x and y by Other Two Variables u and v

Let $z=f(x,y)$ with $x=\varphi(u,v)$ and $y=\psi(u,v)$. Applying the previous rule while $v$ is held fixed, and then while $u$ is held fixed:

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§1.15 Chain rule for two new variables u, v">

$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial u}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial u}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial u},\qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial v}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial v}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial v}$$

</RuleBox>

### 1.16 Change in Both the Independent and Dependent Variables (Polar Co-ordinates)

Changing from Cartesian $(x,y)$ to polar $(r,\theta)$ uses $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$, with $r^2=x^2+y^2$ and $\theta=\tan^{-1}\dfrac yx$. A function $w=f(x,y)$ then becomes a function of $r,\theta$, and the chain rule of §1.15 supplies the first derivatives. For second derivatives, apply the first-derivative operators twice.

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§1.16 Polar co-ordinate operators">

For $x=r\cos\theta,\;y=r\sin\theta$:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial x}=\cos\theta\frac{\partial}{\partial r}-\frac{\sin\theta}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta},\qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial y}=\sin\theta\frac{\partial}{\partial r}+\frac{\cos\theta}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}$$

These operators are applied once for $u_x,u_y$ and again (to the result) for $u_{xx},u_{yy}$.

</RuleBox>

### 1.17 Important Deductions

Let $z=f(x,y)$, so $dz=\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,dx+\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}\,dy$. If $z$ is held constant, $dz=0$, which gives a formula for the derivative of an **implicit function** $f(x,y)=0$.

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§1.17 Derivative of an implicit function">

If $f(x,y)=0$, then

$$\frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{\partial f/\partial x}{\partial f/\partial y}.$$

For the second derivative write $p=\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}$, $q=\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}$, $r=\dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}$, $s=\dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\partial y}$, $t=\dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2}$. Then $\dfrac{dy}{dx}=-\dfrac pq$ and

$$\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}=-\frac{q^2r-2pqs+p^2t}{q^3}.$$

</RuleBox>

The second-derivative formula follows by differentiating $-p/q$ once more: $\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}=-\dfrac{q\,\frac{dp}{dx}-p\,\frac{dq}{dx}}{q^2}$ with $\dfrac{dp}{dx}=r+s\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{qr-ps}{q}$ and $\dfrac{dq}{dx}=s+t\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{qs-tp}{q}$.

### 1.18 Typical Cases

When $x$ and $y$ are given as functions of two new variables, $x=f(u,v)$ and $y=\varphi(u,v)$, and we want the derivatives of $u,v$ with respect to $x,y$ (the inverse relations), we differentiate the two given equations partially with respect to $x$ (treating $y$ as constant) and then with respect to $y$ (treating $x$ as constant), and solve each pair of linear equations.

<RuleBox kind="working-rule" title="§1.18 Typical case: x = f(u, v), y = φ(u, v)">

Differentiate both relations with respect to $x$ ($y$ constant):

$$1=\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial x},\qquad 0=\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}.$$

Then with respect to $y$ ($x$ constant):

$$0=\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial y},\qquad 1=\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}.$$

Solve each pair simultaneously for $\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x},\dfrac{\partial v}{\partial x}$ and $\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial y},\dfrac{\partial v}{\partial y}$.

</RuleBox>

### 1.19 Geometrical Interpretation of ∂z/∂x and ∂z/∂y

Consider a surface $S$ defined explicitly by $z = f(x, y)$. Slice this surface with a vertical plane $y = k$ parallel to the $XZ$-plane, passing through the point $P(x, k, z)$ on $S$. The intersection is a plane curve $APB$ lying in the plane $y = k$, described by the pair of equations

$$z = f(x, y), \qquad y = k.$$

Along this curve only $x$ and $z$ vary while $y$ stays fixed at $k$. Hence the slope of the tangent line to the curve $APB$ at $P$ is $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}$. By symmetry, slicing the surface with a plane $x = c$ parallel to the $YZ$-plane gives a section curve whose tangent slope at the point is $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}$.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§1.19 Geometrical meaning of first partial derivatives">

For a surface $S: z = f(x, y)$:

- $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}$ is the **slope of the tangent** to the curve of intersection of the surface $z = f(x, y)$ with a plane $y = k$ parallel to the $XZ$-plane.
- $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}$ is the **slope of the tangent** to the curve of intersection of the surface $z = f(x, y)$ with a plane $x = c$ parallel to the $YZ$-plane.

</RuleBox>

### 1.20 Tangent Plane to a Surface

Now let a surface $S$ be given implicitly by $F(x, y, z) = 0$, and let $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ be a point on $S$. Take a neighbouring point $Q(x_1 + \delta x_1,\, y_1 + \delta y_1,\, z_1 + \delta z_1)$ on a smooth curve drawn on $S$ through $P$, with arc length $PQ = \delta s$ and chord length $PQ = \delta c$.

The direction cosines of the chord $PQ$ are

$$\frac{\delta x}{\delta c},\; \frac{\delta y}{\delta c},\; \frac{\delta z}{\delta c} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \frac{\delta x}{\delta s}\cdot\frac{\delta s}{\delta c},\; \frac{\delta y}{\delta s}\cdot\frac{\delta s}{\delta c},\; \frac{\delta z}{\delta s}\cdot\frac{\delta s}{\delta c}.$$

As $\delta s \to 0$, $Q \to P$, $\delta s/\delta c \to 1$, and the chord $PQ$ approaches the tangent line $PT$, whose direction cosines are

$$\frac{dx}{ds},\; \frac{dy}{ds},\; \frac{dz}{ds}.$$

Differentiating $F(x, y, z) = 0$ totally with respect to the arc length $s$ yields

$$\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{ds} + \frac{\partial F}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{ds} + \frac{\partial F}{\partial z}\frac{dz}{ds} = 0.$$

This orthogonality condition shows that **every** tangent line $PT$ at $P$ is perpendicular to the fixed direction having direction ratios $\left(\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial x},\, \dfrac{\partial F}{\partial y},\, \dfrac{\partial F}{\partial z}\right)$ evaluated at $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$. Therefore all tangent lines to curves on $S$ through $P$ lie in a single plane — the **tangent plane** — and the line through $P$ perpendicular to that plane is the **normal line**.

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§1.20 Equations of the tangent plane and normal line">

At a point $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ on the surface $F(x, y, z) = 0$, with partial derivatives evaluated at $(x_1, y_1, z_1)$:

**Equation of the tangent plane:**

$$(x - x_1)\frac{\partial F}{\partial x} + (y - y_1)\frac{\partial F}{\partial y} + (z - z_1)\frac{\partial F}{\partial z} = 0$$

**Equation of the normal line:**

$$\frac{x - x_1}{\partial F/\partial x} = \frac{y - y_1}{\partial F/\partial y} = \frac{z - z_1}{\partial F/\partial z}$$

Two surfaces $f(x, y, z) = 0$ and $F(x, y, z) = 0$ intersect **orthogonally** (perpendicularly) at a common point if the dot product of their normal direction ratios vanishes: $l_1 l_2 + m_1 m_2 + n_1 n_2 = f_x F_x + f_y F_y + f_z F_z = 0$.

</RuleBox>

### 1.21 Error Determination

When physical quantities are measured in the laboratory or field, small measurement errors $\delta x, \delta y, \dots$ propagate into any computed output $u = f(x, y, \dots)$. Since $\lim_{\delta x \to 0}\dfrac{\delta y}{\delta x} = \dfrac{dy}{dx}$, for sufficiently small errors we replace $\delta y$ by the total differential $dy$:

$$\delta y \approx \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)\delta x, \qquad \delta u \approx \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\,\delta x + \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\,\delta y + \frac{\partial u}{\partial z}\,\delta z.$$

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§1.21 Absolute, relative and percentage errors">

If $x$ is measured with a small error $\delta x$:

1. **Absolute error** in $x$: $\;\delta x$
2. **Relative error** in $x$: $\;\dfrac{\delta x}{x}$
3. **Percentage error** in $x$: $\;\left(\dfrac{\delta x}{x}\right)\times 100$

</RuleBox>

<RuleBox kind="working-rule" title="§1.21 Logarithmic differentiation and maximum possible error">

1. For formulas built from products, quotients, and powers (such as $u = k\,x^a y^b z^{-c}$), **take natural logarithms first**:

$$\log u = \log k + a\log x + b\log y - c\log z$$

and then differentiate to get the relative error directly:

$$\frac{\delta u}{u} \approx a\frac{\delta x}{x} + b\frac{\delta y}{y} - c\frac{\delta z}{z}.$$

2. Multiply by $100$ to pass directly from relative error to percentage error.
3. For **maximum possible error**, take the **positive sum of magnitudes** of every term so that individual errors reinforce rather than cancel:

$$\left|\frac{\delta u}{u}\right|_{\max} \approx |a|\left|\frac{\delta x}{x}\right| + |b|\left|\frac{\delta y}{y}\right| + |c|\left|\frac{\delta z}{z}\right|.$$

4. For numerical approximations of $f(x + \delta x,\, y + \delta y)$, evaluate $f(x, y)$ at a convenient nearby point $(x, y)$ and add $df = f_x\,\delta x + f_y\,\delta y$.

</RuleBox>

### 1.22 Jacobians

When changing multiple variables simultaneously — from Cartesian $(x, y)$ to polar $(r, \theta)$ or spherical $(r, \theta, \phi)$, or between arbitrary functions $u, v, w$ and $x, y, z$ — all first partial derivatives organize naturally into a square determinant called the **Jacobian**.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§1.22 Jacobian of two and three variables">

If $u$ and $v$ are differentiable functions of the two independent variables $x$ and $y$, the **Jacobian** of $u, v$ with respect to $x, y$ is the $2\times 2$ determinant

$$\frac{\partial(u, v)}{\partial(x, y)} = J\!\left(\frac{u, v}{x, y}\right) = \begin{vmatrix} \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial y} \\[8pt] \dfrac{\partial v}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial v}{\partial y} \end{vmatrix}.$$

Similarly, for three functions $u, v, w$ of $x, y, z$:

$$\frac{\partial(u, v, w)}{\partial(x, y, z)} = \begin{vmatrix} \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial y} & \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial z} \\[8pt] \dfrac{\partial v}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial v}{\partial y} & \dfrac{\partial v}{\partial z} \\[8pt] \dfrac{\partial w}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial w}{\partial y} & \dfrac{\partial w}{\partial z} \end{vmatrix}.$$

</RuleBox>

### 1.23 Properties of Jacobians

Jacobians obey three fundamental algebraic properties that mirror ordinary derivatives: the **inverse (reciprocal) rule**, the **chain rule**, and the **vanishing criterion for functional dependence**.

#### (1) First Property: Reciprocal Relation ($JJ' = 1$)

Let $u = f(x, y)$ and $v = \varphi(x, y)$. Multiplying $\dfrac{\partial(u, v)}{\partial(x, y)}$ by $\dfrac{\partial(x, y)}{\partial(u, v)}$ using row-by-column determinant multiplication (or transposing the second determinant and multiplying row-by-row) gives

$$\frac{\partial(u, v)}{\partial(x, y)} \times \frac{\partial(x, y)}{\partial(u, v)} = \begin{vmatrix} \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x}\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial u} + \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial y}\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial u} & \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x}\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial v} + \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial y}\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial v} \\[10pt] \dfrac{\partial v}{\partial x}\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial u} + \dfrac{\partial v}{\partial y}\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial u} & \dfrac{\partial v}{\partial x}\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial v} + \dfrac{\partial v}{\partial y}\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial v} \end{vmatrix} = \begin{vmatrix} \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial u} & \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial v} \\[8pt] \dfrac{\partial v}{\partial u} & \dfrac{\partial v}{\partial v} \end{vmatrix} = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = 1.$$

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="§1.23 First property of Jacobians (JJ' = 1)">

If $u, v$ are invertible functions of $x, y$, then

$$J \cdot J' = \frac{\partial(u, v)}{\partial(x, y)} \times \frac{\partial(x, y)}{\partial(u, v)} = 1.$$

Equivalently, whenever $x, y$ are given explicitly in terms of $u, v$ (or vice versa), compute the easier Jacobian first and take its reciprocal. The same property holds for $n$ variables: $\dfrac{\partial(u, v, w)}{\partial(x, y, z)} \times \dfrac{\partial(x, y, z)}{\partial(u, v, w)} = 1$.

</RuleBox>

#### (2) Second Property: Chain Rule for Jacobians

If $u, v$ are functions of $r, s$ and $r, s$ are functions of $x, y$, multiplying the two determinants $\dfrac{\partial(u, v)}{\partial(r, s)}$ and $\dfrac{\partial(r, s)}{\partial(x, y)}$ produces entries like $\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial r}\dfrac{\partial r}{\partial x} + \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial s}\dfrac{\partial s}{\partial x} = \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x}$, which reconstructs $\dfrac{\partial(u, v)}{\partial(x, y)}$ entry by entry.

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="§1.23 Second property of Jacobians (Chain Rule)">

If $u, v$ depend on $r, s$ and $r, s$ depend on $x, y$:

$$\frac{\partial(u, v)}{\partial(x, y)} = \frac{\partial(u, v)}{\partial(r, s)} \times \frac{\partial(r, s)}{\partial(x, y)}.$$

Similarly, for three variables:

$$\frac{\partial(u, v, w)}{\partial(x, y, z)} = \frac{\partial(u, v, w)}{\partial(r, s, t)} \times \frac{\partial(r, s, t)}{\partial(x, y, z)}.$$

</RuleBox>

#### (3) Third Property: Functional Dependence

Suppose three functions $u, v, w$ of $x, y, z$ are **not independent** — that is, they satisfy a non-trivial relation $f(u, v, w) = 0$ free of $x, y, z$. Differentiating $f(u, v, w) = 0$ partially with respect to $x, y, z$ gives three homogeneous linear equations in $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial u}, \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial v}, \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial w}$:

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial w}\frac{\partial w}{\partial x} = 0,$$
$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial w}\frac{\partial w}{\partial y} = 0,$$
$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial z} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial z} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial w}\frac{\partial w}{\partial z} = 0.$$

For a non-trivial solution $\left(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial u}, \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial v}, \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial w}\right) \neq (0, 0, 0)$, the coefficient determinant must vanish. Transposing rows and columns leaves the determinant unchanged, so $\dfrac{\partial(u, v, w)}{\partial(x, y, z)} = 0$.

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="§1.23 Third property of Jacobians (Functional dependence)">

**Necessary and sufficient condition:** The functions $u, v, w$ of $x, y, z$ (or $u, v$ of $x, y$) are **functionally dependent** — connected by a relation $f(u, v, w) = 0$ (or $f(u, v) = 0$) independent of $x, y, z$ — if and only if their Jacobian vanishes identically:

$$\frac{\partial(u, v, w)}{\partial(x, y, z)} = 0 \qquad \left(\text{or } \frac{\partial(u, v)}{\partial(x, y)} = 0\right).$$

</RuleBox>

### 1.24 Jacobian of Implicit Functions

Often $u, v$ are not given explicitly in terms of $x, y$, but instead satisfy two implicit equations:

$$f_1(x, y, u, v) = 0, \qquad f_2(x, y, u, v) = 0.$$

Differentiating both equations partially with respect to $x$ and $y$ gives four relations:

$$\frac{\partial f_1}{\partial u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} = -\frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x}, \qquad \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = -\frac{\partial f_1}{\partial y},$$
$$\frac{\partial f_2}{\partial u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} = -\frac{\partial f_2}{\partial x}, \qquad \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial y} = -\frac{\partial f_2}{\partial y}.$$

Multiplying $\dfrac{\partial(f_1, f_2)}{\partial(u, v)}$ by $\dfrac{\partial(u, v)}{\partial(x, y)}$ yields

$$\frac{\partial(f_1, f_2)}{\partial(u, v)} \times \frac{\partial(u, v)}{\partial(x, y)} = \begin{vmatrix} -\dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x} & -\dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial y} \\[8pt] -\dfrac{\partial f_2}{\partial x} & -\dfrac{\partial f_2}{\partial y} \end{vmatrix} = (-1)^2\frac{\partial(f_1, f_2)}{\partial(x, y)}.$$

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§1.24 Jacobian of implicit functions">

For $n$ implicit equations $f_1(x_1,\dots,x_n, u_1,\dots,u_n) = 0, \dots, f_n(x_1,\dots,x_n, u_1,\dots,u_n) = 0$:

$$\frac{\partial(u_1, u_2, \dots, u_n)}{\partial(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n)} = (-1)^n \frac{\partial(f_1, f_2, \dots, f_n)/\partial(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n)}{\partial(f_1, f_2, \dots, f_n)/\partial(u_1, u_2, \dots, u_n)}.$$

In particular:
- For $n = 2$: $\;(-1)^2 = +1$, so $\;\dfrac{\partial(u, v)}{\partial(x, y)} = +\dfrac{\partial(f_1, f_2)/\partial(x, y)}{\partial(f_1, f_2)/\partial(u, v)}$.
- For $n = 3$: $\;(-1)^3 = -1$, so $\;\dfrac{\partial(u, v, w)}{\partial(x, y, z)} = -\dfrac{\partial(f_1, f_2, f_3)/\partial(x, y, z)}{\partial(f_1, f_2, f_3)/\partial(u, v, w)}$.

</RuleBox>

### 1.25 Partial Derivatives of Implicit Functions by Jacobian

When $u, v$ are defined implicitly as functions of $x, y$ by $f_1(x, y, u, v) = 0$ and $f_2(x, y, u, v) = 0$, differentiating both equations with respect to $x$ (holding $y$ constant) gives a $2\times 2$ linear system in $\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x}$ and $\dfrac{\partial v}{\partial x}$:

$$\frac{\partial f_1}{\partial u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x} = 0,$$
$$\frac{\partial f_2}{\partial u}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial x} = 0.$$

Solving by cross-multiplication (Cramer's rule) expresses each partial derivative cleanly as a ratio of two Jacobians.

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§1.25 Implicit partial derivatives via Jacobians">

For $f_1(x, y, u, v) = 0$, $f_2(x, y, u, v) = 0$ (treating $u, v$ as dependent on $x, y$):

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = -\frac{\partial(f_1, f_2)/\partial(x, v)}{\partial(f_1, f_2)/\partial(u, v)}, \qquad \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} = -\frac{\partial(f_1, f_2)/\partial(u, x)}{\partial(f_1, f_2)/\partial(u, v)} = \frac{\partial(f_1, f_2)/\partial(x, u)}{\partial(f_1, f_2)/\partial(v, u)}.$$

For three implicit equations $f_1 = 0, f_2 = 0, f_3 = 0$ defining $x, y, z$ in terms of $u, v, w$:

$$\frac{\partial x}{\partial u} = -\frac{\partial(f_1, f_2, f_3)/\partial(u, y, z)}{\partial(f_1, f_2, f_3)/\partial(x, y, z)}, \qquad \frac{\partial y}{\partial u} = -\frac{\partial(f_1, f_2, f_3)/\partial(x, u, z)}{\partial(f_1, f_2, f_3)/\partial(x, y, z)}, \qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial u} = -\frac{\partial(f_1, f_2, f_3)/\partial(x, y, u)}{\partial(f_1, f_2, f_3)/\partial(x, y, z)}.$$

**Memory rule (Dass §1.25):** First write the Jacobian of all equations with respect to the **dependent variables** in the denominator; then form the numerator by replacing the differentiated dependent variable (e.g. $x$) with the independent variable (e.g. $u$), and attach a minus sign.

</RuleBox>

## Common Exam Pitfalls

<Callout variant="warning" title="Pitfall 1: Treating polar or transformed operators like constants when taking second derivatives">

When transforming $\dfrac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}$ using $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x} = \cos\theta\dfrac{\partial}{\partial r} - \dfrac{\sin\theta}{r}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\theta}$, you **must** apply the product rule to $\cos\theta$, $\dfrac{\sin\theta}{r}$, and $\dfrac{1}{r}$ when differentiating a second time. Dropping the derivative of $1/r$ or $\sin\theta$ loses the $\dfrac{1}{r}\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial r}$ term in Laplace's equation.

</Callout>

<Callout variant="warning" title="Pitfall 2: Subtracting percentage errors when asked for the maximum possible error">

For $T = 2\pi\sqrt{l/g}$, logarithmic differentiation gives $\dfrac{\delta T}{T} = \dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{\delta l}{l} - \dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{\delta g}{g}$. If the problem gives *specific signed changes* (e.g. $E$ increased by $3\%$ and $R$ decreased by $2\%$), keep the exact signs. If it asks for the **maximum possible error** (e.g. errors up to $1\%$ in $l$ and $2\%$ in $g$), take absolute values so the errors add: $\dfrac{1}{2}(1\% + 2\%) = 1.5\%$, **not** $\dfrac{1}{2}(1\% - 2\%)$.

</Callout>

<Callout variant="warning" title="Pitfall 3: Assuming ∂u/∂x = 1 / (∂x/∂u) in multivariable systems">

In single-variable calculus $\dfrac{du}{dx} = \left(\dfrac{dx}{du}\right)^{-1}$, but for two or more variables $\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x} \neq \left(\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial u}\right)^{-1}$ because different variables are held constant! For example, in polar co-ordinates $\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial r} = \cos\theta$ while $\dfrac{\partial r}{\partial x} = \cos\theta \neq \sec\theta$. Always use §1.18 (simultaneous differentiation) or §1.25 (ratio of Jacobians).

</Callout>

<Callout variant="warning" title="Pitfall 4: Forgetting the (−1)ⁿ factor in implicit Jacobians or swapping variable order">

In §1.24 the formula is $\dfrac{\partial(u_1,\dots,u_n)}{\partial(x_1,\dots,x_n)} = (-1)^n \dfrac{\partial(f_1,\dots,f_n)/\partial(x_1,\dots,x_n)}{\partial(f_1,\dots,f_n)/\partial(u_1,\dots,u_n)}$ — the sign is $+$ for $n = 2$ and $-$ for $n = 3$. In §1.25 every single-variable implicit partial derivative carries a single minus sign, **provided** you replace the dependent variable in its exact column slot (e.g. replacing $v$ by $x$ in $(u, v)$ gives $-\dfrac{\partial(f_1,f_2)/\partial(u,x)}{\partial(f_1,f_2)/\partial(u,v)}$; swapping $(u, x)$ to $(x, u)$ flips the sign).

</Callout>

## Summary of Formulas

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="Key Formulas — H. K. Dass, Ch. 1 (§1.12–1.25)">

**1. Total differential & chain rules (§1.13–1.16):**

$$dz = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,dx + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\,dy, \qquad \frac{dz}{dt} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt}$$

$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial u} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial u} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial u}, \qquad \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2} = \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial r^2} + \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial u}{\partial r} + \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial\theta^2}$$

**2. Implicit differentiation (§1.17):**

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{f_x}{f_y} = -\frac{p}{q}, \qquad \frac{d^2 y}{dx^2} = -\frac{q^2 r - 2pqs + p^2 t}{q^3}$$

**3. Tangent plane & normal line to $F(x, y, z) = 0$ at $(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ (§1.20):**

$$(x - x_1)F_x + (y - y_1)F_y + (z - z_1)F_z = 0, \qquad \frac{x - x_1}{F_x} = \frac{y - y_1}{F_y} = \frac{z - z_1}{F_z}$$

**4. Error determination (§1.21):**

$$\delta u \approx \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\,\delta x + \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\,\delta y + \dots, \qquad \text{Relative error} = \frac{\delta u}{u}, \qquad \text{Percentage error} = 100\frac{\delta u}{u}$$

**5. Jacobians & properties (§1.22–1.23):**

$$\frac{\partial(u, v)}{\partial(x, y)} = \begin{vmatrix} u_x & u_y \\ v_x & v_y \end{vmatrix}, \qquad \frac{\partial(u, v)}{\partial(x, y)}\cdot\frac{\partial(x, y)}{\partial(u, v)} = 1, \qquad \frac{\partial(u, v)}{\partial(x, y)} = \frac{\partial(u, v)}{\partial(r, s)}\cdot\frac{\partial(r, s)}{\partial(x, y)}$$

$$\text{Functional dependence } f(u, v, w) = 0 \iff \frac{\partial(u, v, w)}{\partial(x, y, z)} = 0$$

**6. Implicit Jacobians & implicit partial derivatives (§1.24–1.25):**

$$\frac{\partial(u_1,\dots,u_n)}{\partial(x_1,\dots,x_n)} = (-1)^n \frac{\partial(f_1,\dots,f_n)/\partial(x_1,\dots,x_n)}{\partial(f_1,\dots,f_n)/\partial(u_1,\dots,u_n)}, \qquad \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = -\frac{\partial(f_1, f_2)/\partial(x, v)}{\partial(f_1, f_2)/\partial(u, v)}$$

</RuleBox>
