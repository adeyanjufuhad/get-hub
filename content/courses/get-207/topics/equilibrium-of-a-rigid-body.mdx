## Overview

In the preceding chapter, we established that any system of external forces acting on a rigid body can be reduced to an equivalent force-couple system at an arbitrary reference point $O$. When both the resultant force and the resultant couple moment vanish, the external forces form a system equivalent to zero and the rigid body is in **static equilibrium**.

This chapter develops a systematic methodology for analyzing the equilibrium of rigid bodies in both two and three dimensions. We begin with the universal vector and scalar conditions of equilibrium (§4.1) and the discipline of drawing a complete **free-body diagram** (§4.2). We then classify two-dimensional supports (§4.3), solve planar equilibrium problems (§4.4), diagnose **statical indeterminacy** and **partial or improper constraints** (§4.5), leverage the special geometry of **two-force** (§4.6) and **three-force bodies** (§4.7), and extend the entire framework to **three-dimensional rigid bodies** and spatial supports (§4.8–4.9).

---

### 4.1 Introduction

When the resultant force $\mathbf{R}$ and the resultant couple moment $\mathbf{M}_O^R$ of all external forces acting on a rigid body are both zero, the external force system is equivalent to zero and the body experiences neither translational nor rotational acceleration.

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="§4.1 Necessary and Sufficient Conditions for Rigid-Body Equilibrium">

A rigid body is in equilibrium when the external forces acting on it satisfy the two vector equations:

$$
\sum \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0} \qquad \sum \mathbf{M}_O = \sum (\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}) = \mathbf{0} \tag{4.1}
$$

Resolving each force and moment into rectangular $x, y, z$ components yields six independent scalar equilibrium equations:

$$
\sum F_x = 0 \qquad \sum F_y = 0 \qquad \sum F_z = 0 \tag{4.2}
$$

$$
\sum M_x = 0 \qquad \sum M_y = 0 \qquad \sum M_z = 0 \tag{4.3}
$$

Equations (4.2) enforce translational balance along the $x$, $y$, and $z$ axes, while Equations (4.3) enforce rotational balance about those axes.

</RuleBox>

---

### 4.2 Free-Body Diagram

Omitting an acting force or introducing an extraneous one invalidates the equilibrium equations. Every equilibrium solution therefore begins by isolating the body of interest in a **free-body diagram (FBD)**.

<RuleBox kind="working-rule" title="§4.2 Five-Step Procedure for Drawing a Free-Body Diagram">

1. **Select and isolate the free body:** Decide clearly which rigid body (or connected assembly) is to be analyzed, detach it from the ground and all surrounding bodies, and sketch its isolated contour.
2. **Show all external forces:** Indicate every force exerted *on* the free body by the ground and detached bodies at the points of support or connection. Include the body's weight $\mathbf{W}$ applied at its center of gravity $G$. Do **not** include internal forces between parts that remain connected inside the chosen free body.
3. **Label known external forces:** Mark the magnitudes and directions (lines of action and senses) of all known applied loads and weights clearly. Remember these are forces exerted *on*, not *by*, the body.
4. **Represent unknown reactions:** At each support or connection where motion is constrained, introduce the appropriate unknown reaction forces and/or couples (see §4.3 for 2D and §4.9 for 3D).
5. **Include essential dimensions:** Record all lengths and angles needed to compute moments and resolve components, omitting unnecessary geometric clutter.

</RuleBox>

### 4.3 Reactions at Supports and Connections for a Two-Dimensional Structure

In a two-dimensional (planar) structure, the body and all applied forces lie in a single plane, and all support reactions lie in that same plane. Beer & Johnston classify 2D supports and connections into three groups according to the number of unknown scalar quantities they introduce.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§4.3 Three Groups of 2D Support Reactions">

1. **Force with Known Line of Action (1 unknown):** Caused by *rollers, rockers, frictionless surfaces, short cables, short links, collars on frictionless rods,* and *frictionless pins in slots*. The single unknown is the magnitude of the reaction.
   - A *frictionless surface* pushes **toward** the free body; a *cable* pulls **away** from the free body.
   - Double-track rollers, short links, collars on rods, and pins in slots can exert forces in **either sense** along the known line of action (single-track rollers and rockers are also generally assumed reversible).
2. **Force of Unknown Direction and Magnitude (2 unknowns):** Caused by *frictionless pins in fitted holes, hinges,* and *rough surfaces*. They prevent translation in all directions in the plane while permitting rotation about the connection. They are normally represented by rectangular components $R_x$ and $R_y$ (on a rough surface, the normal component must be directed away from the surface).
3. **Force and Couple (3 unknowns):** Caused by *fixed supports*, which prevent both translation and rotation completely. The distributed contact forces reduce to a force of two components ($R_x, R_y$) and a couple moment $M$.

When the sense of an unknown reaction component or couple is not immediately obvious, assume a sense arbitrarily; a **negative** numerical result simply indicates that the actual sense is opposite to the assumed sense.

</RuleBox>

### 4.4 Equilibrium of a Rigid Body in Two Dimensions

Choosing the $x$ and $y$ axes in the plane of the structure makes $F_z = 0$ and $M_x = M_y = 0$ identically, reducing the six general equations to three independent scalar equations.

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§4.4 Equations of Equilibrium in Two Dimensions">

For a two-dimensional rigid body, the equilibrium conditions reduce to:

$$
\sum F_x = 0 \qquad \sum F_y = 0 \qquad \sum M_O = 0 \tag{4.4}
$$

More generally, moments may be summed about **any** point $A$ in the plane of the structure:

$$
\sum F_x = 0 \qquad \sum F_y = 0 \qquad \sum M_A = 0 \tag{4.5}
$$

Two equivalent alternative forms are equally valid and often faster:

$$
\sum F_x = 0 \qquad \sum M_A = 0 \qquad \sum M_B = 0 \tag{4.6}
$$

*(provided line $AB$ is **not** parallel to the $y$ axis)*, and

$$
\sum M_A = 0 \qquad \sum M_B = 0 \qquad \sum M_C = 0 \tag{4.7}
$$

*(provided points $A$, $B$, and $C$ are **not collinear**)*.

Because there are only **three independent equations**, at most **three unknowns** can be solved. Writing a fourth equilibrium equation (e.g., $\sum M_B = 0$ after Eq. 4.5) provides no new information, though it is valuable as an independent **check** on arithmetic.

</RuleBox>

To avoid solving simultaneous equations, pick moment centers where **two unknown lines of action intersect**, or sum forces perpendicular to **two parallel unknown reactions** so each equation contains only a single unknown.

### 4.5 Statically Indeterminate Reactions. Partial Constraints

Counting unknown reactions and checking their geometric arrangement reveals whether a structure is properly supported.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§4.5 Constraint Classifications in Two Dimensions">

- **Completely Constrained & Statically Determinate:** The supports prevent all rigid-body motion under any loading, and the **3 unknowns** can be found directly from the 3 equilibrium equations.
- **Statically Indeterminate Reactions ($> 3$ unknowns):** Supports provide redundant constraints (e.g., two pins, Fig. 4.4). Some reaction components cannot be determined from statics alone (though partial reactions such as $A_y$ and $B_y$ may still be obtainable).
- **Partially Constrained ($< 3$ unknowns):** Supports do not restrain all possible motions (e.g., two rollers, Fig. 4.5). Equilibrium fails under general loading unless the applied loads happen to satisfy the unrestrained equation.
- **Improperly Constrained (Geometric Instability):** Even with $\ge 3$ unknowns, the body can still move if the support reactions are **parallel** (Fig. 4.6) or **concurrent** at a single point (Fig. 4.7). Improper constraints also render the reactions statically indeterminate.

In summary, a 2D rigid body is completely constrained and statically determinate if and only if: (1) its reactions involve **exactly three unknowns**, and (2) the support reactions are **neither parallel nor concurrent**.

</RuleBox>

### 4.6 Equilibrium of a Two-Force Body

Recognizing a **two-force body** immediately reduces two unknown force components at each pin to a single unknown magnitude along a known line of action.

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="§4.6 Equilibrium of a Two-Force Body">

A **two-force body** is a rigid body subjected to forces acting at **only two points**, $A$ and $B$ (or two sets of forces whose resultants $\mathbf{F}_1$ and $\mathbf{F}_2$ act at $A$ and $B$).

If a two-force body is in equilibrium, then $\mathbf{F}_1$ and $\mathbf{F}_2$ must have:
1. the **same magnitude** ($F_1 = F_2$),
2. the **same line of action** (line $AB$ joining the two points of application), and
3. **opposite sense** (either pulling in tension or pushing in compression).

</RuleBox>

### 4.7 Equilibrium of a Three-Force Body

Similarly, when forces act on a rigid body at only three points, moment equilibrium places a strong geometric constraint on their lines of action.

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="§4.7 Equilibrium of a Three-Force Body">

A **three-force body** is a rigid body subjected to three forces $\mathbf{F}_1, \mathbf{F}_2, \mathbf{F}_3$ (or forces acting at only three points $A, B, C$).

If a three-force body is in equilibrium, the lines of action of the three forces must be either **concurrent** (intersecting at a single point $D$, proved by $\sum M_D = 0$) or **parallel**.

This property allows problems with three forces to be solved directly from geometry and a closed **force triangle** using the law of sines or law of cosines.

</RuleBox>

### 4.8 Equilibrium of a Rigid Body in Three Dimensions

In three dimensions, all six scalar equations (4.2) and (4.3) are required in general and can be solved for up to **six unknowns**.

<RuleBox kind="working-rule" title="§4.8 Vector Formulation of 3D Equilibrium">

In most 3D problems, it is cleaner to write the equilibrium conditions first in vector form:

$$
\sum \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0} \qquad \sum \mathbf{M}_O = \sum (\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}) = \mathbf{0} \tag{4.1}
$$

1. Express every force $\mathbf{F}$ and position vector $\mathbf{r}$ in rectangular components $(\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k})$.
2. Choose the moment center $O$ at a support with the most unknown force components (such as a ball-and-socket joint or hinge) to eliminate up to three unknowns from $\sum \mathbf{M}_O = \mathbf{0}$.
3. Compute all cross products $\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}$ directly or by determinants.
4. Equate the coefficients of $\mathbf{i}$, $\mathbf{j}$, and $\mathbf{k}$ in both $\sum \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$ and $\sum \mathbf{M}_O = \mathbf{0}$ to zero to obtain the six scalar equations.
5. Where helpful, eliminate reactions at two points $A$ and $B$ simultaneously by taking moments about the axis $AB$ joining them: $\sum M_{AB} = \boldsymbol{\lambda}_{AB} \cdot \sum (\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}) = 0$.

</RuleBox>

### 4.9 Reactions at Supports and Connections for a Three-Dimensional Structure

To determine the reaction components at a 3D support or connection, identify which of the **six fundamental motions** (translations along $x, y, z$ and rotations about $x, y, z$) are prevented: each prevented translation introduces an unknown force component, and each prevented rotation introduces an unknown couple component.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§4.9 3D Support Reactions & Constraint Conditions">

- **1 Unknown:** Ball, frictionless surface, or cable (force with known line of action).
- **2 Unknowns:** Roller on rough surface or wheel on rail (two force components).
- **3 Unknowns:** Rough surface or ball-and-socket joint (three force components $F_x, F_y, F_z$).
- **4 Unknowns:** Universal joint (three force components and one couple).
- **6 Unknowns:** Fixed support (three force components and three couples $M_x, M_y, M_z$).
- **Hinges and Bearings:**
  - *Radial load only* (e.g., journal or roller bearing): **2 force components** perpendicular to the shaft axis (plus 2 couples if used alone, though normally omitted when paired with another support).
  - *Axial thrust and radial load* (e.g., pin-and-bracket, ball bearing): **3 force components** (plus 2 couples only if required for equilibrium or explicitly designed to resist couples).

If there are **$> 6$ unknowns**, the reactions are statically indeterminate. If there are **$< 6$ unknowns**, the body is partially constrained (though it may still remain in equilibrium under particular loads if the extra equations reduce to $0 = 0$). Even with $\ge 6$ unknowns, the body is **improperly constrained** if all reactions are **parallel** or **intersect a common line**.

</RuleBox>

## Common Exam Pitfalls

<Callout variant="warning" title="Frequent Mistakes in Rigid-Body Equilibrium Problems">

1. **Omitting zero reaction components prematurely:** Never drop $B_x$ at a pin just because there are no visible horizontal loads (Sample Problem 4.2). Keep all support reaction unknowns on the FBD and show $\sum F_x = 0 \implies B_x = 0$ explicitly so you preserve the equation count.
2. **Double-counting or omitting internal forces:** When isolating a composite assembly (frame + cable), never draw the internal tension inside the cut-free segment; draw forces only where external supports or cables are cut. Conversely, always include the body's weight at $G$ unless stated as negligible.
3. **Misreading 3D bearing thrust assumptions:** A radial bearing or hinge exerts two force components unless it is the *only* axial restraint (or explicitly carries axial thrust). Never arbitrarily add couple moments $M_y, M_z$ at aligned hinges unless a single hinge acts alone.
4. **Assuming 3 unknowns guarantee determinacy:** Three parallel reactions or three concurrent reactions (all lines of action passing through one point) are **improperly constrained** and cannot resist general loading.
5. **Treating a bent member with an applied load or couple as a two-force body:** A member is a two-force body *only* if forces act at two points with **no** intermediate loads, weights, or couple moments.

</Callout>

| General 3D Scalar Equilibrium | $\sum F_x = \sum F_y = \sum F_z = 0$, $\quad \sum M_x = \sum M_y = \sum M_z = 0$ | Max 6 unknowns (Eqs. 4.2–4.3) |

| Three-Force Body | Lines of action of $\mathbf{F}_1, \mathbf{F}_2, \mathbf{F}_3$ are concurrent or parallel | Solve via geometry + force triangle |
