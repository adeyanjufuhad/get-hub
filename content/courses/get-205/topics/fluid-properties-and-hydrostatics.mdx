## 1. Overview

From sizing water supply mains on OAU campus to designing hydraulic jacks in Mechanical Engineering or chemical reactors in CHE, fluids behave very differently from rigid solids. A **fluid** cannot resist a shear force at rest—no matter how tiny the shear stress, it continuously deforms (flows).

GET 205 (Fundamentals of Fluid Mechanics) begins with **fluid properties** (especially density and viscosity) and **hydrostatics** (fluids at rest). Get comfortable with units, specific gravity, and datum pressure balances here, because Bernoulli's equation and pipe-loss problems later in the course build directly on them.

## 2. Key Concepts

### Fundamental Fluid Properties

- **Density ($\rho$):** Mass per unit volume ($\text{kg/m}^3$). For water at $4^\circ\text{C}$, $\rho_w \approx 1000\,\text{kg/m}^3$.
- **Specific Weight ($\gamma$):** Weight per unit volume ($\text{N/m}^3$):
  $$\gamma = \rho g$$
- **Specific Gravity ($SG$):** Ratio of a fluid's density to the density of water at $4^\circ\text{C}$ (dimensionless):
  $$SG = \frac{\rho}{\rho_{\text{water}}} = \frac{\gamma}{\gamma_{\text{water}}}$$
- **Dynamic Viscosity ($\mu$):** Measures a fluid's internal resistance to shear deformation ($\text{Pa}\cdot\text{s}$ or $\text{N}\cdot\text{s/m}^2$). For a Newtonian fluid with linear velocity gradient $\frac{du}{dy}$:
  $$\tau = \mu \frac{du}{dy}$$
  while **kinematic viscosity** is $\nu = \frac{\mu}{\rho}$ ($\text{m}^2\text{/s}$).

### Hydrostatic Pressure & U-Tube Manometry

In a static, incompressible fluid of constant density $\rho$, pressure increases linearly with vertical depth $h$ below a reference level:

$$P = P_0 + \rho g h$$

Points at the **same horizontal elevation** inside the **same continuous static fluid** share the exact same pressure (Pascal's Law). When solving any U-tube manometer:
1. Pick a horizontal **datum line $Z\text{–}Z$** passing through the lower meniscus where the continuous manometer fluid connects both legs.
2. Equate pressures at $Z\text{–}Z$ on the left and right sides.
3. Move upward (subtract $\rho g h$) or downward (add $\rho g h$) column by column.

### Hydrostatic Force on Vertical Submerged Plane Surfaces

Because gauge pressure grows linearly from $0$ at the free surface to $\rho g H$ at depth $H$, the pressure distribution forms a triangle (pressure prism):

$$F_R = \rho g \bar{h} A$$

where $\bar{h}$ is the vertical depth to the **centroid** of the submerged area $A$. For a vertical rectangular gate of width $b$ and depth $H$ whose top edge sits at the free surface ($\bar{h} = \frac{H}{2}$, $A = bH$), the resultant force acts at the **centre of pressure** $h_p = \frac{2}{3}H$ below the surface.

## 4. Common Mistakes

<Callout variant="warning" title="Watch out for these exam traps">
- **Forgetting to convert millimetres or centimetres to metres:** Always convert `mm` ($10^{-3}\,\text{m}$) and `cm` ($10^{-2}\,\text{m}$) before multiplying by $\rho g$, otherwise your pressure will be off by a factor of $100$ or $1000$.
- **Mixing gauge and absolute pressure:** When a manometer leg is open to the air and the question asks for **gauge pressure**, set $P_{\text{atm}} = 0$. Only add $101.3\,\text{kPa}$ if **absolute pressure** is requested.
- **Putting $F_R$ at the centroid $\bar{h}$ instead of the centre of pressure $h_p$:** Magnitude uses the centroid depth $\bar{h}$, but the line of action on a vertical wall always sits *deeper* at $h_p = \bar{h} + \frac{I_{xx}}{\bar{h}A}$ (which equals $\frac{2}{3}H$ for a rectangle starting at the surface).
</Callout>
