## Overview

Engineering quantities such as displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, electric field and fluid velocity possess both magnitude and direction. Chapter 5 of H. K. Dass builds the complete vector toolkit used across mechanics, electromagnetism and fluid flow: first **vector algebra** (§5.1–§5.21 — addition, rectangular resolution, dot and cross products, triple and quadruple products), then **vector differentiation** of functions of a single scalar parameter (§5.22–§5.24 — tangents, velocity and acceleration), and finally **vector differential calculus** using the operator $\nabla$ (§5.25–§5.32 — gradient, level surfaces, directional derivatives, divergence, solenoidal fields, curl, irrotational fields, scalar potentials and vector identities). Every section below follows Dass's exact numbering from §5.1 to §5.32, together with his solved examples and end-of-section exercises.

### 5.1 Vectors

Physical quantities fall into two classes: **scalars**, specified completely by a single numerical magnitude (such as mass, length, temperature or time), and **vectors**, which require both a magnitude and a direction in space.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§5.1 Vector quantity">

A **vector** is a quantity having both **magnitude** and **direction**, such as force, velocity, acceleration or displacement. Geometrically, a vector $\vec{a} = \overrightarrow{OA}$ is represented by a directed line segment whose length equals its magnitude $|\vec{a}|$ and whose arrow indicates its direction.

</RuleBox>

### 5.2 Addition of Vectors

To add two vectors $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$, place the tail of $\vec{b}$ at the head of $\vec{a}$. The directed segment drawn from the tail of $\vec{a}$ to the head of $\vec{b}$ represents their sum (the **triangle law of vector addition**).

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§5.2 Triangle law of addition of vectors">

If $\overrightarrow{OA} = \vec{a}$ and $\overrightarrow{AB} = \vec{b}$, then the vector $\overrightarrow{OB}$ is the sum of $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$:

$$\overrightarrow{OA} + \overrightarrow{AB} = \overrightarrow{OB}, \qquad \text{i.e., } \vec{a} + \vec{b} = \overrightarrow{OB}.$$

</RuleBox>

### 5.3 Rectangular Resolution of a Vector

Let $OX$, $OY$ and $OZ$ be three mutually perpendicular coordinate axes, and let $\hat{i}$, $\hat{j}$ and $\hat{k}$ denote unit vectors along the positive $x$-, $y$- and $z$-directions respectively. If a point $P$ has coordinates $(x,y,z)$, drop a perpendicular $PF$ onto the $XY$-plane so $F=(x,y,0)$, and draw $FA \perp OX$ and $FB \perp OY$. Then $\overrightarrow{OA}=x\hat{i}$, $\overrightarrow{OB}=y\hat{j}$ and $\overrightarrow{OC}=z\hat{k}$. Applying the triangle law twice gives

$$\overrightarrow{OP} = \overrightarrow{OF} + \overrightarrow{FP} = (\overrightarrow{OA} + \overrightarrow{AF}) + \overrightarrow{FP} = \overrightarrow{OA} + \overrightarrow{OB} + \overrightarrow{OC}.$$

By the Pythagorean theorem, $OP^2 = OF^2 + FP^2 = OA^2 + AF^2 + FP^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2$.

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§5.3 Rectangular components and magnitude of a vector">

For a point $P(x,y,z)$ with position vector $\vec{r} = \overrightarrow{OP}$:

$$\vec{r} = x\hat{i} + y\hat{j} + z\hat{k}, \qquad |\vec{r}| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}.$$

</RuleBox>

<DiagramFigure src="/diagrams/get-209/vectors-gradient-divergence-and-curl-resolution.svg" caption="H. K. Dass Fig. 5.3 (redrawn) — Rectangular resolution of OP = r into x i + y j + z k." />

### 5.4 Unit Vector

A **unit vector** has magnitude $1$. Dividing any non-zero vector by its own magnitude produces the unit vector pointing in the same direction.

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§5.4 Unit vector in the direction of a given vector">

For a non-zero vector $\vec{r} = x\hat{i} + y\hat{j} + z\hat{k}$, the unit vector along $\vec{r}$ is

$$\hat{r} = \frac{\vec{r}}{|\vec{r}|} = \frac{x\hat{i} + y\hat{j} + z\hat{k}}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}}.$$

</RuleBox>

### 5.5 Position Vector of a Point

The **position vector** of a point $A$ relative to a fixed origin $O$ is the vector $\overrightarrow{OA}$, which uniquely locates $A$ in space. If $A$ and $B$ have position vectors $\vec{a} = \overrightarrow{OA}$ and $\vec{b} = \overrightarrow{OB}$, then by the triangle law $\overrightarrow{OA} + \overrightarrow{AB} = \overrightarrow{OB}$, so $\overrightarrow{AB} = \overrightarrow{OB} - \overrightarrow{OA}$.

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§5.5 Displacement vector between two points">

If the position vectors of $A$ and $B$ are $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$, then

$$\overrightarrow{AB} = \vec{b} - \vec{a} = (\text{Position vector of } B) - (\text{Position vector of } A).$$

</RuleBox>

### 5.6 Ratio Formula

Suppose a point $C$ divides the line segment $AB$ internally in the ratio $m : n$, where $A$ and $B$ have position vectors $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ relative to origin $O$. Because $AC = \dfrac{m}{m+n}AB$ along the same direction, we have

$$\overrightarrow{OC} = \overrightarrow{OA} + \overrightarrow{AC} = \vec{a} + \frac{m}{m+n}(\vec{b} - \vec{a}) = \frac{m\vec{b} + n\vec{a}}{m+n}.$$

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§5.6 Section (ratio) formula and mid-point corollary">

If $C$ divides the join of $A(\vec{a})$ and $B(\vec{b})$ internally in the ratio $m : n$, then

$$\overrightarrow{OC} = \frac{m\vec{b} + n\vec{a}}{m + n}.$$

**Corollary:** Setting $m = n = 1$ gives the position vector of the mid-point of $AB$:

$$\overrightarrow{OC} = \frac{\vec{a} + \vec{b}}{2}.$$

</RuleBox>

### 5.7 Product of Two Vectors

Two vectors can be multiplied in two fundamentally different ways: one yields a scalar (a pure number), written $\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}$ and called the **scalar (or dot) product**, and the other yields a vector, written $\vec{a} \times \vec{b}$ and called the **vector (or cross) product**.

### 5.8 Scalar, or Dot Product

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§5.8 Scalar (dot) product of two vectors">

The **scalar (or dot) product** of two vectors $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ inclined at an angle $\theta$ is the scalar

$$\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = |\vec{a}|\,|\vec{b}|\cos\theta.$$

- **Commutativity:** $\vec{b}\cdot\vec{a} = |\vec{b}||\vec{a}|\cos(-\theta) = |\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\cos\theta = \vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}$.
- **Geometrical interpretation:** If $\overrightarrow{OA} = \vec{a}$, $\overrightarrow{OB} = \vec{b}$ and $BN \perp OA$, then $\cos\theta = \dfrac{ON}{OB}$, so $\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b} = (OA)(ON) = (\text{length of }\vec{a})(\text{projection of }\vec{b}\text{ along }\vec{a})$.

</RuleBox>

<DiagramFigure src="/diagrams/get-209/vectors-gradient-divergence-and-curl-dot-product.svg" caption="H. K. Dass §5.8 (redrawn) — Geometrical meaning of the dot product: a · b = (OA)(ON)." />

### 5.9 Useful Results

Since $\hat{i}$, $\hat{j}$ and $\hat{k}$ are mutually perpendicular unit vectors ($\cos 0^\circ = 1$, $\cos 90^\circ = 0$), the dot product obeys simple algebraic rules in component form.

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§5.9 Dot product identities and orthogonality test">

$$\hat{i}\cdot\hat{i} = \hat{j}\cdot\hat{j} = \hat{k}\cdot\hat{k} = 1, \qquad \hat{i}\cdot\hat{j} = \hat{j}\cdot\hat{k} = \hat{k}\cdot\hat{i} = 0.$$

Consequently, if $\vec{a} = a_1\hat{i} + a_2\hat{j} + a_3\hat{k}$ and $\vec{b} = b_1\hat{i} + b_2\hat{j} + b_3\hat{k}$, then

$$\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b} = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + a_3 b_3.$$

**Perpendicularity condition:** Two non-zero vectors $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ are **perpendicular (orthogonal)** if and only if $\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b} = 0$.

</RuleBox>

### 5.10 Work Done as a Scalar Product

When a constant force $\vec{F}$ acts on a particle and moves it along a straight displacement $\overrightarrow{AB}$ inclined at angle $\theta$ to $\vec{F}$, only the component $F\cos\theta$ along $\overrightarrow{AB}$ performs work.

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§5.10 Work done by a constant force">

$$\text{Work done} = (F\cos\theta)\cdot AB = \vec{F}\cdot\overrightarrow{AB} = \text{Force}\cdot\text{Displacement}.$$

</RuleBox>

<DiagramFigure src="/diagrams/get-209/vectors-gradient-divergence-and-curl-work-done.svg" caption="H. K. Dass §5.10 (redrawn) — Work done by force F through displacement AB is F · AB." />

### 5.11 Vector Product or Cross Product

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§5.11 Vector (cross) product and unit-vector rules">

The **vector (or cross) product** $\vec{a}\times\vec{b}$ of two vectors $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ is a vector defined by:

1. **Magnitude:** $|\vec{a}\times\vec{b}| = |\vec{a}|\,|\vec{b}|\sin\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$.
2. **Direction:** Perpendicular to both $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$, forming a **right-handed system** $(\vec{a},\vec{b},\hat{\eta})$.

Writing $\hat{\eta}$ for the unit vector perpendicular to both $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ in the right-handed sense:

$$\vec{a}\times\vec{b} = |\vec{a}|\,|\vec{b}|\sin\theta\,\hat{\eta}, \qquad \vec{b}\times\vec{a} = -(\vec{a}\times\vec{b}).$$

**Useful results for $\hat{i},\hat{j},\hat{k}$:**

$$\hat{i}\times\hat{i} = \hat{j}\times\hat{j} = \hat{k}\times\hat{k} = \vec{0},$$

$$\hat{i}\times\hat{j} = -\hat{j}\times\hat{i} = \hat{k}, \qquad \hat{j}\times\hat{k} = -\hat{k}\times\hat{j} = \hat{i}, \qquad \hat{k}\times\hat{i} = -\hat{i}\times\hat{k} = \hat{j}.$$

</RuleBox>

<DiagramFigure src="/diagrams/get-209/vectors-gradient-divergence-and-curl-cross-product.svg" caption="H. K. Dass §5.11 (redrawn) — Right-handed cross product a × b = |a||b| sin θ η." />

### 5.12 Vector Product Expressed as a Determinant

Let $\vec{a} = a_1\hat{i} + a_2\hat{j} + a_3\hat{k}$ and $\vec{b} = b_1\hat{i} + b_2\hat{j} + b_3\hat{k}$. Expanding $(a_1\hat{i}+a_2\hat{j}+a_3\hat{k})\times(b_1\hat{i}+b_2\hat{j}+b_3\hat{k})$ term by term and applying the unit-vector cross product identities gives

$$\vec{a}\times\vec{b} = (a_2 b_3 - a_3 b_2)\hat{i} - (a_1 b_3 - a_3 b_1)\hat{j} + (a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1)\hat{k}.$$

This expansion matches a $3\times 3$ determinant along its first row.

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§5.12 Determinant form of the cross product">

$$\vec{a}\times\vec{b} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \\ b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \end{vmatrix} = (a_2 b_3 - a_3 b_2)\hat{i} - (a_1 b_3 - a_3 b_1)\hat{j} + (a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1)\hat{k}.$$

</RuleBox>

### 5.13 Area of Parallelogram

If $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ represent two adjacent sides of a parallelogram inclined at angle $\theta$, the altitude is $|\vec{b}|\sin\theta$, so the **vector area** of the parallelogram is $\vec{a}\times\vec{b}$ and its scalar area is $|\vec{a}\times\vec{b}|$. (Accordingly, the area of the triangle with adjacent sides $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ is $\frac{1}{2}|\vec{a}\times\vec{b}|$.)

### 5.14 Moment of a Force

Let a force $\vec{F} = \overrightarrow{PQ}$ act at a point $P$ whose position vector from a fixed origin $O$ is $\vec{r} = \overrightarrow{OP}$, and let $ON$ be the perpendicular from $O$ onto the line of action of $\vec{F}$. Then $ON = OP\sin\theta$, so the turning moment about $O$ is $(PQ)(ON)\hat{\eta} = (OP)(PQ)\sin\theta\,\hat{\eta} = \overrightarrow{OP}\times\overrightarrow{PQ}$.

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§5.14 Moment of a force about O">

$$\vec{M} = \vec{r}\times\vec{F},$$

where $\vec{r} = \overrightarrow{OP}$ is the position vector from $O$ to any point $P$ on the line of action of $\vec{F}$.

</RuleBox>

<DiagramFigure src="/diagrams/get-209/vectors-gradient-divergence-and-curl-moment-of-force.svg" caption="H. K. Dass §5.14 (redrawn) — Moment of force F about O: M = r × F with magnitude F · ON." />

### 5.15 Angular Velocity

Consider a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis $OA$ with angular velocity vector $\vec{\omega}$ (magnitude $\omega$ rad/s, directed along the rotation axis $OA$). For any particle $P$ on the body at position vector $\vec{r} = \overrightarrow{OP}$ making angle $\theta$ with $OA$, the circular orbit radius is $AP = r\sin\theta$, so the speed of $P$ is $\omega\cdot AP = \omega r\sin\theta$ directed perpendicular to both $\vec{\omega}$ and $\vec{r}$.

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§5.15 Linear velocity in rigid-body rotation">

$$\vec{V} = \vec{\omega}\times\vec{r}.$$

</RuleBox>

<DiagramFigure src="/diagrams/get-209/vectors-gradient-divergence-and-curl-angular-velocity.svg" caption="H. K. Dass §5.15 (redrawn) — Linear velocity V = ω × r of a point P on a rotating rigid body." />

### 5.16 Scalar Triple Product

Given three vectors $\vec{a} = a_1\hat{i}+a_2\hat{j}+a_3\hat{k}$, $\vec{b} = b_1\hat{i}+b_2\hat{j}+b_3\hat{k}$ and $\vec{c} = c_1\hat{i}+c_2\hat{j}+c_3\hat{k}$, the scalar $\vec{a}\cdot(\vec{b}\times\vec{c})$ is called their **scalar triple product** (or **box product**) and is denoted by $[\vec{a}\;\vec{b}\;\vec{c}]$. Taking the dot product of $\vec{a}$ with $\vec{b}\times\vec{c} = (b_2 c_3 - b_3 c_2)\hat{i} + (b_3 c_1 - b_1 c_3)\hat{j} + (b_1 c_2 - b_2 c_1)\hat{k}$ yields a $3\times 3$ determinant.

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§5.16 Scalar triple product and its properties">

$$[\vec{a}\;\vec{b}\;\vec{c}] = \vec{a}\cdot(\vec{b}\times\vec{c}) = \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \\ b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \\ c_1 & c_2 & c_3 \end{vmatrix}.$$

- **Cyclic invariance:** $\vec{a}\cdot(\vec{b}\times\vec{c}) = \vec{b}\cdot(\vec{c}\times\vec{a}) = \vec{c}\cdot(\vec{a}\times\vec{b})$.
- **Reversing cyclic order changes the sign:** $[\vec{a}\;\vec{c}\;\vec{b}] = -[\vec{a}\;\vec{b}\;\vec{c}]$.
- **Dot and cross may be interchanged:** $\vec{a}\cdot(\vec{b}\times\vec{c}) = (\vec{a}\times\vec{b})\cdot\vec{c}$.
- **Repeated vector gives zero:** $[\vec{a}\;\vec{a}\;\vec{c}] = 0$ (two identical rows in the determinant).
- Expressions like $\vec{a}\times(\vec{b}\cdot\vec{c})$ or $(\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b})\times\vec{c}$ are **meaningless** because the cross product requires two vectors.

</RuleBox>

### 5.17 Geometrical Interpretation

Let $\vec{a}$, $\vec{b}$ and $\vec{c}$ be three coterminous edges of a parallelepiped meeting at $O$. The base $OBDC$ spanned by $\vec{b}$ and $\vec{c}$ has vector area $\vec{b}\times\vec{c} = (\text{Area of }\|gm\,OBDC)\hat{\eta}$, where $\hat{\eta}$ is unit normal to the base. Dotting with $\vec{a}$ multiplies the base area by the perpendicular height $\vec{a}\cdot\hat{\eta}$ between the parallel faces $OBDC$ and $AEFG$.

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="§5.17 Volumes of parallelepiped and tetrahedron; coplanarity test">

1. **Volume of parallelepiped** with coterminous edges $\vec{a},\vec{b},\vec{c}$:

$$V_{\text{parallelepiped}} = \big|\vec{a}\cdot(\vec{b}\times\vec{c})\big| = \big|[\vec{a}\;\vec{b}\;\vec{c}]\big|.$$

2. **Coplanarity condition:** Three non-zero vectors $\vec{a},\vec{b},\vec{c}$ are **coplanar** (linearly dependent) if and only if

$$[\vec{a}\;\vec{b}\;\vec{c}] = \vec{a}\cdot(\vec{b}\times\vec{c}) = 0.$$

3. **Volume of tetrahedron** with coterminous edges $\vec{a},\vec{b},\vec{c}$ (or vertices $A,B,C,D$ with edges $\overrightarrow{AB},\overrightarrow{AC},\overrightarrow{AD}$):

$$V_{\text{tetrahedron}} = \frac{1}{6}\big|[\vec{a}\;\vec{b}\;\vec{c}]\big|.$$

</RuleBox>

### 5.19 Vector Product of Three Vectors

The **vector triple product** $\vec{a}\times(\vec{b}\times\vec{c})$ produces a vector perpendicular to $\vec{b}\times\vec{c}$, hence lying in the plane spanned by $\vec{b}$ and $\vec{c}$. Expanding in rectangular components $\vec{a}=(a_1,a_2,a_3)$, $\vec{b}=(b_1,b_2,b_3)$, $\vec{c}=(c_1,c_2,c_3)$ gives the fundamental **BAC − CAB** rule:

$$\vec{a}\times(\vec{b}\times\vec{c}) = (a_1 c_1 + a_2 c_2 + a_3 c_3)\vec{b} - (a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + a_3 b_3)\vec{c} = (\vec{a}\cdot\vec{c})\vec{b} - (\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b})\vec{c}.$$

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§5.19 Vector triple product (BAC − CAB rule)">

$$\vec{a}\times(\vec{b}\times\vec{c}) = (\vec{a}\cdot\vec{c})\vec{b} - (\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b})\vec{c}$$

$$(\vec{a}\times\vec{b})\times\vec{c} = (\vec{a}\cdot\vec{c})\vec{b} - (\vec{b}\cdot\vec{c})\vec{a}$$

Notice that the vector triple product is **not associative**: in general, $\vec{a}\times(\vec{b}\times\vec{c}) \neq (\vec{a}\times\vec{b})\times\vec{c}$. The rule is always **(outer $\cdot$ far) middle − (outer $\cdot$ near) far**.

</RuleBox>

### 5.20 Scalar Product of Four Vectors

To simplify $(\vec{a}\times\vec{b})\cdot(\vec{c}\times\vec{d})$, set $\vec{r} = \vec{c}\times\vec{d}$. Interchanging the dot and cross in the scalar triple product $(\vec{a}\times\vec{b})\cdot\vec{r}$ gives

$$(\vec{a}\times\vec{b})\cdot(\vec{c}\times\vec{d}) = \vec{a}\cdot(\vec{b}\times\vec{r}) = \vec{a}\cdot\big[\vec{b}\times(\vec{c}\times\vec{d})\big] = \vec{a}\cdot\big[(\vec{b}\cdot\vec{d})\vec{c} - (\vec{b}\cdot\vec{c})\vec{d}\big] = (\vec{a}\cdot\vec{c})(\vec{b}\cdot\vec{d}) - (\vec{a}\cdot\vec{d})(\vec{b}\cdot\vec{c}).$$

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§5.20 Lagrange's identity (scalar product of four vectors)">

$$(\vec{a}\times\vec{b})\cdot(\vec{c}\times\vec{d}) = (\vec{a}\cdot\vec{c})(\vec{b}\cdot\vec{d}) - (\vec{a}\cdot\vec{d})(\vec{b}\cdot\vec{c}) = \begin{vmatrix} \vec{a}\cdot\vec{c} & \vec{a}\cdot\vec{d} \\ \vec{b}\cdot\vec{c} & \vec{b}\cdot\vec{d} \end{vmatrix}.$$

</RuleBox>

### 5.21 Vector Product of Four Vectors

The vector product $(\vec{a}\times\vec{b})\times(\vec{c}\times\vec{d})$ can be expanded in two equivalent ways:

1. Setting $\vec{r} = \vec{a}\times\vec{b}$ gives $\vec{r}\times(\vec{c}\times\vec{d}) = (\vec{r}\cdot\vec{d})\vec{c} - (\vec{r}\cdot\vec{c})\vec{d} = [\vec{a}\;\vec{b}\;\vec{d}]\vec{c} - [\vec{a}\;\vec{b}\;\vec{c}]\vec{d}$, showing the result lies in the plane of $\vec{c}$ and $\vec{d}$.
2. Setting $\vec{s} = \vec{c}\times\vec{d}$ gives $(\vec{a}\times\vec{b})\times\vec{s} = -\vec{s}\times(\vec{a}\times\vec{b}) = [\vec{a}\;\vec{c}\;\vec{d}]\vec{b} - [\vec{b}\;\vec{c}\;\vec{d}]\vec{a}$, showing it also lies in the plane of $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$.

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§5.21 Vector product of four vectors and geometrical meaning">

$$(\vec{a}\times\vec{b})\times(\vec{c}\times\vec{d}) = [\vec{a}\;\vec{b}\;\vec{d}]\vec{c} - [\vec{a}\;\vec{b}\;\vec{c}]\vec{d} = [\vec{a}\;\vec{c}\;\vec{d}]\vec{b} - [\vec{b}\;\vec{c}\;\vec{d}]\vec{a}.$$

**Geometrical interpretation:** Since $(\vec{a}\times\vec{b})\times(\vec{c}\times\vec{d})$ lies simultaneously in the plane of $\vec{a},\vec{b}$ and in the plane of $\vec{c},\vec{d}$, it is parallel to the **line of intersection** of those two planes.

</RuleBox>

### 5.22 Vector Function

When the components of a vector depend on a single scalar variable $t$ (typically time or arc length), the vector is called a **vector function** of $t$, written $\vec{r} = \vec{r}(t)$ or

$$\vec{f}(t) = f_1(t)\hat{i} + f_2(t)\hat{j} + f_3(t)\hat{k}.$$

As $t$ varies, the tip of the position vector $\vec{r}(t) = x(t)\hat{i} + y(t)\hat{j} + z(t)\hat{k}$ traces a space curve.

### 5.23 Differentiation of Vectors

Let $P$ be the position of a moving particle at time $t$ with $\overrightarrow{OP} = \vec{r}$, and let $Q$ be its position at time $t + \delta t$ with $\overrightarrow{OQ} = \vec{r} + \delta\vec{r}$. The chord displacement is $\overrightarrow{PQ} = \delta\vec{r}$. As $\delta t \to 0$, $Q$ approaches $P$ along the curve and the direction of $\dfrac{\delta\vec{r}}{\delta t}$ approaches the tangent to the path at $P$.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§5.23 Derivative, velocity and acceleration of a vector function">

$$\frac{d\vec{r}}{dt} = \lim_{\delta t \to 0}\frac{\delta\vec{r}}{\delta t} = \frac{dx}{dt}\hat{i} + \frac{dy}{dt}\hat{j} + \frac{dz}{dt}\hat{k}.$$

- $\vec{v} = \dfrac{d\vec{r}}{dt}$ gives the **velocity** of the particle at $P$, directed along the **tangent** to the curve.
- $\hat{T} = \dfrac{\vec{v}}{|\vec{v}|}$ is the **unit tangent vector** to the curve.
- $\vec{a} = \dfrac{d^2\vec{r}}{dt^2}$ gives the **acceleration** of the particle, and its tangential component is $a_t = \vec{a}\cdot\hat{T}$.

</RuleBox>

### 5.24 Formulae of Differentiation

Differentiating sums, scalar multiples, dot products, cross products and triple products follows product-rule patterns identical to scalar calculus, with one crucial rule: **never change the order of vectors in a cross product**.

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§5.24 Vector differentiation rules">

For differentiable vector functions $\vec{F}, \vec{G}, \vec{a}, \vec{b}, \vec{c}$ and scalar function $\phi$ of $t$:

1. $\dfrac{d}{dt}(\vec{F} + \vec{G}) = \dfrac{d\vec{F}}{dt} + \dfrac{d\vec{G}}{dt}$
2. $\dfrac{d}{dt}(\vec{F}\phi) = \dfrac{d\vec{F}}{dt}\phi + \vec{F}\dfrac{d\phi}{dt}$
3. $\dfrac{d}{dt}(\vec{F}\cdot\vec{G}) = \vec{F}\cdot\dfrac{d\vec{G}}{dt} + \dfrac{d\vec{F}}{dt}\cdot\vec{G}$
4. $\dfrac{d}{dt}(\vec{F}\times\vec{G}) = \vec{F}\times\dfrac{d\vec{G}}{dt} + \dfrac{d\vec{F}}{dt}\times\vec{G}$ (preserve order of $\vec{F}$ and $\vec{G}$)
5. $\dfrac{d}{dt}[\vec{a}\;\vec{b}\;\vec{c}] = \left[\dfrac{d\vec{a}}{dt}\;\vec{b}\;\vec{c}\right] + \left[\vec{a}\;\dfrac{d\vec{b}}{dt}\;\vec{c}\right] + \left[\vec{a}\;\vec{b}\;\dfrac{d\vec{c}}{dt}\right]$
6. $\dfrac{d}{dt}\big[\vec{a}\times(\vec{b}\times\vec{c})\big] = \dfrac{d\vec{a}}{dt}\times(\vec{b}\times\vec{c}) + \vec{a}\times\left(\dfrac{d\vec{b}}{dt}\times\vec{c}\right) + \vec{a}\times\left(\vec{b}\times\dfrac{d\vec{c}}{dt}\right)$

</RuleBox>

### 5.25 Scalar and Vector Point Functions

A variable quantity whose value at each point $P(x,y,z)$ in a region $R$ of space depends on the position of $P$ is called a **point function** (or **field**).

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§5.25 Scalar point function, vector point function, and operator del (∇)">

- **Scalar point function (scalar field):** Assigns a unique scalar $\phi(x,y,z)$ to each point $P(x,y,z)\in R$ — e.g., temperature distribution, fluid density, or gravitational potential.
- **Vector point function (vector field):** Assigns a unique vector $\vec{F}(x,y,z)$ to each point $P(x,y,z)\in R$ — e.g., velocity of a moving fluid or gravitational/electric force field.
- **Vector differential operator Del ($\nabla$, read "del" or "nabla"):**

$$\nabla = \hat{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x} + \hat{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial y} + \hat{k}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}.$$

</RuleBox>

### 5.26 Gradient of a Scalar Function

Applying the operator $\nabla$ to a differentiable scalar point function $\phi(x,y,z)$ produces a vector point function called the **gradient** of $\phi$.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§5.26 Gradient of a scalar field">

$$\operatorname{grad}\phi = \nabla\phi = \left(\hat{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x} + \hat{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial y} + \hat{k}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\right)\phi = \hat{i}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x} + \hat{j}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y} + \hat{k}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z}.$$

Note that $\phi$ is a scalar field, while $\nabla\phi$ is a **vector** field.

</RuleBox>

### 5.27 Geometrical Meaning of Gradient, Normal

A surface $\phi(x,y,z) = c$ (constant) on which $\phi$ takes the same value at every point is called a **level surface** (for example, an equipotential or isothermal surface). Two distinct level surfaces cannot intersect.

Consider a point $P$ on the level surface $\phi = c$ with position vector $\vec{r}$, and a nearby point $Q$ at $\vec{r} + d\vec{r}$. Then

$$\nabla\phi\cdot d\vec{r} = \left(\hat{i}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x} + \hat{j}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y} + \hat{k}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z}\right)\cdot(\hat{i}\,dx + \hat{j}\,dy + \hat{k}\,dz) = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}\,dx + \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}\,dy + \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z}\,dz = d\phi.$$

If $Q$ lies on the *same* level surface $\phi = c$, then $d\phi = 0$, so $\nabla\phi\cdot d\vec{r} = 0$ for every tangent displacement $d\vec{r}$ on the surface. Therefore $\nabla\phi$ is **perpendicular (normal)** to the level surface $\phi(x,y,z) = c$.

Writing $\nabla\phi = |\nabla\phi|\hat{N}$ where $\hat{N}$ is the unit normal, and letting $\delta n = \hat{N}\cdot\delta\vec{r}$ be the perpendicular distance between the adjacent level surfaces $\phi$ and $\phi + \delta\phi$, we obtain

$$\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial n} = \lim_{\delta n \to 0}\frac{\delta\phi}{\delta n} = \lim_{\delta n \to 0}\frac{|\nabla\phi|\hat{N}\cdot\delta\vec{r}}{\delta n} = |\nabla\phi|.$$

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="§5.27 Geometrical meaning of grad φ">

1. $\nabla\phi$ is a vector **normal to the level surface** $\phi(x,y,z) = c$ at every point where $\nabla\phi \neq \vec{0}$.
2. Its magnitude $|\nabla\phi| = \dfrac{\partial\phi}{\partial n}$ equals the **maximum rate of change** of $\phi$ with respect to distance (the rate of change along the normal).

</RuleBox>

### 5.28 Normal and Directional Derivative

Let $\hat{N}'$ be a unit vector along an arbitrary direction $PQ$ making angle $\theta$ with the unit normal $\hat{N}$. Since $\delta n = \delta r\cos\theta = (\hat{N}\cdot\hat{N}')\delta r$, the rate of change of $\phi$ along $PQ$ is

$$\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial r} = \lim_{\delta r\to 0}\frac{\delta\phi}{\delta r} = (\hat{N}\cdot\hat{N}')\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial n} = \hat{N}'\cdot(\hat{N}|\nabla\phi|) = \hat{N}'\cdot\nabla\phi = |\nabla\phi|\cos\theta \le |\nabla\phi|.$$

<RuleBox kind="working-rule" title="§5.28 Unit normal, directional derivative and angle between surfaces">

1. **Unit normal vector** to the surface $\phi(x,y,z) = c$ at point $P$:

$$\hat{N} = \frac{\nabla\phi}{|\nabla\phi|}\bigg|_P.$$

2. **Directional derivative** of $\phi$ at $P$ in the direction of a vector $\vec{d}$:

$$D_{\hat{d}}\phi = \nabla\phi\cdot\hat{d} = \nabla\phi\cdot\frac{\vec{d}}{|\vec{d}|}.$$

3. **Greatest rate of increase** of $\phi$ at $P$ occurs in the direction of $\nabla\phi$ and has magnitude $|\nabla\phi|$.
4. **Angle $\theta$ between two intersecting surfaces** $\phi_1(x,y,z) = c_1$ and $\phi_2(x,y,z) = c_2$ at a common point $P$ is the angle between their normals $\nabla\phi_1$ and $\nabla\phi_2$:

$$\cos\theta = \frac{\nabla\phi_1\cdot\nabla\phi_2}{|\nabla\phi_1|\,|\nabla\phi_2|}.$$

The two surfaces intersect **orthogonally** if and only if $\nabla\phi_1\cdot\nabla\phi_2 = 0$ at $P$.

</RuleBox>

### 5.29 Divergence of a Vector Function

Taking the formal scalar (dot) product of the vector differential operator $\nabla$ with a differentiable vector field $\vec{F}$ produces a scalar field called the **divergence** of $\vec{F}$.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§5.29 Divergence of a vector field">

For $\vec{F} = F_1\hat{i} + F_2\hat{j} + F_3\hat{k}$,

$$\operatorname{div}\vec{F} = \nabla\cdot\vec{F} = \left(\hat{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x} + \hat{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial y} + \hat{k}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\right)\cdot(F_1\hat{i} + F_2\hat{j} + F_3\hat{k}) = \frac{\partial F_1}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial F_3}{\partial z}.$$

Observe that $\operatorname{div}\vec{F}$ is a **scalar** function.

</RuleBox>

### 5.30 Physical Interpretation of Divergence

Consider fluid flowing with velocity field $\vec{V} = V_x\hat{i} + V_y\hat{j} + V_z\hat{k}$ through an infinitesimal rectangular parallelepiped of edges $dx, dy, dz$ parallel to the coordinate axes. Assuming constant unit density:

- Fluid entering through face $ABCD$ (area $dy\,dz$) per unit time $= V_x\,dy\,dz$.
- Fluid leaving through opposite face $PQRS$ (at $x + dx$) per unit time $= \left(V_x + \dfrac{\partial V_x}{\partial x}\,dx\right)dy\,dz$.
- Net outflow (rate of loss of fluid) along the $x$-direction $= \dfrac{\partial V_x}{\partial x}\,dx\,dy\,dz$.

Adding the identical contributions along the $y$- and $z$-axes and dividing by the element volume $dx\,dy\,dz$ gives the **rate of net outflow of fluid per unit volume**:

$$\frac{\partial V_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial V_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial V_z}{\partial z} = \nabla\cdot\vec{V} = \operatorname{div}\vec{V}.$$

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="§5.30 Solenoidal vector field and equation of continuity">

- **Physical meaning:** $\operatorname{div}\vec{V}$ measures the net outward flux (loss of fluid) per unit volume per unit time at a point.
- **Solenoidal vector field:** A vector field $\vec{V}$ is called **solenoidal** if

$$\operatorname{div}\vec{V} = \nabla\cdot\vec{V} = 0.$$

For an **incompressible** fluid with no sources or sinks, there can be no net gain or loss in the volume element, yielding $\nabla\cdot\vec{V} = 0$, the **equation of continuity** (conservation of mass).

</RuleBox>

### 5.31 Curl

Taking the formal cross product of $\nabla$ with a differentiable vector field $\vec{F}$ yields a new vector field called the **curl** (or **rotation**) of $\vec{F}$.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§5.31 Curl of a vector field">

For $\vec{F} = F_1\hat{i} + F_2\hat{j} + F_3\hat{k}$,

$$\operatorname{curl}\vec{F} = \nabla\times\vec{F} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial}{\partial y} & \dfrac{\partial}{\partial z} \\ F_1 & F_2 & F_3 \end{vmatrix} = \left(\frac{\partial F_3}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial z}\right)\hat{i} - \left(\frac{\partial F_3}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial F_1}{\partial z}\right)\hat{j} + \left(\frac{\partial F_2}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial F_1}{\partial y}\right)\hat{k}.$$

Unlike divergence, $\operatorname{curl}\vec{F}$ is a **vector** quantity.

</RuleBox>

### 5.32 Physical Meaning of Curl

Consider a rigid body rotating with constant angular velocity $\vec{\omega} = \omega_1\hat{i} + \omega_2\hat{j} + \omega_3\hat{k}$. By §5.15, the linear velocity at $\vec{r} = x\hat{i} + y\hat{j} + z\hat{k}$ is

$$\vec{V} = \vec{\omega}\times\vec{r} = (\omega_2 z - \omega_3 y)\hat{i} + (\omega_3 x - \omega_1 z)\hat{j} + (\omega_1 y - \omega_2 x)\hat{k}.$$

Taking the curl of $\vec{V}$ yields

$$\operatorname{curl}\vec{V} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial}{\partial y} & \dfrac{\partial}{\partial z} \\ \omega_2 z - \omega_3 y & \omega_3 x - \omega_1 z & \omega_1 y - \omega_2 x \end{vmatrix} = (\omega_1 + \omega_1)\hat{i} - (-\omega_2 - \omega_2)\hat{j} + (\omega_3 + \omega_3)\hat{k} = 2\vec{\omega}.$$

Thus the curl of a velocity field equals **twice the local angular velocity**, directly capturing the rotational tendency of the field.

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="§5.32 Irrotational (conservative) fields, scalar potential & vector identities">

1. **Physical meaning of curl:** For rigid-body rotation $\vec{V} = \vec{\omega}\times\vec{r}$, $\operatorname{curl}\vec{V} = 2\vec{\omega}$.
2. **Irrotational / conservative field:** A vector field $\vec{F}$ is **irrotational** (or **conservative** for a force field) if

$$\operatorname{curl}\vec{F} = \nabla\times\vec{F} = \vec{0}.$$

3. **Scalar potential $\phi$:** Every irrotational field on a simply connected domain can be written as $\vec{F} = \nabla\phi$. To find $\phi$, integrate the exact total differential:

$$d\phi = \nabla\phi\cdot d\vec{r} = \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r} = F_1\,dx + F_2\,dy + F_3\,dz.$$

4. **Fundamental second-order and product identities:**
   - $\operatorname{curl}(\operatorname{grad}\phi) = \nabla\times(\nabla\phi) = \vec{0}$ (every gradient field is irrotational).
   - $\operatorname{div}(\operatorname{curl}\vec{V}) = \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec{V}) = 0$ (every curl field is solenoidal).
   - $\operatorname{div}(\phi\vec{V}) = \phi(\operatorname{div}\vec{V}) + (\operatorname{grad}\phi)\cdot\vec{V}$.
   - $\operatorname{curl}(\phi\vec{V}) = (\operatorname{grad}\phi)\times\vec{V} + \phi(\operatorname{curl}\vec{V})$.
   - $\operatorname{div}(\vec{U}\times\vec{V}) = \vec{V}\cdot\operatorname{curl}\vec{U} - \vec{U}\cdot\operatorname{curl}\vec{V}$.
   - $\operatorname{curl}(\operatorname{curl}\vec{F}) = \operatorname{grad}(\operatorname{div}\vec{F}) - \nabla^2\vec{F}$.

</RuleBox>

## Common Exam Pitfalls

<Callout variant="warning" title="Frequent mistakes in GET 209 vector calculus exams">

- **Forgetting to normalize the direction vector:** In directional derivatives $D_{\hat{d}}\phi = \nabla\phi\cdot\hat{d}$, you **must** divide the given direction vector $\vec{d}$ by $|\vec{d}|$ before taking the dot product. Dotting with an unnormalized $\vec{d}$ multiplies the answer by $|\vec{d}|$.
- **Middle-term minus sign in determinants:** Both $\vec{a}\times\vec{b}$ and $\nabla\times\vec{F}$ have a **minus** sign on the $\hat{j}$-cofactor: $-\left(\dfrac{\partial F_3}{\partial x} - \dfrac{\partial F_1}{\partial z}\right)\hat{j} = \left(\dfrac{\partial F_1}{\partial z} - \dfrac{\partial F_3}{\partial x}\right)\hat{j}$.
- **Confusing solenoidal and irrotational:** **Solenoidal** means $\nabla\cdot\vec{F} = 0$ (scalar condition, zero net outflow); **irrotational / conservative** means $\nabla\times\vec{F} = \vec{0}$ (three component equations, zero rotation, scalar potential $\phi$ exists).
- **Reversing vector order in cross-product differentiation:** In $\dfrac{d}{dt}(\vec{F}\times\vec{G}) = \vec{F}\times\dfrac{d\vec{G}}{dt} + \dfrac{d\vec{F}}{dt}\times\vec{G}$, never write $\vec{G}\times\dfrac{d\vec{F}}{dt}$ without flipping the sign.
- **Omitting the chain-rule factor for $r$:** When differentiating functions of $r = \sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}$, always use $\dfrac{\partial r}{\partial x} = \dfrac{x}{r}$, $\dfrac{\partial r}{\partial y} = \dfrac{y}{r}$, $\dfrac{\partial r}{\partial z} = \dfrac{z}{r}$.

</Callout>

## Summary of Formulas

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="Chapter 5 (§5.1–§5.32) — Master Formula Sheet">

- **Components, magnitude & unit vector (§5.3–§5.4):** $\vec{r} = x\hat{i} + y\hat{j} + z\hat{k}$, $|\vec{r}| = \sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}$, $\hat{r} = \dfrac{\vec{r}}{|\vec{r}|}$.
- **Position & ratio formulae (§5.5–§5.6):** $\overrightarrow{AB} = \vec{b} - \vec{a}$; $\overrightarrow{OC} = \dfrac{m\vec{b} + n\vec{a}}{m + n}$.
- **Dot & cross products (§5.8–§5.15):** $\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b} = |\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\cos\theta = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + a_3 b_3$; $W = \vec{F}\cdot\overrightarrow{AB}$; $|\vec{a}\times\vec{b}| = |\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\sin\theta$ (area of parallelogram); $\vec{M} = \vec{r}\times\vec{F}$; $\vec{V} = \vec{\omega}\times\vec{r}$.
- **Triple & quadruple products (§5.16–§5.21):**
  - $[\vec{a}\;\vec{b}\;\vec{c}] = \vec{a}\cdot(\vec{b}\times\vec{c}) = V_{\text{parallelepiped}}$; $V_{\text{tetrahedron}} = \frac{1}{6}|[\vec{a}\;\vec{b}\;\vec{c}]|$; coplanar $\iff [\vec{a}\;\vec{b}\;\vec{c}] = 0$.
  - $\vec{a}\times(\vec{b}\times\vec{c}) = (\vec{a}\cdot\vec{c})\vec{b} - (\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b})\vec{c}$.
  - $(\vec{a}\times\vec{b})\cdot(\vec{c}\times\vec{d}) = (\vec{a}\cdot\vec{c})(\vec{b}\cdot\vec{d}) - (\vec{a}\cdot\vec{d})(\vec{b}\cdot\vec{c})$.
  - $(\vec{a}\times\vec{b})\times(\vec{c}\times\vec{d}) = [\vec{a}\;\vec{b}\;\vec{d}]\vec{c} - [\vec{a}\;\vec{b}\;\vec{c}]\vec{d}$.
- **Vector differentiation (§5.22–§5.24):** $\vec{v} = \dfrac{d\vec{r}}{dt}$, $\vec{a} = \dfrac{d^2\vec{r}}{dt^2}$, $\hat{T} = \dfrac{\vec{v}}{|\vec{v}|}$, $a_t = \vec{a}\cdot\hat{T}$.
- **Gradient, normal & directional derivative (§5.25–§5.28):** $\nabla\phi = \hat{i}\dfrac{\partial\phi}{\partial x} + \hat{j}\dfrac{\partial\phi}{\partial y} + \hat{k}\dfrac{\partial\phi}{\partial z}$; $\hat{N} = \dfrac{\nabla\phi}{|\nabla\phi|}$; $D_{\hat{d}}\phi = \nabla\phi\cdot\hat{d}$; max rate $= |\nabla\phi|$; $\cos\theta = \dfrac{\nabla\phi_1\cdot\nabla\phi_2}{|\nabla\phi_1||\nabla\phi_2|}$.
- **Divergence & curl (§5.29–§5.32):**
  - $\operatorname{div}\vec{F} = \nabla\cdot\vec{F} = \dfrac{\partial F_1}{\partial x} + \dfrac{\partial F_2}{\partial y} + \dfrac{\partial F_3}{\partial z}$; solenoidal $\iff \nabla\cdot\vec{F} = 0$.
  - $\operatorname{curl}\vec{F} = \nabla\times\vec{F}$; $\operatorname{curl}(\vec{\omega}\times\vec{r}) = 2\vec{\omega}$; irrotational $\iff \nabla\times\vec{F} = \vec{0} \iff \vec{F} = \nabla\phi$.
  - Radial results: $\nabla r = \dfrac{\vec{r}}{r}$, $\nabla(r^n) = n r^{n-2}\vec{r}$, $\nabla\cdot(r^n\vec{r}) = (n+3)r^n$, $\nabla^2(r^n) = n(n+1)r^{n-2}$, $\nabla^2 f(r) = f''(r) + \dfrac{2}{r}f'(r)$.
  - Key identities: $\nabla\times(\nabla\phi) = \vec{0}$, $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec{V}) = 0$, $\nabla\cdot(\vec{U}\times\vec{V}) = \vec{V}\cdot(\nabla\times\vec{U}) - \vec{U}\cdot(\nabla\times\vec{V})$, $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec{F}) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec{F}) - \nabla^2\vec{F}$.

</RuleBox>
