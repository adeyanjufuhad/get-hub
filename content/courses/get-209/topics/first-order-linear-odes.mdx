## 1. Overview

Engineering systems change over time—capacitors charge through resistors, hot billets cool in air, and chemical concentrations shift inside mixing tanks. Every one of those models leads directly to a **first-order linear ordinary differential equation (ODE)**.

Once you master the **Integrating Factor** recipe below, almost every GET 209 first-order ODE question turns into a predictable 4-step mechanical routine.

---

## 2. Key Concepts

### Standard Form

Always normalize the coefficient of $\frac{dy}{dx}$ to **$1$** first:

$$\frac{dy}{dx} + P(x)\,y = Q(x)$$

### The Integrating Factor Method

Multiply both sides by the **integrating factor**:

$$\mu(x) = e^{\int P(x)\,dx}$$

Why does this work? Because by the product rule, the entire left-hand side collapses cleanly into a single derivative:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[\mu(x)\,y\bigr] = \mu(x)\,Q(x)$$

Now integrate both sides with respect to $x$ (never forget the arbitrary constant $+C$ before dividing by $\mu(x)$!):

$$y(x) = \frac{1}{\mu(x)}\left[\int \mu(x)\,Q(x)\,dx + C\right]$$

### Quick Check: Separable vs. Linear

- If you can factor the equation as $g(y)\,dy = f(x)\,dx$, **separate variables** directly—it is even faster.
- If $y$ and $dy/dx$ appear only to the first power (no $y^2$, $\sin y$, or $y\cdot y'$), use the **integrating factor**.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Never skip Step 1 (Normalizing to dy/dx)">
If the equation starts as $x\frac{dy}{dx} + 2y = 4x^2$, do **NOT** set $P(x) = 2$! Divide through by $x$ first to get $\frac{dy}{dx} + \frac{2}{x}y = 4x$, so $P(x) = \frac{2}{x}$ and $\mu(x) = e^{2\ln x} = x^2$.
</Callout>

## 4. Common Exam Pitfalls

<Callout variant="warning" title="Where most marks get lost">
1. **Adding $+C$ *after* dividing by $\mu(x)$:** Wrong! $C$ MUST be divided by $\mu(x)$ too (e.g., $Ce^{-2x}$, NOT $+C$).
2. **Forgetting the sign of $P(x)$:** In $\frac{dy}{dx} - 3y = 4$, $P(x) = -3$, so $\mu(x) = e^{-3x}$, not $e^{3x}$.
</Callout>
