## Overview

Determinants turn linear elimination problems into compact scalar invariants. Whenever an engineer balances forces in a truss, solves mesh currents in a circuit, or tests whether three coordinate points lie on one straight line, a square array of coefficients governs whether a unique solution exists. This folio covers the complete determinant framework of Chapter 4 (§4.1–4.17): elementary expansions, minors and cofactors, Sarrus' rule, algebraic properties, factor theorem, pivotal condensation, special symmetry classes, Laplace expansion, coordinate geometry applications, Cramer's rule, determinant multiplication, and consistency criteria.

### 4.1 Introduction

Solving coupled linear equations sits at the core of engineering analysis. Rather than repeatedly isolating variables by hand, determinants encode the elimination condition in a single structured scalar built from the equation coefficients.

### 4.2 Determinant

Start with two homogeneous linear equations in a single unknown $x$:

$$a_1 x + b_1 = 0, \qquad a_2 x + b_2 = 0.$$

Solving the first gives $x = -\dfrac{b_1}{a_1}$ (for $a_1 \neq 0$). Substituting into the second yields the compatibility condition (eliminant):

$$a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1 = 0.$$

Suppressing $x$ and enclosing the two rows $(a_1, b_1)$ and $(a_2, b_2)$ between vertical bars produces a **second-order determinant**. Each entry $a_1, b_1, a_2, b_2$ is called an **element** (or constituent).

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§4.2 Second-order determinant">

$$\Delta = \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & b_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 \end{vmatrix} = a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1$$

Multiply along the main diagonal (top-left to bottom-right) with a $+$ sign, then subtract the product along the secondary diagonal (top-right to bottom-left).

</RuleBox>

### 4.3 Determinant as Eliminant

Now take three homogeneous linear equations in $x, y, z$:

$$a_1 x + b_1 y + c_1 z = 0, \qquad a_2 x + b_2 y + c_2 z = 0, \qquad a_3 x + b_3 y + c_3 z = 0.$$

Applying cross-multiplication to the second and third equations gives

$$\frac{x}{b_2 c_3 - b_3 c_2} = \frac{y}{a_3 c_2 - a_2 c_3} = \frac{z}{a_2 b_3 - a_3 b_2} = k.$$

Substituting $x, y, z$ in terms of $k$ into the first equation and dividing out $k \neq 0$ produces the eliminant:

$$a_1(b_2 c_3 - b_3 c_2) - b_1(a_2 c_3 - a_3 c_2) + c_1(a_2 b_3 - a_3 b_2) = 0.$$

Writing the coefficient array between vertical bars defines the **third-order determinant** and its standard first-row expansion.

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§4.3 Expansion of a third-order determinant">

$$\begin{vmatrix} a_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 & c_2 \\ a_3 & b_3 & c_3 \end{vmatrix} = a_1 \begin{vmatrix} b_2 & c_2 \\ b_3 & c_3 \end{vmatrix} - b_1 \begin{vmatrix} a_2 & c_2 \\ a_3 & c_3 \end{vmatrix} + c_1 \begin{vmatrix} a_2 & b_2 \\ a_3 & b_3 \end{vmatrix}$$

</RuleBox>

### 4.4 Minor

Every element in an $n \times n$ determinant sits at the intersection of one row and one column. Removing both that row and that column leaves a sub-determinant of order $n - 1$.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§4.4 Minor of an element">

The **minor** $M_{rc}$ of the element in row $r$ and column $c$ is the determinant formed by deleting the $r$th row and the $c$th column.

For the first row of a $3 \times 3$ determinant:
$$M_{11} = \begin{vmatrix} b_2 & c_2 \\ b_3 & c_3 \end{vmatrix}, \qquad M_{12} = \begin{vmatrix} a_2 & c_2 \\ a_3 & c_3 \end{vmatrix}, \qquad M_{13} = \begin{vmatrix} a_2 & b_2 \\ a_3 & b_3 \end{vmatrix}.$$

</RuleBox>

### 4.5 Cofactor

Attaching a position-dependent sign to a minor turns the alternating signs of expansion into a uniform summation rule.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§4.5 Cofactor and expansion along any row or column">

The **cofactor** $A_{rc}$ of the element in row $r$ and column $c$ is:

$$A_{rc} = (-1)^{r+c} M_{rc}$$

A determinant equals the sum of the products of the elements of **any single row or column** with their corresponding cofactors:

$$\Delta = \sum_{c=1}^{n} a_{rc} A_{rc} = \sum_{r=1}^{n} a_{rc} A_{rc}.$$

</RuleBox>

### 4.6 Rules of Sarrus (For Third Order Determinants Only)

For quick hand evaluation of a $3 \times 3$ determinant without forming $2 \times 2$ minors, Sarrus' mnemonic repeats the first two columns to the right of the third column and sums diagonal triple products.

<RuleBox kind="working-rule" title="§4.6 Rule of Sarrus (order 3 only)">

Write columns $C_1$ and $C_2$ again to the right of $C_3$:
1. Multiply along the **three downward-right diagonals** and attach $+$ signs: $a_1 b_2 c_3 + b_1 c_2 a_3 + c_1 a_2 b_3$.
2. Multiply along the **three downward-left diagonals** and attach $-$ signs: $- c_1 b_2 a_3 - a_1 c_2 b_3 - b_1 a_2 c_3$.
3. Add all six terms to obtain $\Delta$.

**Restriction:** Sarrus' rule works **exclusively** for $3 \times 3$ determinants; applying it to $4 \times 4$ or larger arrays omits most permutation terms and produces wrong answers.

</RuleBox>

### 4.7 Properties of Determinants

Direct cofactor expansion becomes tedious for symbolic or large numerical determinants. Six algebraic properties let us create zeros, extract factors, and evaluate determinants cleanly without heavy arithmetic.

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="§4.7 Six fundamental properties of determinants">

Let $\Delta$ be an $n$th-order determinant.

- **Property (i) — Transpose invariance:** Converting all rows into columns (and columns into rows) leaves the determinant unchanged: $|\Delta^T| = \Delta$.
- **Property (ii) — Row or column swap:** Interchanging any two rows (or any two columns) multiplies the determinant by $-1$: $\Delta' = -\Delta$.
- **Property (iii) — Identical rows or columns:** If two rows (or two columns) are identical (or proportional), then $\Delta = 0$.
- **Property (iv) — Scalar multiplication of one line:** Multiplying every element of a **single** row (or column) by a constant $k$ multiplies the determinant by $k$. Conversely, a common factor from one row or column may be factored outside the determinant.
- **Property (v) — Row/column replacement:** Adding any constant multiple of one row (or column) to another row (or column) leaves the determinant unchanged: e.g., $R_i \to R_i + k R_j$ ($i \neq j$) keeps $\Delta' = \Delta$.
- **Property (vi) — Additivity along one line:** If every entry in one row (or column) is an algebraic sum of $m$ terms, the determinant splits into the sum of $m$ determinants sharing all remaining rows (or columns):

$$\begin{vmatrix} a_1 + p_1 + q_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 + p_2 + q_2 & b_2 & c_2 \\ a_3 + p_3 + q_3 & b_3 & c_3 \end{vmatrix} = \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 & c_2 \\ a_3 & b_3 & c_3 \end{vmatrix} + \begin{vmatrix} p_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ p_2 & b_2 & c_2 \\ p_3 & b_3 & c_3 \end{vmatrix} + \begin{vmatrix} q_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ q_2 & b_2 & c_2 \\ q_3 & b_3 & c_3 \end{vmatrix}.$$

</RuleBox>

### 4.8 Factor Theorem

When entries of a determinant are polynomials in variables such as $x, y, z$, the expanded determinant is itself a polynomial. Property (iii) combines directly with the algebraic factor theorem to factor symmetric determinants almost effortlessly.

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="§4.8 Factor theorem for determinants">

If the entries of $\Delta(x)$ are polynomials in $x$ and substituting $x = a$ causes two rows (or two columns) to become identical—so $\Delta(a) = 0$—then $(x - a)$ is a factor of $\Delta(x)$.

**Working method:**
1. Identify all linear factors by testing pairwise equalities (e.g., $x = y$, $y = z$, $z = x$).
2. Compare the total degree of the product of those factors against the degree of the leading-diagonal product of $\Delta$.
3. Include the most general symmetric polynomial factor of the remaining degree (just a numerical constant $k$ if the degrees already match).
4. Determine unknown constants by matching the coefficient of the leading-diagonal term on both sides.

</RuleBox>

### 4.9 Pivotal Condensation Method

Chio's pivotal condensation algorithm systematically shrinks an $n$th-order determinant to order $n - 1$ using the top-left element $a_1 \neq 0$ as a **pivot**. Subtracting $\frac{b_1}{a_1}, \frac{c_1}{a_1}, \dots$ multiples of $C_1$ from $C_2, C_3, \dots$ clears the first row except for $a_1$; factoring $\frac{1}{a_1}$ from each of the remaining $n - 1$ rows yields an $(n-1)$th-order determinant of $2 \times 2$ blocks.

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§4.9 Pivotal condensation formula">

For an $n$th-order determinant with nonzero leading element $a_1$:

$$D = \frac{1}{(a_1)^{n-2}} \begin{vmatrix} \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & b_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 \end{vmatrix} & \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 & c_2 \end{vmatrix} & \cdots \\ \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & b_1 \\ a_3 & b_3 \end{vmatrix} & \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & c_1 \\ a_3 & c_3 \end{vmatrix} & \cdots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots \\ \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & b_1 \\ a_n & b_n \end{vmatrix} & \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & c_1 \\ a_n & c_n \end{vmatrix} & \cdots \end{vmatrix}$$

Repeated condensation reduces any order down to $2 \times 2$. If $a_1 = 0$, swap two columns (or rows) first to bring a nonzero element into the pivot position, remembering to multiply by $-1$.

</RuleBox>

### 4.10 Conjugate Elements

In any square determinant, the elements $a_{11}, a_{22}, \dots, a_{nn}$ form the **leading (principal) diagonal**.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§4.10 Conjugate elements">

Two elements that lie symmetrically opposite each other across the leading diagonal—i.e., $a_{ij}$ and $a_{ji}$ ($i \neq j$)—are called **conjugate elements**.

In $\begin{vmatrix} a_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 & c_2 \\ a_3 & b_3 & c_3 \end{vmatrix}$, the conjugate pairs are $(a_2, b_1)$, $(a_3, c_1)$, and $(b_3, c_2)$.

</RuleBox>

### 4.11 Special Types of Determinants

Symmetry relations among diagonal and conjugate elements yield powerful shortcuts.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§4.11 Orthosymmetric and skew-symmetric determinants">

1. **Orthosymmetric determinant:** All leading-diagonal entries are identical and every pair of conjugate elements is equal ($a_{ii} = a$, $a_{ij} = a_{ji}$):
$$\begin{vmatrix} a & h & g \\ h & a & f \\ g & f & a \end{vmatrix}.$$
2. **Skew-symmetric determinant:** Every leading-diagonal element is zero ($a_{ii} = 0$) and every off-diagonal element equals the negative of its conjugate ($a_{ij} = -a_{ji}$):
$$\begin{vmatrix} 0 & -a & -b \\ a & 0 & -c \\ b & c & 0 \end{vmatrix}.$$

**Two key properties of skew-symmetric determinants:**
- **Property 1:** Every skew-symmetric determinant of **odd order** vanishes ($\Delta = 0$).
- **Property 2:** Every skew-symmetric determinant of **even order** is a **perfect square** of a polynomial in its entries.

</RuleBox>

### 4.12 Laplace Method for the Expansion of a Determinant in Terms of First Two Rows

Single-row cofactor expansion generalizes to expanding along a **pair of rows** simultaneously—especially effective for block-structured $4 \times 4$ determinants containing blocks of zeros.

<RuleBox kind="working-rule" title="§4.12 Laplace expansion by the first two rows">

For a $4 \times 4$ determinant:
1. Form all $\binom{4}{2} = 6$ second-order minors from Rows 1 and 2 by choosing every pair of columns $(j, k)$ with $j < k$.
2. Multiply each $2 \times 2$ minor by its **complementary** $2 \times 2$ minor formed by deleting Rows 1 & 2 and Columns $j$ & $k$.
3. Attach the sign $(-1)^{(1+2)+(j+k)} = (-1)^{j+k+3}$—equivalently, count the number of adjacent column swaps needed to shift Columns $j$ and $k$ into the first two positions: even swaps give $+$, odd swaps give $-$.
4. Sum all six signed products.

</RuleBox>

### 4.13 Application of Determinants

Determinants give compact coordinate formulas in planar geometry for both the area enclosed by three vertices and the test for collinearity.

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§4.13 Area of a triangle and collinearity condition">

For three points $A(x_1, y_1)$, $B(x_2, y_2)$, and $C(x_3, y_3)$ in the $xy$-plane:

$$\text{Area of } \triangle ABC = \frac{1}{2} \left|\, \begin{vmatrix} x_1 & y_1 & 1 \\ x_2 & y_2 & 1 \\ x_3 & y_3 & 1 \end{vmatrix} \,\right|$$

Always take the **absolute value** because geometric area is non-negative.

**Collinearity criterion:** Three points $A, B, C$ are collinear if and only if the triangle area is zero:

$$\begin{vmatrix} x_1 & y_1 & 1 \\ x_2 & y_2 & 1 \\ x_3 & y_3 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = 0.$$

</RuleBox>

### 4.14 Solution of Simultaneous Linear Equations by Determinants (Cramer's Rule)

Consider a system of three linear equations in three unknowns $x, y, z$:

$$a_1 x + b_1 y + c_1 z = d_1, \qquad a_2 x + b_2 y + c_2 z = d_2, \qquad a_3 x + b_3 y + c_3 z = d_3.$$

Let $D$ denote the coefficient determinant. Multiplying Column 1 of $D$ by $x$ (Property iv) gives $x D$. Now perform $C_1 \to C_1 + y C_2 + z C_3$ (Property v); the entries of Column 1 become $a_i x + b_i y + c_i z = d_i$, which yields $x D = D_1$. Repeating the same idea on Columns 2 and 3 yields **Cramer's Rule**.

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§4.14 Cramer's Rule">

Define the coefficient determinant $D$ and the three column-replaced determinants $D_1, D_2, D_3$:

$$D = \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 & c_2 \\ a_3 & b_3 & c_3 \end{vmatrix}, \quad D_1 = \begin{vmatrix} d_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ d_2 & b_2 & c_2 \\ d_3 & b_3 & c_3 \end{vmatrix}, \quad D_2 = \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & d_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 & d_2 & c_2 \\ a_3 & d_3 & c_3 \end{vmatrix}, \quad D_3 = \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & b_1 & d_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 & d_2 \\ a_3 & b_3 & d_3 \end{vmatrix}.$$

Whenever $D \neq 0$, the unique solution is:

$$x = \frac{D_1}{D}, \qquad y = \frac{D_2}{D}, \qquad z = \frac{D_3}{D}.$$

The same rule holds for any $n \times n$ linear system by replacing the $j$th column of $D$ with the right-hand-side vector to form $D_j$.

</RuleBox>

### 4.15 Rule for Multiplication of Two Determinants

Two determinants of the **same order** can be multiplied directly into a single determinant of that order. Because transposing a determinant preserves its value (Property i), row-by-row multiplication and row-by-column multiplication yield equal determinant values. Dass standardizes **row-by-row** multiplication.

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§4.15 Row-by-row multiplication of two third-order determinants">

$$\begin{vmatrix} a_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 & c_2 \\ a_3 & b_3 & c_3 \end{vmatrix} \times \begin{vmatrix} \alpha_1 & \beta_1 & \gamma_1 \\ \alpha_2 & \beta_2 & \gamma_2 \\ \alpha_3 & \beta_3 & \gamma_3 \end{vmatrix} = \begin{vmatrix} a_1\alpha_1 + b_1\beta_1 + c_1\gamma_1 & a_1\alpha_2 + b_1\beta_2 + c_1\gamma_2 & a_1\alpha_3 + b_1\beta_3 + c_1\gamma_3 \\ a_2\alpha_1 + b_2\beta_1 + c_2\gamma_1 & a_2\alpha_2 + b_2\beta_2 + c_2\gamma_2 & a_2\alpha_3 + b_2\beta_3 + c_2\gamma_3 \\ a_3\alpha_1 + b_3\beta_1 + c_3\gamma_1 & a_3\alpha_2 + b_3\beta_2 + c_3\gamma_2 & a_3\alpha_3 + b_3\beta_3 + c_3\gamma_3 \end{vmatrix}$$

Entry $(i, j)$ of the product is the dot product of Row $i$ of $\Delta_1$ with Row $j$ of $\Delta_2$.

</RuleBox>

### 4.16 Condition for Consistency of a System of Simultaneous Homogeneous Equations

A **homogeneous** linear system has all right-hand constants equal to zero:

$$a_{11} x + a_{12} y + a_{13} z = 0, \qquad a_{21} x + a_{22} y + a_{23} z = 0, \qquad a_{31} x + a_{32} y + a_{33} z = 0.$$

Because $(x, y, z) = (0, 0, 0)$ always satisfies every equation, a homogeneous system is **never inconsistent**. The coefficient determinant $D$ decides whether nontrivial (non-zero) solutions exist.

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="§4.16 Consistency of homogeneous systems">

Let $D = \det(a_{ij})$ be the coefficient determinant of a $3 \times 3$ homogeneous system:

1. **If $D \neq 0$:** The system is consistent with the **unique trivial solution** $x = 0, y = 0, z = 0$.
2. **If $D = 0$:** The system is consistent with **infinitely many (nontrivial) solutions**.

</RuleBox>

### 4.17 For a System of Three Simultaneous Linear Equations with Three Unknowns

For a general **non-homogeneous** system $a_i x + b_i y + c_i z = d_i$ ($i = 1, 2, 3$), Cramer's determinants $D, D_1, D_2, D_3$ classify consistency completely.

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="§4.17 Consistency test for three non-homogeneous linear equations">

1. **$D \neq 0$:** Consistent with a **unique solution** given by $x = \dfrac{D_1}{D}$, $y = \dfrac{D_2}{D}$, $z = \dfrac{D_3}{D}$.
2. **$D = 0$ and $D_1 = D_2 = D_3 = 0$:** Consistent with **infinitely many solutions** (provided the planes are not parallel distinct planes—always verify by back-substitution when $D = 0$).
3. **$D = 0$ and at least one of $D_1, D_2, D_3 \neq 0$:** **Inconsistent** (no solution exists).

</RuleBox>

## Common Exam Pitfalls

<Callout variant="warning" title="High-frequency determinant errors to avoid">

- **Applying Sarrus' rule to $4 \times 4$ determinants:** Sarrus' diagonal trick is valid *only* for $3 \times 3$ arrays. For order $n \ge 4$, use row/column operations, pivotal condensation (§4.9), or Laplace expansion (§4.12).
- **Factoring a scalar out of the whole determinant instead of one line:** Unlike matrices, $\det(kA) = k^n \det(A)$ for an $n \times n$ determinant because $k$ factors out of *each* of the $n$ rows separately (Property iv).
- **Forgetting the minus sign on row/column swaps or cofactor signs:** Every single row or column exchange flips the sign of $\Delta$ (Property ii), and any entry at $(r, c)$ with $r + c$ odd carries a minus sign in $A_{rc} = (-1)^{r+c}M_{rc}$.
- **Missing zero placeholders in Cramer's rule:** If a variable does not appear in an equation (as in Example 66), insert $0$ in the corresponding column before forming $D, D_1, D_2, D_3$.
- **Omitting absolute value in triangle area:** A negative determinant value simply reflects clockwise vertex orientation; geometric area is always $\frac{1}{2}|\det|$.

</Callout>

## Summary of Formulas

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="Chapter 4 (§4.1–4.17) Essential Formula Sheet">

- **2nd-order determinant:** $\begin{vmatrix} a_1 & b_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 \end{vmatrix} = a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1$.
- **Cofactor:** $A_{rc} = (-1)^{r+c} M_{rc}$; expansion $\Delta = \sum_c a_{rc} A_{rc} = \sum_r a_{rc} A_{rc}$.
- **Sarrus ($3\times 3$ only):** $(a_1b_2c_3 + b_1c_2a_3 + c_1a_2b_3) - (c_1b_2a_3 + a_1c_2b_3 + b_1a_2c_3)$.
- **Pivotal condensation ($a_1 \neq 0$):** $D = \dfrac{1}{(a_1)^{n-2}} \det\big(a_1 a_{ij} - a_{i1} a_{1j}\big)_{i,j \ge 2}$.
- **Skew-symmetric parity:** Odd order $\implies \Delta = 0$; even order $\implies \Delta$ is a perfect square.
- **Triangle area & collinearity:** $\text{Area} = \dfrac{1}{2}\left|\det\begin{pmatrix} x_1 & y_1 & 1 \\ x_2 & y_2 & 1 \\ x_3 & y_3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\right|$; collinear $\iff \det = 0$.
- **Cramer's Rule ($D \neq 0$):** $x = \dfrac{D_1}{D},\; y = \dfrac{D_2}{D},\; z = \dfrac{D_3}{D}$.
- **Homogeneous consistency:** $D \neq 0 \implies$ trivial solution only; $D = 0 \implies$ infinitely many nontrivial solutions.
- **Non-homogeneous consistency:** $D \neq 0 \implies$ unique solution; $D = 0$ with $D_1=D_2=D_3=0 \implies$ infinitely many solutions; $D = 0$ with some $D_j \neq 0 \implies$ inconsistent.

</RuleBox>
