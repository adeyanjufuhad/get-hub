## Overview

In earlier topics, contacting surfaces were idealized as either perfectly *frictionless* (transmitting only normal forces) or infinitely *rough* (preventing all relative tangential slip). This topic replaces that idealization with the physical laws of **dry (Coulomb) friction**, where tangential resisting forces develop up to a finite limit proportional to the normal reaction. After establishing the fundamental laws and angles of friction, we apply them to **wedges**, **square-threaded screws**, **journal and thrust bearings**, **rolling resistance**, and **flat and V-belt drives**.

---

### 8.1 Introduction

Whenever two surfaces are in contact and one tends to move relative to the other, tangential **friction forces** develop along the contact surface to oppose that relative motion. These forces are bounded in magnitude and will fail to prevent motion if sufficiently large external forces are applied.

Friction is classified into two broad categories:

- **Dry friction (Coulomb friction):** Develops between the nonlubricated surfaces of rigid bodies in contact. This is the focus of this topic.
- **Fluid friction:** Develops between layers of fluid moving at different velocities (important in pipe flow, immersed bodies, and fully lubricated mechanisms; studied in fluid mechanics).

---

### 8.2 The Laws of Dry Friction. Coefficients of Friction

Consider a block of weight $W$ resting on a horizontal plane surface under a horizontal force $P$). As $P$ increases from zero, the static-friction force $\mathbf{F}$ increases equally to maintain equilibrium until it reaches a **maximum static-friction force** $F_m$. Once $P$ exceeds $F_m$, the block breaks away and slides, and the friction force drops abruptly to a lower, nearly constant **kinetic-friction force** $F_k$ because surface irregularities have less interpenetration during motion.

<DiagramFigure
  src="/diagrams/get-207/dry-friction-and-inclines-fig-8-1.png"
  caption="Variation of friction force F with applied horizontal load P, showing equilibrium up to Fm and sliding at constant Fk."
/>

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="Equations (8.1)–(8.2) — Laws of Dry Friction">

The maximum static-friction force $F_m$ and the kinetic-friction force $F_k$ are proportional to the normal component $N$ of the surface reaction and are independent of the apparent area of contact:

$$
F_m = \mu_s N \qquad (8.1)
$$

$$
F_k = \mu_k N \qquad (8.2)
$$

where $\mu_s$ is the dimensionless **coefficient of static friction** and $\mu_k$ is the dimensionless **coefficient of kinetic friction** (typically about $25\%$ smaller than $\mu_s$).

</RuleBox>

Because $\mu_s$ and $\mu_k$ depend strongly on surface material and condition (seldom known to better than $5\%$), standard mechanics tabulate representative static values in **Table 8.1**:

| Contacting Surfaces | $\mu_s$ (Approximate) |
|---|---|
| Metal on metal | $0.15\text{–}0.60$ |
| Metal on wood | $0.20\text{–}0.60$ |
| Metal on stone | $0.30\text{–}0.70$ |
| Metal on leather | $0.30\text{–}0.60$ |
| Wood on wood | $0.25\text{–}0.50$ |
| Wood on leather | $0.25\text{–}0.50$ |
| Stone on stone | $0.40\text{–}0.70$ |
| Earth on earth | $0.20\text{–}1.00$ |
| Rubber on concrete | $0.60\text{–}0.90$ |

Four distinct physical regimes can occur for a rigid body in contact with a surface):

1. **No friction ($P_x = 0$):** No tendency to slide; $F = 0$.
2. **No motion ($P_x < F_m$):** Equilibrium holds; $F$ is determined strictly from $\sum F_x = 0$. **Do not use $F_m = \mu_s N$**, because $F$ has not reached its ceiling.
3. **Motion impending ($P_x = F_m$):** The body is on the verge of sliding. Both equilibrium equations and $F_m = \mu_s N$ apply simultaneously, with $F_m$ directed opposite to the impending motion.
4. **Motion ($P_x > F_m$):** Equilibrium no longer holds; the body slides and friction equals $F_k = \mu_k N$, directed opposite to the actual motion.

<DiagramFigure
  src="/diagrams/get-207/dry-friction-and-inclines-fig-8-2.png"
  caption="Four regimes of dry friction: (a) no friction, (b) static equilibrium with F < μsN, (c) impending motion with Fm = μsN, (d) kinetic sliding with Fk = μkN."
/>

<Callout variant="info" title="Sliding vs. Tipping Footnote">

As $F$ increases from $0$ to $F_m$, the point of application $A$ of the resultant normal force $\mathbf{N}$ shifts toward the leading edge $B$ so that the couple formed by $\mathbf{W}$ and $\mathbf{N}$ balances the couple formed by $\mathbf{P}$ and $\mathbf{F}$. If $\mathbf{N}$ reaches the corner $B$ before $F$ reaches $F_m = \mu_s N$, the block will **tip about $B$** before it can start sliding.

</Callout>

### 8.3 Angles of Friction

It is often advantageous—especially in three-force problems—to combine the normal force $\mathbf{N}$ and friction force $\mathbf{F}$ into a single **surface reaction** $\mathbf{R}$, inclined at an angle $\phi$ to the normal).

<DiagramFigure
  src="/diagrams/get-207/dry-friction-and-inclines-fig-8-3.png"
  caption="Combining N and F into the resultant reaction R inclined at angle φ to the normal."
/>

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="Equations (8.3)–(8.4) — Angles of Static and Kinetic Friction & Angle of Repose">

When motion is impending, $\phi$ reaches its maximum value $\phi_s$, the **angle of static friction**; when sliding occurs, $\phi$ drops to $\phi_k$, the **angle of kinetic friction**:

$$
\tan \phi_s = \frac{F_m}{N} = \frac{\mu_s N}{N} \implies \tan \phi_s = \mu_s \qquad (8.3)
$$

$$
\tan \phi_k = \frac{F_k}{N} = \frac{\mu_k N}{N} \implies \tan \phi_k = \mu_k \qquad (8.4)
$$

For a block resting solely under its weight $\mathbf{W}$ and surface reaction $\mathbf{R}$ on a board inclined at angle $\theta$), motion becomes impending when $\theta = \phi_s$. This limiting inclination is called the **angle of repose**, and is equal to $\phi_s$.

</RuleBox>

  src="/diagrams/get-207/dry-friction-and-inclines-fig-8-4.png"

### 8.4 Problems Involving Dry Friction

Depending on the geometry and number of forces, dry-friction equilibrium problems use either component equations ($\sum F_x = 0, \sum F_y = 0, \sum M = 0$) with $N$ and $F$ shown separately, or a **force triangle** with the single resultant $\mathbf{R}$ when only three forces act on the body.

<RuleBox kind="working-rule" title="standard mechanics Three Problem Categories & Relative-Motion Rule">

1. **All applied forces and $\mu_s, \mu_k$ known (equilibrium check):** Do *not* assume $F = \mu_s N$. Solve equilibrium equations for the required $F$ and $N$, then compare $|F|$ with $F_m = \mu_s N$. If $|F| \le F_m$, the body remains at rest; if $|F| > F_m$, motion occurs and $F_{\text{actual}} = F_k = \mu_k N$.
2. **All applied forces known, motion impending (find $\mu_s$):** Solve equilibrium equations for $F$ and $N$, set $F = F_m = \mu_s N$, and compute $\mu_s = F/N$.
3. **Motion impending and $\mu_s$ known (find a force, angle, or distance):** Determine the sense of impending motion, draw $F_m = \mu_s N$ opposing it (eliminating one unknown), and solve the equilibrium equations (or force triangle).

**Two Bodies in Contact:** By Newton's third law, the friction forces on bodies $A$ and $B$ are equal and opposite. The friction force acting on $A$ is directed **opposite to the motion (or impending motion) of $A$ as observed from $B$**.

</RuleBox>

### 8.5 Wedges

**Wedges** are simple machines used to lift or position heavy loads using a relatively small driving force $P$. Because of dry friction along its faces, a properly angled wedge remains self-locked in position after insertion.

Consider raising block $A$ (weight $W$) against vertical wall $B$ by driving wedge $C$ to the right). Separate free-body diagrams are drawn for block $A$ and wedge $C$, taking care to obey Newton's third law ($-\mathbf{N}_2$ and $-\mathbf{F}_2$ on wedge $C$) and to orient every friction force opposite to the relative impending motion. Because each free body is typically acted on by only three resultant forces ($\mathbf{W}$ or $\mathbf{P}$ plus two surface reactions $\mathbf{R}_i$ tilted by $\phi_s$), the **force-triangle method (law of sines)** is faster and less error-prone than solving four simultaneous component equations.

### 8.6 Square-Threaded Screws

Square-threaded screws are widely used in jacks, vises, clamps, and presses). Unwrapping one full turn of the base thread yields an inclined plane of horizontal base $2\pi r$ (where $r$ is the **mean radius** of the thread) and vertical rise equal to the **lead** $L$—the axial distance the screw advances in one revolution).

<DiagramFigure
  src="/diagrams/get-207/dry-friction-and-inclines-fig-8-8.png"
  caption="Square-threaded jack of mean thread radius r carrying load W via force P at lever arm a."
/>

<DiagramFigure
  src="/diagrams/get-207/dry-friction-and-inclines-fig-8-9.png"
  caption="Equivalent block-and-incline model of a screw: (a) raising load, (b) lowering self-locking screw (φs > θ), (c) holding non-self-locking screw (φs < θ)."
/>

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="Square-Threaded Screw Formulas & Self-Locking Condition">

1. **Lead vs. Pitch:** The **pitch** $p$ is the axial distance between two consecutive threads. The **lead** $L$ is the advance per revolution: $L = p$ for a single-threaded screw, $L = 2p$ for a double-threaded screw, and $L = np$ for an $n$-threaded screw.
2. **Lead angle $\theta$ and equivalent horizontal force $Q$:**

$$
\tan \theta = \frac{L}{2\pi r}, \qquad Q = \frac{Pa}{r} = \frac{M}{r}
$$

3. **Couple $M = Qr$ for impending motion** (with $\tan\phi_s = \mu_s$):
   - **To raise / tighten load $W$:**
     $$
     M = Wr \tan(\phi_s + \theta)
     $$
   - **Self-locking condition ($\phi_s > \theta$):** The screw holds the load when torque is removed. Couple required to **lower / loosen** load $W$:
     $$
     M = Wr \tan(\phi_s - \theta)
     $$
   - **Unwinding / overhauling ($\phi_s < \theta$):** The screw unwinds on its own; couple required to **hold** the load in equilibrium:
     $$
     M = Wr \tan(\theta - \phi_s)
     $$

</RuleBox>

### 8.7 Journal Bearings. Axle Friction

**Journal bearings** provide lateral support to rotating shafts and axles. When unlubricated or partially lubricated, the axle "climbs" inside the bearing until contact occurs along a line $B$) where the bearing reaction $\mathbf{R}$ makes the friction angle $\phi_k$ (or $\phi_s$ if impending) with the normal. Thus $\mathbf{R}$ does not pass through the axle center $O$, but is tangent to a **circle of friction** of radius $r_f$ centered at $O$.

<DiagramFigure
  src="/diagrams/get-207/dry-friction-and-inclines-fig-8-10.png"
  caption="Journal bearing contact and circle of friction of radius rf = r sin φk ≈ rμk."
/>

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="Equations (8.5)–(8.7) — Journal Bearing Friction & Circle of Friction">

For an axle of radius $r$ carrying radial load $R$, the moment $M$ required to overcome bearing friction and the radius $r_f$ of the **circle of friction** are:

$$
M = R r \sin \phi_k \approx R r \mu_k \qquad (8.5, 8.6)
$$

$$
r_f = r \sin \phi_k \approx r \mu_k \qquad (8.7)
$$

For **impending** rotation, replace $\phi_k, \mu_k$ with $\phi_s, \mu_s$ so $r_f = r \sin\phi_s \approx r \mu_s$. The line of action of $\mathbf{R}$ is always tangent to the circle of friction on the side that creates a moment opposing the rotation (or impending rotation) of the shaft relative to the bearing.

</RuleBox>

### 8.8 Thrust Bearings. Disk Friction

**Thrust bearings** provide *axial* support to rotating shafts via either an **end bearing** (full circular or hollow end face) or a **collar bearing** (annular ring area)). The same analysis applies to **disk clutches**.

<DiagramFigure
  src="/diagrams/get-207/dry-friction-and-inclines-fig-8-11.png"
  caption="Axial thrust bearings: (a) end bearing and (b) collar bearing."
/>

<DiagramFigure
  src="/diagrams/get-207/dry-friction-and-inclines-fig-8-12.png"
  caption="Integration of elemental friction moments ΔM = r ΔF over an annular contact zone from R1 to R2."
/>

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="Equations (8.8)–(8.9) — Disk & Thrust-Bearing Friction (Uniform Pressure)">

Assuming uniform normal pressure $p = P / [\pi(R_2^2 - R_1^2)]$ over an annular contact zone of inner radius $R_1$ and outer radius $R_2$ under axial load $P$, integrating $dM = r\,\mu_k\,p\,dA$ yields:

$$
M = \frac{2}{3}\mu_k P \frac{R_2^3 - R_1^3}{R_2^2 - R_1^2} \qquad (8.8)
$$

For a **solid circular area** ($R_1 = 0, R_2 = R$), Eq. (8.8) reduces to:

$$
M = \frac{2}{3}\mu_k P R \qquad (8.9)
$$

For impending slip (e.g., maximum torque transmitted by a disk clutch or starting torque of a thrust bearing), replace $\mu_k$ with $\mu_s$.

</RuleBox>

### 8.9 Wheel Friction. Rolling Resistance

A wheel rolling at constant speed experiences two distinct sources of resistance: (1) axle/bearing friction paired with rim traction), and (2) **rolling resistance**, caused by slight deformation of the wheel and ground so contact occurs over a finite patch whose resultant reaction $\mathbf{R}$ acts at a point $B$ slightly ahead of the wheel center $O$ by horizontal distance $b$).

<DiagramFigure
  src="/diagrams/get-207/dry-friction-and-inclines-fig-8-13.png"
  caption="(a) Effect of axle friction, (b) idealized free wheel, and (c) rolling resistance with reaction R offset ahead by distance b."
/>

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="Equation (8.10) — Coefficient of Rolling Resistance">

Summing moments about the leading contact point $B$ ($\sum M_B = 0$) for a wheel of radius $r$ and load $W$ pushed horizontally at its axle by $P$:

$$
P r = W b \implies P = \frac{W b}{r} \qquad (8.10)
$$

Unlike $\mu_s$ and $\mu_k$, the **coefficient of rolling resistance $b$** is *not* dimensionless—it is a **length** (typically $0.01\text{ in.}$ or $0.25\text{ mm}$ for steel on steel rail, up to $5.0\text{ in.}$ or $125\text{ mm}$ on soft ground).

</RuleBox>

### 8.10 Belt Friction

For a flat belt, rope, or band brake wrapped over a cylindrical drum through contact angle $\beta$), let the belt be on the verge of slipping toward the $T_2$ side. Writing equilibrium for an infinitesimal element $PP'$ subtending $\Delta\theta$) gives Eqs. (8.11)–(8.12):

$$
\Delta T \cos\frac{\Delta\theta}{2} - \mu_s(2T + \Delta T)\sin\frac{\Delta\theta}{2} = 0 \implies \frac{dT}{d\theta} = \mu_s T
$$

Integrating from $\theta = 0$ ($T = T_1$) to $\theta = \beta$ ($T = T_2$) produces the exponential **belt-friction equations**.

<DiagramFigure
  src="/diagrams/get-207/dry-friction-and-inclines-fig-8-14.png"
  caption="(a) Flat belt on cylindrical drum with angle of wrap β, and (b) free-body diagram of infinitesimal belt element PP'."
/>

<DiagramFigure
  src="/diagrams/get-207/dry-friction-and-inclines-fig-8-15.png"
  caption="V-belt drive of groove angle α: wedging doubles the normal reaction components to 2 ΔN sin(α/2)."
/>

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="Equations (8.13)–(8.16) — Flat-Belt and V-Belt Friction">

For **flat belts, ropes on bollards, and band brakes** at impending slip (with $T_2 > T_1$ and $\beta$ strictly in **radians**):

$$
\ln \frac{T_2}{T_1} = \mu_s \beta \qquad (8.13)
$$

$$
\frac{T_2}{T_1} = e^{\mu_s \beta} \qquad (8.14)
$$

For a **V-belt** running in a sheave of groove angle $\alpha$), friction acts on both flanks ($2\Delta F$) while the $y$-normal sum is $2\Delta N\sin(\alpha/2)$, yielding:

$$
\ln \frac{T_2}{T_1} = \frac{\mu_s \beta}{\sin(\alpha/2)} \qquad (8.15)
$$

$$
\frac{T_2}{T_1} = e^{\mu_s \beta / \sin(\alpha/2)} \qquad (8.16)
$$

**Crucial rules:** (1) $T_2$ is always the *larger* tension (on the side toward which the belt tends to slip *relative to the drum*); (2) $\beta$ must be in **radians** ($n$ full turns $\implies \beta = 2\pi n$); (3) if the belt is actively slipping, replace $\mu_s$ with $\mu_k$; (4) if the belt is neither slipping nor about to slip, these equations do *not* apply.

</RuleBox>

## Common Exam Pitfalls

<Callout variant="warning" title="Five Classic Friction Traps">

1. **Assuming $F = \mu_s N$ when motion is NOT known to be impending:** In Category 1 problems, $F$ and $N$ are independent unknowns solved from equilibrium; only *after* computing $F_{\text{req}}$ do you test $|F_{\text{req}}| \le \mu_s N$. If $|F_{\text{req}}| > \mu_s N$, the body slides and friction drops to $F_k = \mu_k N$.
2. **Ignoring tipping vs. slipping:** Always verify that the normal reaction acts within the physical base of the body (or that the rear support reaction $N_A \ge 0$). On a tall crate or cabinet, tipping often occurs at a lower load $P$ than sliding.
3. **Confusing screw pitch $p$ with lead $L$:** For a double-threaded screw, $L = 2p$; using $p$ instead of $L$ halves $\tan\theta$ and distorts both clamping torque and the self-locking check ($\phi_s > \theta$).
4. **Using degrees instead of radians for belt wrap $\beta$:** In $T_2 / T_1 = e^{\mu_s \beta}$, $\beta$ MUST be in **radians** (e.g., $120^\circ = 2\pi/3\,\text{rad}$, $2\text{ turns} = 4\pi\,\text{rad}$). Also, in a two-pulley drive with equal $\mu_s$, slip always initiates on the pulley with the **smaller wrap angle $\beta$**.
5. **Reversing $T_1$ and $T_2$ in band brakes:** $T_2$ is always on the end toward which the drum tries to drag the belt (the tight side); swapping $T_1$ and $T_2$ flips the brake from non-self-locking to self-locking or miscalculates the braking torque by a factor of $e^{\mu\beta}$.

</Callout>

## Summary of Formulas

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="Master Formula Sheet">

- **Coulomb Dry Friction:** $F_m = \mu_s N$, $\quad F_k = \mu_k N$, $\quad \tan\phi_s = \mu_s$, $\quad \tan\phi_k = \mu_k$
- **Angle of Repose (block on incline under weight only):** $\theta_{\max} = \phi_s = \tan^{-1}\mu_s$
- **Square-Threaded Screw ($\tan\theta = \frac{L}{2\pi r}$, $M = Qr$):**
  - Raise / tighten: $M = Wr\tan(\phi_s + \theta)$
  - Lower (self-locking, $\phi_s > \theta$): $M = Wr\tan(\phi_s - \theta)$
  - Hold (overhauling, $\phi_s < \theta$): $M = Wr\tan(\theta - \phi_s)$
- **Journal Bearing & Circle of Friction:** $M = Rr\sin\phi_k \approx Rr\mu_k$, $\quad r_f = r\sin\phi \approx r\mu$
- **Thrust Bearing / Disk Clutch (uniform pressure):**
  - Annular collar ($R_1, R_2$): $M = \frac{2}{3}\mu P \frac{R_2^3 - R_1^3}{R_2^2 - R_1^2}$
  - Full circular disk ($R_1 = 0$): $M = \frac{2}{3}\mu P R$
- **Rolling Resistance (length $b$):** $P r = W b \implies P = \frac{Wb}{r}$
- **Flat Belt / Rope / Band Brake ($\beta$ in radians):** $\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1} = \mu\beta \iff \frac{T_2}{T_1} = e^{\mu\beta}$
- **V-Belt (groove angle $\alpha$, $\beta$ in radians):** $\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1} = \frac{\mu\beta}{\sin(\alpha/2)} \iff \frac{T_2}{T_1} = e^{\mu\beta/\sin(\alpha/2)}$

</RuleBox>
