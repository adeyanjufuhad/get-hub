## Overview

In particle statics (Chapter 2), every force acting on a body was assumed to pass through a single point. Real engineering members—beams, brackets, shafts, trusses, and hulls—have finite size, so forces act at different points and tend not only to translate the body but also to rotate it. In **Beer, Johnston, Mazurek & Cornwell (*Vector Mechanics for Engineers: Statics and Dynamics*, 10th Edition), Chapter 3**, we treat bodies as **rigid** and develop the vector tools—cross products, moments about points and axes, couples, and equivalent (equipollent) force-couple systems—needed to simplify any system of forces without changing its external mechanical effect.

---

### 3.1 Introduction

A **rigid body** is an idealized assembly of particles whose mutual distances remain fixed under load. Although real structural materials experience small elastic deformations, those deformations ordinarily have negligible influence on overall equilibrium or rigid-body motion and are deferred to mechanics of materials.

Because forces on a rigid body act at distinct points, studying their external effect requires three core concepts:
1. **Moment of a force about a point** (§3.6) and **about an axis** (§3.11), evaluated using vector and scalar products.
2. **Couples** (§3.12)—pairs of equal, parallel, opposite forces producing pure rotation.
3. **Equivalent force-couple systems** (§3.16–3.21), which allow any force system to be replaced by one resultant force at a chosen reference point plus one resultant couple, and in special cases reduced further to a single resultant force or a wrench.

---

### 3.2 External and Internal Forces

Forces acting on a rigid body fall into two distinct categories:

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§3.2 External vs. Internal Forces">

1. **External forces** represent the actions exerted on the rigid body by other bodies (such as gravitational weight $\mathbf{W}$, applied cable pulls $\mathbf{F}$, and ground or support reactions $\mathbf{R}_1, \mathbf{R}_2$). They completely govern the external behavior—whether the body remains at rest or accelerates in **translation**, **rotation**, or both.
2. **Internal forces** are the mutual forces binding the particles of a rigid body together, or holding sub-components together within a composite structure. Because internal forces occur in equal, opposite, collinear pairs inside the isolated body, they do not appear in rigid-body force-system reductions and are analyzed separately in Chapters 6 and 7.

</RuleBox>

---

### 3.3 Principle of Transmissibility. Equivalent Forces

Unlike forces on a single particle (which are **bound vectors** with a fixed point of application), forces acting on a rigid body behave as **sliding vectors** because they may be shifted along their line of action without altering external equilibrium or motion.

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="§3.3 Principle of Transmissibility">

The conditions of equilibrium or motion of a rigid body remain unchanged if a force $\mathbf{F}$ acting at a given point is replaced by a force $\mathbf{F}'$ of the same magnitude and same direction acting at another point, provided both forces share the **same line of action**. Such forces $\mathbf{F}$ and $\mathbf{F}'$ are **equivalent** with respect to the rigid body's external behavior.

</RuleBox>

<Callout variant="info" title="Important limitation on internal effects">

While sliding a force along its line of action leaves external motion and support reactions unaltered, it changes the internal stress distribution. Pushing inward on both ends of a bar puts it in compression, whereas pulling outward at both ends puts it in tension, even though both systems are externally equivalent to zero net force. Therefore, apply transmissibility freely for rigid-body statics and external reactions, never for internal deformation or stress analysis.

</Callout>

### 3.4 Vector Product of Two Vectors

To express rotational tendencies cleanly in three dimensions, we introduce the **vector product** (or **cross product**) of two vectors $\mathbf{P}$ and $\mathbf{Q}$.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§3.4 Vector Product (Cross Product) V = P × Q">

The vector product $\mathbf{V} = \mathbf{P} \times \mathbf{Q}$ is the unique vector satisfying three conditions:

1. **Line of action:** $\mathbf{V}$ is perpendicular to the plane containing $\mathbf{P}$ and $\mathbf{Q}$.
2. **Magnitude:** With $\theta \le 180^\circ$ denoting the angle between $\mathbf{P}$ and $\mathbf{Q}$ when drawn from a common origin,

$$V = PQ \sin\theta \tag{3.1}$$

3. **Direction (Right-Hand Rule):** Curl the fingers of your right hand in the sense of the rotation through $\theta$ that aligns $\mathbf{P}$ with $\mathbf{Q}$; your extended thumb points in the direction of $\mathbf{V}$. Thus $\mathbf{P}, \mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{V}$ (in that order) form a **right-handed triad**.

</RuleBox>

<DiagramFigure
  src="/diagrams/get-207/force-system-resultants-fig-3-6-vector-product.png"
  caption="Beer & Johnston Fig. 3.6 — (a) Cross product V = P × Q perpendicular to the plane of P and Q; (b) right-hand rule."
/>

Geometrically, $|\mathbf{P} \times \mathbf{Q}| = PQ\sin\theta$ equals the area of the parallelogram spanned by $\mathbf{P}$ and $\mathbf{Q}$. Consequently, sliding the tip of $\mathbf{Q}$ parallel to $\mathbf{P}$ leaves the cross product unchanged ($\mathbf{P} \times \mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{P} \times \mathbf{Q}'$), and when $\mathbf{P}$ and $\mathbf{Q}$ are parallel or antiparallel ($\theta = 0^\circ$ or $180^\circ$), $\mathbf{P} \times \mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{0}$.

**Algebraic laws for cross products:**
- **Not commutative:** $\mathbf{Q} \times \mathbf{P} = -(\mathbf{P} \times \mathbf{Q})$ (Eq. 3.4).
- **Distributive:** $\mathbf{P} \times (\mathbf{Q}_1 + \mathbf{Q}_2) = \mathbf{P} \times \mathbf{Q}_1 + \mathbf{P} \times \mathbf{Q}_2$ (Eq. 3.5).
- **Not associative:** $(\mathbf{P} \times \mathbf{Q}) \times \mathbf{S} \neq \mathbf{P} \times (\mathbf{Q} \times \mathbf{S})$ in general (Eq. 3.6).

---

### 3.5 Vector Products Expressed in Terms of Rectangular Components

Because the Cartesian unit vectors $\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k}$ form a right-handed orthogonal triad, their pairwise cross products follow the cyclic rule (counterclockwise around $\mathbf{i} \to \mathbf{j} \to \mathbf{k}$ is positive, clockwise is negative):

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§3.5 Unit-Vector Cross Products & Determinant Form">

$$\mathbf{i} \times \mathbf{i} = \mathbf{0}, \qquad \mathbf{j} \times \mathbf{j} = \mathbf{0}, \qquad \mathbf{k} \times \mathbf{k} = \mathbf{0}$$

$$\mathbf{i} \times \mathbf{j} = \mathbf{k}, \qquad \mathbf{j} \times \mathbf{k} = \mathbf{i}, \qquad \mathbf{k} \times \mathbf{i} = \mathbf{j} \tag{3.7}$$

$$\mathbf{j} \times \mathbf{i} = -\mathbf{k}, \qquad \mathbf{k} \times \mathbf{j} = -\mathbf{i}, \qquad \mathbf{i} \times \mathbf{k} = -\mathbf{j}$$

Expanding $\mathbf{V} = (P_x\mathbf{i} + P_y\mathbf{j} + P_z\mathbf{k}) \times (Q_x\mathbf{i} + Q_y\mathbf{j} + Q_z\mathbf{k})$ by distributivity gives:

$$\mathbf{V} = (P_y Q_z - P_z Q_y)\mathbf{i} + (P_z Q_x - P_x Q_z)\mathbf{j} + (P_x Q_y - P_y Q_x)\mathbf{k} \tag{3.8}$$

Equivalently, in compact $3\times 3$ determinant form:

$$\mathbf{V} = \mathbf{P} \times \mathbf{Q} = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ P_x & P_y & P_z \\ Q_x & Q_y & Q_z \end{vmatrix} \tag{3.10}$$

</RuleBox>

### 3.6 Moment of a Force about a Point

Consider a force $\mathbf{F}$ applied at point $A$, whose position relative to a fixed reference point $O$ is given by the position vector $\mathbf{r} = \overrightarrow{OA}$.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§3.6 Moment of a Force about Point O">

The **moment of $\mathbf{F}$ about $O$** is the vector product:

$$\mathbf{M}_O = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F} \tag{3.11}$$

Its magnitude is

$$M_O = r F \sin\theta = F d \tag{3.12}$$

where $d = r\sin\theta$ is the **perpendicular distance** (moment arm) from $O$ to the line of action of $\mathbf{F}$. By the right-hand rule, $\mathbf{M}_O$ is perpendicular to the plane of $\mathbf{r}$ and $\mathbf{F}$ and points along the axis about which $\mathbf{F}$ tends to rotate the body (counterclockwise as seen from the tip of $\mathbf{M}_O$). Units are $\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$ in SI and $\text{lb}\cdot\text{ft}$ or $\text{lb}\cdot\text{in.}$ in U.S. customary units.

</RuleBox>

<DiagramFigure
  src="/diagrams/get-207/force-system-resultants-fig-3-12-moment-point.png"
  caption="Beer & Johnston Fig. 3.12 — Moment M_O = r × F of a force about point O and its sense by the right-hand rule."
/>

Restating the principle of transmissibility in terms of moments: two forces $\mathbf{F}$ and $\mathbf{F}'$ are equivalent on a rigid body if and only if they have the same magnitude, same direction, and equal moments about a reference point $O$:

$$\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{F}' \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbf{M}_O = \mathbf{M}'_O \tag{3.13}$$

**Two-Dimensional Problems:** When all forces lie in the $xy$ plane (the plane of the page), $\mathbf{M}_O$ is always perpendicular to the plane. A counterclockwise tendency ($\circlearrowleft$, pointing out of the page along $+z$) is taken as positive ($M_O = +Fd$), while a clockwise tendency ($\circlearrowright$, pointing into the page along $-z$) is negative ($M_O = -Fd$).

---

### 3.7 Varignon's Theorem

Because cross products distribute over vector addition, the moment of the sum of concurrent forces equals the sum of their individual moments.

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="§3.7 Varignon's Theorem">

If concurrent forces $\mathbf{F}_1, \mathbf{F}_2, \dots$ act at the same point $A$ (with position vector $\mathbf{r}$ from $O$), then:

$$\mathbf{r} \times (\mathbf{F}_1 + \mathbf{F}_2 + \cdots) = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}_1 + \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}_2 + \cdots \tag{3.14}$$

That is, the moment about a point $O$ of the resultant of several concurrent forces equals the sum of the moments of the individual forces about $O$. In practice, we resolve a single force $\mathbf{F}$ into convenient components (rectangular or along/perpendicular to $\mathbf{r}$) and sum their moments algebraically or vectorially.

</RuleBox>

### 3.8 Rectangular Components of the Moment of a Force

Resolving $\mathbf{r} = x\mathbf{i} + y\mathbf{j} + z\mathbf{k}$ and $\mathbf{F} = F_x\mathbf{i} + F_y\mathbf{j} + F_z\mathbf{k}$ gives the rectangular components of $\mathbf{M}_O = M_x\mathbf{i} + M_y\mathbf{j} + M_z\mathbf{k}$:

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§3.8 Rectangular Components & Moment about Arbitrary Point B">

About the origin $O$:

$$M_x = y F_z - z F_y, \qquad M_y = z F_x - x F_z, \qquad M_z = x F_y - y F_x \tag{3.18}$$

$$\mathbf{M}_O = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ x & y & z \\ F_x & F_y & F_z \end{vmatrix} \tag{3.19}$$

About an arbitrary point $B(x_B, y_B, z_B)$, replace $\mathbf{r}$ with the relative position vector $\mathbf{r}_{A/B} = \mathbf{r}_A - \mathbf{r}_B$ drawn **from $B$ to $A$**:

$$\mathbf{M}_B = \mathbf{r}_{A/B} \times \mathbf{F} = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ x_A - x_B & y_A - y_B & z_A - z_B \\ F_x & F_y & F_z \end{vmatrix} \tag{3.20,\;3.21}$$

In 2D ($z_{A/B} = 0$, $F_z = 0$), $\mathbf{M}_B = M_B\mathbf{k}$ reduces to the scalar:

$$M_B = (x_A - x_B)F_y - (y_A - y_B)F_x \tag{3.23}$$

</RuleBox>

### 3.9 Scalar Product of Two Vectors

To project a force or moment onto a specified axis and to determine angles between spatial lines, we use the **scalar product** (or **dot product**).

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§3.9 Scalar Product (Dot Product) P · Q">

The scalar product of $\mathbf{P}$ and $\mathbf{Q}$ forming an angle $\theta$ is the scalar:

$$\mathbf{P} \cdot \mathbf{Q} = P Q \cos\theta \tag{3.24}$$

It is **commutative** ($\mathbf{P} \cdot \mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{Q} \cdot \mathbf{P}$) and **distributive** ($\mathbf{P} \cdot (\mathbf{Q}_1 + \mathbf{Q}_2) = \mathbf{P} \cdot \mathbf{Q}_1 + \mathbf{P} \cdot \mathbf{Q}_2$). Because $\mathbf{i} \cdot \mathbf{i} = \mathbf{j} \cdot \mathbf{j} = \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{k} = 1$ and $\mathbf{i} \cdot \mathbf{j} = \mathbf{j} \cdot \mathbf{k} = \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{i} = 0$, the component formula is:

$$\mathbf{P} \cdot \mathbf{Q} = P_x Q_x + P_y Q_y + P_z Q_z \tag{3.30}$$

</RuleBox>

Two direct engineering applications follow immediately:
1. **Angle $\theta$ between two vectors:**
   $$\cos\theta = \frac{P_x Q_x + P_y Q_y + P_z Q_z}{P Q} \tag{3.32}$$
2. **Scalar projection $P_{OL}$ of $\mathbf{P}$ onto a directed axis $OL$ (unit vector $\boldsymbol{\lambda} = \cos\theta_x\mathbf{i} + \cos\theta_y\mathbf{j} + \cos\theta_z\mathbf{k}$):**
   $$P_{OL} = P \cos\theta = \mathbf{P} \cdot \boldsymbol{\lambda} = P_x \cos\theta_x + P_y \cos\theta_y + P_z \cos\theta_z \tag{3.36,\;3.37}$$

### 3.10 Mixed Triple Product of Three Vectors

Combining a dot product and a cross product yields the **mixed triple product** (scalar triple product) $\mathbf{S} \cdot (\mathbf{P} \times \mathbf{Q})$.

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§3.10 Mixed Triple Product & Determinant">

$$\mathbf{S} \cdot (\mathbf{P} \times \mathbf{Q}) = \begin{vmatrix} S_x & S_y & S_z \\ P_x & P_y & P_z \\ Q_x & Q_y & Q_z \end{vmatrix} \tag{3.41}$$

- **Geometric meaning:** $|\mathbf{S} \cdot (\mathbf{P} \times \mathbf{Q})|$ equals the **volume of the parallelepiped** with coterminous edges $\mathbf{S}, \mathbf{P}, \mathbf{Q}$. It is positive if $(\mathbf{S}, \mathbf{P}, \mathbf{Q})$ form a right-handed triad, negative if left-handed, and **zero if and only if the three vectors are coplanar**.
- **Circular permutation invariance:**
  $$\mathbf{S} \cdot (\mathbf{P} \times \mathbf{Q}) = \mathbf{P} \cdot (\mathbf{Q} \times \mathbf{S}) = \mathbf{Q} \cdot (\mathbf{S} \times \mathbf{P}) = -\mathbf{S} \cdot (\mathbf{Q} \times \mathbf{P}) \tag{3.39}$$

</RuleBox>

### 3.11 Moment of a Force about a Given Axis

Suppose a force $\mathbf{F}$ acts at point $A$ on a rigid body constrained to rotate about a fixed axis $OL$ with unit vector $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§3.11 Moment of F about Axis OL or Axis BL">

The **moment $M_{OL}$ of $\mathbf{F}$ about axis $OL$** (passing through $O$) is the orthogonal projection of $\mathbf{M}_O = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}$ onto $OL$:

$$M_{OL} = \boldsymbol{\lambda} \cdot \mathbf{M}_O = \boldsymbol{\lambda} \cdot (\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}) = \begin{vmatrix} \lambda_x & \lambda_y & \lambda_z \\ x & y & z \\ F_x & F_y & F_z \end{vmatrix} \tag{3.42,\;3.43}$$

More generally, for an arbitrary axis $BL$ **not** passing through the origin, choose any point $B$ on the axis and draw $\mathbf{r}_{A/B} = \mathbf{r}_A - \mathbf{r}_B$ from $B$ to any point $A$ on the line of action of $\mathbf{F}$:

$$M_{BL} = \boldsymbol{\lambda} \cdot \mathbf{M}_B = \boldsymbol{\lambda} \cdot (\mathbf{r}_{A/B} \times \mathbf{F}) = \begin{vmatrix} \lambda_x & \lambda_y & \lambda_z \\ x_{A/B} & y_{A/B} & z_{A/B} \\ F_x & F_y & F_z \end{vmatrix} \tag{3.45,\;3.46}$$

The result is completely independent of which point $B$ on the axis and which point $A$ on the line of action of $\mathbf{F}$ are selected.

</RuleBox>

  src="/diagrams/get-207/force-system-resultants-fig-3-27-moment-axis.png"
  caption="Beer & Johnston Fig. 3.27 — Moment M_OL of force F about axis OL as the projection OC of M_O onto OL."
/>

Note that choosing $\boldsymbol{\lambda} = \mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k}$ recovers $M_x, M_y, M_z$ (Eq. 3.18), confirming that $M_x, M_y, M_z$ measure the tendency of $\mathbf{F}$ to rotate the body about the $x, y, z$ axes respectively.

### 3.12 Moment of a Couple

Two forces $\mathbf{F}$ and $-\mathbf{F}$ having the **same magnitude**, **parallel lines of action**, and **opposite sense** constitute a **couple**.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§3.12 Couple Moment M = r × F">

Although the resultant force of a couple is zero ($\mathbf{F} + (-\mathbf{F}) = \mathbf{0}$), the sum of the moments of the two forces about any origin $O$ is independent of $O$:

$$\mathbf{r}_A \times \mathbf{F} + \mathbf{r}_B \times (-\mathbf{F}) = (\mathbf{r}_A - \mathbf{r}_B) \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}$$

Thus the **moment of the couple** is:

$$\mathbf{M} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}, \qquad M = r F \sin\theta = F d \tag{3.47,\;3.48}$$

where $\mathbf{r}$ connects any point on the line of action of $-\mathbf{F}$ to any point on the line of action of $\mathbf{F}$, and $d$ is the perpendicular distance between the two lines of action. Because $\mathbf{M}$ does not depend on $O$, **a couple moment is a free vector** that can be applied at any point on the rigid body.

</RuleBox>

### 3.13 Equivalent Couples

Using the parallelogram law and principle of transmissibility, Beer & Johnston prove a fundamental equivalence property:

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="§3.13 Condition for Equivalent Couples">

Two couples—whether acting in the same plane or in parallel planes—are **equivalent** (produce the identical rotational effect on a rigid body) if and only if they have the **same couple moment $\mathbf{M}$**:

$$F_1 d_1 = F_2 d_2 \tag{3.49}$$

with parallel planes of action and the same rotational sense. Neither the individual force magnitudes nor the exact points of application matter—only the moment vector $\mathbf{M}$ matters.

</RuleBox>

---

### 3.14 Addition of Couples

Consider two couples acting in intersecting planes $P_1$ and $P_2$ with couple moments $\mathbf{M}_1$ and $\mathbf{M}_2$. Transforming both couples onto the same segment $\mathbf{r}$ along the intersection of $P_1$ and $P_2$ and adding forces by Varignon's theorem yields a single resultant couple whose moment is:

$$\mathbf{M} = \mathbf{M}_1 + \mathbf{M}_2 \tag{3.50}$$

That is, **couples add vectorially** by summing their moment vectors.

---

### 3.15 Couples Can Be Represented by Vectors

Because two couples with equal moments are equivalent and couples obey vector addition, any couple can be completely represented by a **couple vector** $\mathbf{M}$ (a free vector perpendicular to the plane of the couple, denoted with a curl arrow in Beer & Johnston's diagrams to distinguish it from force vectors). Like any vector, a couple vector $\mathbf{M}$ may be placed at the origin $O$ and resolved into Cartesian components $\mathbf{M}_x, \mathbf{M}_y, \mathbf{M}_z$ representing couples in the $yz, zx,$ and $xy$ planes respectively.

---

### 3.16 Resolution of a Given Force into a Force at $O$ and a Couple

Suppose a force $\mathbf{F}$ acts at point $A$ (position vector $\mathbf{r}$ from $O$) and we wish to shift its point of application to $O$, which is *not* on the line of action of $\mathbf{F}$. Attaching $+\mathbf{F}$ and $-\mathbf{F}$ at $O$ leaves the system unchanged; the $+\mathbf{F}$ at $O$ remains, while the original $\mathbf{F}$ at $A$ and $-\mathbf{F}$ at $O$ form a couple of moment $\mathbf{M}_O = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}$.

<RuleBox kind="working-rule" title="§3.16 Force-Couple Transformation">

1. **Moving a force $\mathbf{F}$ from $A$ to an arbitrary point $O$:** Place $\mathbf{F}$ at $O$ and add a couple vector $\mathbf{M}_O = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}$ (where $\mathbf{r} = \overrightarrow{OA}$). Notice $\mathbf{M}_O$ is always **perpendicular** to $\mathbf{F}$.
2. **Transferring a force-couple system from $O$ to another point $O'$ (with $\mathbf{s} = \overrightarrow{O'O}$):**
   $$\mathbf{M}_{O'} = \mathbf{M}_O + \mathbf{s} \times \mathbf{F} \tag{3.51}$$
3. **Converse reduction:** Any force-couple system $(\mathbf{F}, \mathbf{M}_O)$ in which $\mathbf{F} \perp \mathbf{M}_O$ can be replaced by a **single equivalent force** $\mathbf{F}$ moved in the plane perpendicular to $\mathbf{M}_O$ to a line of action at perpendicular distance $d = M_O / F$ from $O$ such that $\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{M}_O$.

</RuleBox>

### 3.17 Reduction of a System of Forces to One Force and One Couple

Any arbitrary system of forces $\mathbf{F}_1, \mathbf{F}_2, \mathbf{F}_3, \dots$ acting at points $A_1, A_2, A_3, \dots$ (position vectors $\mathbf{r}_1, \mathbf{r}_2, \mathbf{r}_3, \dots$ from a chosen base point $O$) can be moved to $O$ one force at a time by adding the corresponding couples $\mathbf{M}_i = \mathbf{r}_i \times \mathbf{F}_i$. Adding all concurrent forces at $O$ and adding all couple vectors gives the general reduction theorem:

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="§3.17 Equivalent Force-Couple System at Point O">

Any system of forces acting on a rigid body can be reduced to a single resultant force $\mathbf{R}$ acting at $O$ and a single resultant couple vector $\mathbf{M}_O^R$:

$$\mathbf{R} = \sum \mathbf{F}, \qquad \mathbf{M}_O^R = \sum \mathbf{M}_O = \sum (\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}) \tag{3.52}$$

In rectangular components:

$$\mathbf{R} = R_x\mathbf{i} + R_y\mathbf{j} + R_z\mathbf{k}, \qquad \mathbf{M}_O^R = M_x^R\mathbf{i} + M_y^R\mathbf{j} + M_z^R\mathbf{k} \tag{3.56}$$

To transfer the force-couple system from $O$ to another point $O'$ (with $\mathbf{s} = \overrightarrow{O'O}$):

$$\mathbf{M}_{O'}^R = \mathbf{M}_O^R + \mathbf{s} \times \mathbf{R} \tag{3.53}$$

while $\mathbf{R}$ remains unchanged. Note that in general 3D systems, $\mathbf{R}$ and $\mathbf{M}_O^R$ are **not** mutually perpendicular.

</RuleBox>

### 3.18 Equivalent Systems of Forces

Because the external effect of any force system on a rigid body is completely determined by its force-couple system at a given point $O$, two systems of forces are equivalent if and only if they reduce to the same force-couple system at $O$:

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="§3.18 Necessary & Sufficient Conditions for Equivalence">

Two systems of forces $\mathbf{F}_1, \mathbf{F}_2, \dots$ and $\mathbf{F}'_1, \mathbf{F}'_2, \dots$ acting on the same rigid body are **equivalent** if and only if:

$$\sum \mathbf{F} = \sum \mathbf{F}' \quad \text{and} \quad \sum \mathbf{M}_O = \sum \mathbf{M}'_O \tag{3.57}$$

Equivalently, in six scalar equations:

$$\sum F_x = \sum F'_x, \quad \sum F_y = \sum F'_y, \quad \sum F_z = \sum F'_z$$

$$\sum M_x = \sum M'_x, \quad \sum M_y = \sum M'_y, \quad \sum M_z = \sum M'_z \tag{3.58}$$

Establishing $\sum \mathbf{M}_O = \sum \mathbf{M}'_O$ about **one** point $O$ guarantees equality about every other point provided $\sum \mathbf{F} = \sum \mathbf{F}'$.

</RuleBox>

---

### 3.19 Equipollent Systems of Vectors

When two systems of vectors satisfy $\sum \mathbf{F} = \sum \mathbf{F}'$ and $\sum \mathbf{M}_O = \sum \mathbf{M}'_O$ about an arbitrary point $O$, they are said to be **equipollent**. For forces acting on a **rigid body**, *equipollent systems are always equivalent*. For a collection of **independent (non-rigid) particles**, however, two equipollent systems can move individual particles differently and are therefore **not** necessarily equivalent.

---

### 3.20 Further Reduction of a System of Forces

When can the force-couple system $(\mathbf{R}, \mathbf{M}_O^R)$ be simplified even further?
- If $\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{0}$ and $\mathbf{M}_O^R \neq \mathbf{0}$, the system reduces to a **single resultant couple** $\mathbf{M}_O^R$.
- If $\mathbf{R} \neq \mathbf{0}$ and $\mathbf{R} \perp \mathbf{M}_O^R$ (i.e., $\mathbf{R} \cdot \mathbf{M}_O^R = 0$), the system reduces to a **single resultant force** $\mathbf{R}$ placed along a specific line of action satisfying $\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{M}_O^R$.

<RuleBox kind="working-rule" title="§3.20 Three Important Cases Reducible to a Single Force">

1. **Concurrent forces:** All forces meet at one point, so $\mathbf{M}_O^R = \mathbf{0}$ about that point and the system reduces directly to $\mathbf{R} = \sum \mathbf{F}$ at the point of concurrency.
2. **Coplanar forces (in the $xy$ plane):** $\mathbf{R} = R_x\mathbf{i} + R_y\mathbf{j}$ lies in the plane while $\mathbf{M}_O^R = M_z^R\mathbf{k}$ is perpendicular to it ($R_x = \sum F_x,\; R_y = \sum F_y,\; M_z^R = \sum M_O$, Eq. 3.59). The line of action of the single resultant $\mathbf{R}$ satisfies:
   $$x R_y - y R_x = M_O^R$$
   giving $x$-intercept $x = M_O^R / R_y$ and $y$-intercept $y = -M_O^R / R_x$ (perpendicular distance $d = M_O^R / R$ from $O$).
3. **Parallel forces (e.g., parallel to the $y$ axis):** $\mathbf{R} = R_y\mathbf{j}$ where $R_y = \sum F_y$, while $\mathbf{M}_O^R = M_x^R\mathbf{i} + M_z^R\mathbf{k}$ lies in the $zx$ plane (Eq. 3.60). Moving $\mathbf{R}$ to $(x, 0, z)$ so $\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{M}_O^R$ yields:
   $$-z R_y = M_x^R, \qquad x R_y = M_z^R$$

</RuleBox>

---

### *3.21 Reduction of a System of Forces to a Wrench

In the most general 3D case, $\mathbf{R} \neq \mathbf{0}$, $\mathbf{M}_O^R \neq \mathbf{0}$, and $\mathbf{R}$ is **not** perpendicular to $\mathbf{M}_O^R$. Resolve $\mathbf{M}_O^R$ into a component $\mathbf{M}_1$ parallel to $\mathbf{R}$ and a component $\mathbf{M}_2$ perpendicular to $\mathbf{R}$. Then $\mathbf{R}$ and $\mathbf{M}_2$ reduce to a single force $\mathbf{R}$ shifted to a new line of action (the **axis of the wrench**), leaving the collinear pair $(\mathbf{R}, \mathbf{M}_1)$—a push-and-twist combination called a **wrench**.

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§3.21 Wrench Equations (Pitch & Axis)">

$$\mathbf{M}_1 = p \mathbf{R}, \qquad p = \frac{M_1}{R} = \frac{\mathbf{R} \cdot \mathbf{M}_O^R}{R^2} \tag{3.61,\;3.62}$$

where $p$ is the **pitch** of the wrench (with units of length, positive if $\mathbf{M}_1$ has the same sense as $\mathbf{R}$, negative if opposite; $p$ is invariant with respect to the choice of $O$). An arbitrary point $P$ with position vector $\mathbf{r}$ on the **axis of the wrench** satisfies:

$$\mathbf{M}_1 + \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{M}_O^R \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad p\mathbf{R} + \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{M}_O^R \tag{3.63,\;3.64}$$

</RuleBox>

## Common Exam Pitfalls

<Callout variant="warning" title="Five frequent mistakes in Beer & Johnston Ch. 3 exams">

- **Reversing the cross product order:** Always compute $\mathbf{M}_B = \mathbf{r}_{A/B} \times \mathbf{F}$ with $\mathbf{r}_{A/B} = \mathbf{r}_A - \mathbf{r}_B$ directed **from the moment center $B$ to the point of application $A$**. Using $\mathbf{F} \times \mathbf{r}$ or $\mathbf{r}_B - \mathbf{r}_A$ flips every sign!
- **Forgetting to normalize the axis vector in $M_{BL}$:** In $M_{BL} = \boldsymbol{\lambda} \cdot (\mathbf{r}_{A/B} \times \mathbf{F})$, the first row of the determinant must be the **unit** vector $\boldsymbol{\lambda} = \overrightarrow{BL}/BL$, not the raw unnormalized vector $\overrightarrow{BL}$.
- **Confusing couple equivalence with force equivalence:** A couple moment $\mathbf{M}$ is a **free vector** (valid anywhere on the rigid body), whereas a force $\mathbf{F}$ is a **sliding vector** (valid only along its own line of action unless a compensating couple $\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}$ is added).
- **Trying to reduce a 3D system to a single force when $\mathbf{R} \cdot \mathbf{M}_O^R \neq 0$:** A force-couple system reduces to a single resultant force *only* if $\mathbf{R} \perp \mathbf{M}_O^R$ (such as concurrent, coplanar, or parallel systems). Otherwise, it reduces to a **wrench** with non-zero pitch $p = (\mathbf{R}\cdot\mathbf{M}_O^R)/R^2$.
- **Dropping existing couples when moving forces:** When computing $\mathbf{M}_O^R$ for a system that already contains applied couples $\mathbf{C}_i$, include all free couples directly in $\mathbf{M}_O^R = \sum (\mathbf{r}_i \times \mathbf{F}_i) + \sum \mathbf{C}_i$.

</Callout>

## Summary of Formulas

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="Chapter 3 Key Equations (Beer & Johnston 10e)">

- **Vector product:** $\mathbf{V} = \mathbf{P} \times \mathbf{Q}$, $V = PQ\sin\theta$, $\mathbf{Q}\times\mathbf{P} = -(\mathbf{P}\times\mathbf{Q})$
- **Moment about point $O$ or $B$:** $\mathbf{M}_O = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}$, $M_O = Fd$, $\mathbf{M}_B = (\mathbf{r}_A - \mathbf{r}_B) \times \mathbf{F}$
- **Rectangular moment components:** $M_x = yF_z - zF_y$, $M_y = zF_x - xF_z$, $M_z = xF_y - yF_x$
- **Scalar product & projection:** $\mathbf{P}\cdot\mathbf{Q} = PQ\cos\theta = P_xQ_x + P_yQ_y + P_zQ_z$, $P_{OL} = \mathbf{P}\cdot\boldsymbol{\lambda}$
- **Moment about axis $BL$:** $M_{BL} = \boldsymbol{\lambda} \cdot (\mathbf{r}_{A/B} \times \mathbf{F})$ (determinant of $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$, $\mathbf{r}_{A/B}$, $\mathbf{F}$)
- **Couple moment:** $\mathbf{M} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}$, $M = Fd$ (free vector); addition $\mathbf{M} = \sum \mathbf{M}_i$
- **Force-couple reduction at $O$:** $\mathbf{R} = \sum \mathbf{F}$, $\mathbf{M}_O^R = \sum (\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F})$; shift to $O'$: $\mathbf{M}_{O'}^R = \mathbf{M}_O^R + \overrightarrow{O'O} \times \mathbf{R}$
- **Wrench:** $p = \dfrac{\mathbf{R} \cdot \mathbf{M}_O^R}{R^2}$, $\mathbf{M}_1 = p\mathbf{R}$, axis: $p\mathbf{R} + \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{M}_O^R$

</RuleBox>
