## Overview

Kinematics describes motion without asking what causes it. Kinetics links motion to its cause through **Newton's second law**: the resultant of the forces acting on a particle equals the mass of the particle times its acceleration. Newton's first and third laws were used throughout statics; the second law is what turns statics into dynamics.

The chapter has two parts. The first states the second law and the related **linear-momentum** form, reviews the SI and U.S. customary **systems of units**, and shows how to write **equations of motion** in rectangular or in tangential–normal components — or, equivalently, as **dynamic equilibrium** with an inertia vector $-m\mathbf{a}$. The second introduces the **angular momentum** of a particle, the equations of motion in **radial and transverse components**, **motion under a central force** (where angular momentum is conserved), **Newton's law of gravitation**, and their application to **space mechanics** and **Kepler's laws**. The emphasis of this course is on the first part; the second is treated more briefly.

All nine Sample Problems of the chapter are worked below, together with four end-of-section problems solved in full; further problems are listed with the standard answers.

### 12.1 Introduction

Newton's first and third laws of motion were used extensively in statics for bodies at rest. They also hold for bodies in motion. The **second law** relates the resultant force on a particle to its acceleration. In this chapter the second law is used to analyse the motion of particles; the next two chapters derive from it the **work–energy** and **impulse–momentum** methods.

A particle is any body whose motion can be described by that of a single point; when the effect of rotation is not negligible the body must be treated as a rigid body (Chapters 16 and 17).

---

### 12.2 Newton's Second Law of Motion

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="Newton's second law">

*If the resultant force acting on a particle is not zero, the particle will have an acceleration proportional to the magnitude of the resultant and in the direction of this resultant force.*

The constant ratio of force magnitude to acceleration magnitude is the **mass** $m$ of the particle:

$$
\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a} \tag{12.1}
$$

When several forces act on the particle, $\mathbf{F}$ is replaced by their resultant:

$$
\sum \mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a} \tag{12.2}
$$

</RuleBox>

Experiments show that applying different forces $\mathbf{F}_1, \mathbf{F}_2, \mathbf{F}_3, \dots$ to the same particle produces accelerations in the directions of the forces, with $F_1/a_1 = F_2/a_2 = F_3/a_3 = \text{constant}$ — this constant is a characteristic of the particle, its mass.

- The acceleration in Eq. (12.2) must be measured with respect to a **newtonian frame of reference** — a frame that is not accelerating, ideally one attached to the "fixed" stars (in practice, axes with origin at the sun, not rotating with respect to the stars). For most engineering problems, axes attached to the earth may be used; the error is negligible except for problems such as the flight of long-range missiles.
- If the resultant force is zero, $\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0}$: a particle initially at rest stays at rest, and a particle in motion keeps a constant velocity in a straight line — **Newton's first law** is a special case of the second.
- The second law fails at speeds approaching that of light (relativity) and at atomic scales (quantum mechanics) — conditions outside engineering mechanics.

---

### 12.3 Linear Momentum of a Particle. Rate of Change of Linear Momentum

Replacing $\mathbf{a}$ by $d\mathbf{v}/dt$ in (12.2), and since $m$ is constant:

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="Linear momentum">

$$
\sum \mathbf{F} = \frac{d}{dt}(m\mathbf{v}) \tag{12.3}
$$

$$
\mathbf{L} = m\mathbf{v} \tag{12.4}
$$

$$
\sum \mathbf{F} = \dot{\mathbf{L}} \tag{12.5}
$$

The vector $m\mathbf{v}$ is the **linear momentum** of the particle; it has the direction of the velocity. *The resultant of the forces acting on the particle equals the rate of change of its linear momentum* — the form in which Newton originally stated the second law. SI unit: $\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s} = \text{N}\cdot\text{s}$; U.S. unit: $\text{lb}\cdot\text{s}$.

**Principle of conservation of linear momentum:** if the resultant force on a particle is zero, its linear momentum remains constant in both magnitude and direction.

</RuleBox>

Eq. (12.3) as written assumes constant mass; it does not apply to systems that gain or lose mass, such as rockets (Sec. 14.12).

---

### 12.4 Systems of Units

In $F = ma$ the units of force, mass, length and time cannot all be chosen arbitrarily: three may be chosen, and the fourth must be **derived** so that $F = ma$ holds exactly. Such units form a system of **consistent kinetic units**.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="SI and U.S. customary units">

**SI units.** Base units: length (meter, m), mass (kilogram, kg), time (second, s). The unit of force is derived — the **newton** (N), the force that gives a $1\text{-kg}$ mass an acceleration of $1\,\text{m/s}^2$:

$$
1\,\text{N} = (1\,\text{kg})(1\,\text{m/s}^2) = 1\,\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2
$$

The weight of a body is a force: with $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$,

$$
W = mg \tag{12.6}
$$

so a $1\text{-kg}$ mass weighs $9.81\,\text{N}$. Mass is an absolute quantity — the mass of a body is the same on the earth, on the moon or in orbit.

**U.S. customary units.** Base units: length (foot, ft), force (pound, lb), time (second, s). The unit of mass is derived — the **slug**, the mass that receives an acceleration of $1\,\text{ft/s}^2$ under a $1\text{-lb}$ force: $1\,\text{slug} = 1\,\text{lb}\cdot\text{s}^2/\text{ft}$. With $g = 32.2\,\text{ft/s}^2$,

$$
m = \frac{W}{g} \tag{12.7}
$$

Conversions: $1\,\text{ft} = 0.3048\,\text{m}$, $1\,\text{lb} = 4.448\,\text{N}$, $1\,\text{slug} = 14.59\,\text{kg}$.

</RuleBox>

### 12.5 Equations of Motion

For a particle of mass $m$ acted upon by several forces, the vector equation (12.2) is usually replaced by scalar component equations.

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="Equations of motion">

**Rectangular components:**

$$
\sum F_x = ma_x \qquad \sum F_y = ma_y \qquad \sum F_z = ma_z \tag{12.8}
$$

$$
\sum F_x = m\ddot x \qquad \sum F_y = m\ddot y \qquad \sum F_z = m\ddot z \tag{12.8'}
$$

**Tangential and normal components** (plane curvilinear motion):

$$
\sum F_t = ma_t \qquad \sum F_n = ma_n \tag{12.9}
$$

$$
\sum F_t = m\frac{dv}{dt} \qquad \sum F_n = m\frac{v^2}{\rho} \tag{12.9'}
$$

</RuleBox>

**Motion of a projectile.** If air resistance is neglected, the only force is the weight $\mathbf{W} = -W\mathbf{j}$, so $m\ddot x = 0$, $m\ddot y = -W$, $m\ddot z = 0$, i.e. $\ddot x = 0$, $\ddot y = -g$, $\ddot z = 0$ — the same equations used in projectile kinematics.

<RuleBox kind="working-rule" title="Solution procedure">

1. **Kinematics first** (if needed): relate the accelerations of the bodies — e.g. a cable constraint ($a_B = \tfrac12 a_A$) or a relative-motion equation ($\mathbf{a}_B = \mathbf{a}_A + \mathbf{a}_{B/A}$).
2. **Draw the free-body diagram** of each body (all applied forces, weights, normal and friction forces, tensions) **and an equivalent diagram** showing the vector $m\mathbf{a}$ or its components.
3. **Write the equations of motion** for each body separately, choosing rectangular or tangential–normal components. Use the **absolute** acceleration (newtonian frame). If the sense of $\mathbf{a}$ is unknown, assume one and let the sign decide.
4. With **dry friction**, $F = \mu_k N$ applies only when sliding occurs; $F \le \mu_s N$ when it does not. If the motion is not specified, assume a motion and check it.

</RuleBox>

### 12.6 Dynamic Equilibrium

Transposing the right-hand side of (12.2):

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="d'Alembert's principle — dynamic equilibrium">

$$
\sum \mathbf{F} - m\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0} \tag{12.10}
$$

If we add to the applied forces the vector $-m\mathbf{a}$ — of magnitude $ma$ and direction **opposite** to the acceleration — the particle may be considered in **equilibrium** under the given forces and this **inertia vector**. The particle is then in **dynamic equilibrium**, and the methods of statics apply:

$$
\sum F_x = 0 \qquad \sum F_y = 0 \qquad \text{including inertia vector} \tag{12.11}
$$

In tangential and normal components, the inertia vector is resolved into $-m\,\dfrac{dv}{dt}$ (opposite to the tangential acceleration) and $-m\,\dfrac{v^2}{\rho}$ directed **away from** the center of curvature. The latter is often called the **centrifugal force**.

</RuleBox>

The inertia vector is not a force: it measures the resistance a particle offers to a change of motion. Using it is a matter of preference — the dynamic-equilibrium and the $\sum\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$ approaches give identical equations. the syllabus prefers the kinetic diagram ($\mathbf{F}$'s equivalent to $m\mathbf{a}$) because it keeps the physics clear. For example, in Sample Problem 12.5 the inertia components $ma_t$ and $ma_n$ would be drawn opposite to $\mathbf{a}_t$ and $\mathbf{a}_n$, and $\sum F_t = 0$, $\sum F_n = 0$ would reproduce the same results.

### 12.7 Angular Momentum of a Particle. Rate of Change of Angular Momentum

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="Angular momentum about O">

The **moment about $O$ of the linear momentum** $m\mathbf{v}$ of a particle at position $\mathbf{r}$ is its **angular momentum** about $O$:

$$
\mathbf{H}_O = \mathbf{r} \times m\mathbf{v} \tag{12.12}
$$

$$
H_O = rmv\sin\phi \tag{12.13}
$$

where $\phi$ is the angle between $\mathbf{r}$ and $m\mathbf{v}$. $\mathbf{H}_O$ is perpendicular to the plane of $\mathbf{r}$ and $m\mathbf{v}$. Units: $\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{s}$ (SI) or $\text{ft}\cdot\text{lb}\cdot\text{s}$.

In rectangular components (12.14):

$$
H_x = m(yv_z - zv_y) \qquad H_y = m(zv_x - xv_z) \qquad H_z = m(xv_y - yv_x) \tag{12.15}
$$

For motion in the $xy$ plane, $H_O = H_z = m(xv_y - yv_x)$ (12.16). In radial and transverse components:

$$
H_O = rmv\sin\phi = rmv_\theta \tag{12.17}
$$

$$
H_O = mr^2\dot\theta \tag{12.18}
$$

</RuleBox>

Differentiating (12.12), and noting that $\dot{\mathbf{r}} \times m\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} \times m\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ and $m\mathbf{a} = \sum\mathbf{F}$:

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="Rate of change of angular momentum">

$$
\sum \mathbf{M}_O = \dot{\mathbf{H}}_O \tag{12.19}
$$

*The sum of the moments about $O$ of the forces acting on a particle equals the rate of change of the angular momentum of the particle about $O$.*

</RuleBox>

### 12.8 Equations of Motion in Terms of Radial and Transverse Components

Substituting the components of the acceleration from (11.46) into (12.2):

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="Radial and transverse equations of motion">

$$
\sum F_r = ma_r \qquad \sum F_\theta = ma_\theta \tag{12.20}
$$

$$
\sum F_r = m(\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2) \tag{12.21}
$$

$$
\sum F_\theta = m(r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta) \tag{12.22}
$$

Eq. (12.22) can also be derived from (12.19): $r\sum F_\theta = \dfrac{d}{dt}(mr^2\dot\theta) = m(r^2\ddot\theta + 2r\dot r\dot\theta)$, which, divided by $r$, is (12.22).

</RuleBox>

### 12.9 Motion under a Central Force. Conservation of Angular Momentum

When the only force acting on a particle $P$ is directed toward or away from a fixed point $O$, the particle is moving under a **central force**, and $O$ is the **center of force**. The line of action of $\mathbf{F}$ passes through $O$, so $\sum\mathbf{M}_O = \mathbf{0}$ at every instant, and by (12.19) $\dot{\mathbf{H}}_O = \mathbf{0}$:

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="Conservation of angular momentum under a central force">

$$
\mathbf{H}_O = \text{constant} \tag{12.23}
$$

$$
\mathbf{r} \times m\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{H}_O = \text{constant} \tag{12.24}
$$

The particle therefore moves in a fixed plane perpendicular to $\mathbf{H}_O$, and

$$
rmv\sin\phi = r_0mv_0\sin\phi_0 \tag{12.25}
$$

$$
mr^2\dot\theta = H_O = \text{constant} \tag{12.26}
$$

$$
r^2\dot\theta = h \tag{12.27}
$$

$h$ is the angular momentum per unit mass. The **areal velocity** $\dfrac{dA}{dt} = \tfrac12 r^2\dot\theta = \tfrac12 h$ is constant: the radius vector $OP$ sweeps equal areas in equal times.

</RuleBox>

### 12.10 Newton's Law of Gravitation

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="Newton's law of universal gravitation">

Two particles of masses $M$ and $m$ at a distance $r$ attract each other with equal and opposite forces $\mathbf{F}$ and $-\mathbf{F}$ along the line joining them, of magnitude

$$
F = G\,\frac{Mm}{r^2} \tag{12.28}
$$

with the **constant of gravitation** $G = (66.73 \pm 0.03) \times 10^{-12}\,\text{m}^3/\text{kg}\cdot\text{s}^2$ (approximately $34.4 \times 10^{-9}\,\text{ft}^4/\text{lb}\cdot\text{s}^4$).

At the surface of the earth (radius $R$), the weight of a body is the gravitational attraction:

$$
W = mg = \frac{GM}{R^2}\,m \quad\text{or}\quad g = \frac{GM}{R^2} \tag{12.29}
$$

$$
GM = gR^2 \tag{12.30}
$$

with $R = 6.37 \times 10^6\,\text{m} = 3960\,\text{mi}$ and $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$.

</RuleBox>

The force exerted by the earth on a satellite is directed toward the earth's center — a central force — so the central-force equations apply to satellites, planets and spacecraft.

### 12.11 Trajectory of a Particle under a Central Force

For a particle moving under a central force $\mathbf{F}$ directed toward $O$, Eqs. (12.21) and (12.22) become

$$
m(\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2) = -F \tag{12.31}
$$

$$
m(r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta) = 0 \tag{12.32}
$$

Eq. (12.32) is equivalent to $r^2\dot\theta = h$, i.e. $r^2\,\dfrac{d\theta}{dt} = h$ (12.33). Eliminating $t$ with $\dot\theta = h/r^2$ (12.34) and introducing $u = 1/r$, one obtains $\dot r = -h\,\dfrac{du}{d\theta}$ (12.35) and $\ddot r = -h^2u^2\,\dfrac{d^2u}{d\theta^2}$ (12.36), so that (12.31) becomes **Binet's equation**:

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="Differential equation of the trajectory">

$$
\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2} + u = \frac{F}{mh^2u^2} \tag{12.37}
$$

with $u = 1/r$. Given $F$ as a function of $r$, this determines the path; together with (12.33) it determines the motion. A **positive** $F$ is directed toward $O$ (attraction).

</RuleBox>

### 12.12 Application to Space Mechanics

After its last booster rocket burns out, a satellite moves under the earth's gravitational attraction alone. With $F = GMm/r^2 = GMmu^2$ in (12.37):

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="Orbits under gravitational attraction">

$$
\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2} + u = \frac{GM}{h^2} \tag{12.38}
$$

$$
\frac{1}{r} = \frac{GM}{h^2} + C\cos\theta \tag{12.39}
$$

(the polar axis is chosen through the point of closest approach). The path is a **conic section** with **eccentricity**

$$
\varepsilon = \frac{C}{GM/h^2} = \frac{Ch^2}{GM} \tag{12.40}
$$

$$
\frac{1}{r} = \frac{GM}{h^2}(1 + \varepsilon\cos\theta) \tag{12.39'}
$$

- $\varepsilon > 1$: **hyperbola**; $\varepsilon = 1$: **parabola**; $\varepsilon < 1$: **ellipse** ($\varepsilon = 0$: **circle**).

For launch parallel to the earth's surface at $A$ (distance $r_0$, speed $v_0$):

$$
h = r_0v_0 \tag{12.41}
$$

$$
C = \frac{1}{r_0} - \frac{GM}{h^2} \tag{12.42}
$$

$$
v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r_0}} \quad\text{or}\quad v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2gR^2}{r_0}} \tag{12.43}
$$

$$
v_{\text{circ}} = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r_0}} \quad\text{or}\quad v_{\text{circ}} = \sqrt{\frac{gR^2}{r_0}} \tag{12.44}
$$

The trajectory is hyperbolic if $v_0 > v_{\text{esc}}$, parabolic if $v_0 = v_{\text{esc}}$, elliptic if $v_0 < v_{\text{esc}}$. For $v_{\text{circ}} < v_0 < v_{\text{esc}}$, $A$ is the **perigee** and the opposite point $A'$ the **apogee**; for $v_0 < v_{\text{circ}}$, $A$ is the apogee.

**Periodic time** (area of ellipse $\pi ab$ divided by areal velocity $h/2$), with $r_0$, $r_1$ the perigee and apogee distances:

$$
\tau = \frac{2\pi ab}{h} \tag{12.45}
$$

$$
a = \tfrac12(r_0 + r_1) \tag{12.46}
$$

$$
b = \sqrt{r_0r_1} \tag{12.47}
$$

</RuleBox>

### 12.13 Kepler's Laws of Planetary Motion

The equations derived for an earth satellite also describe the motion of the moon about the earth (approximately, since the moon's mass is not negligible) and of the planets about the sun (with $M$ the mass of the sun, neglecting the forces between planets). Before Newton, Johann Kepler (1571–1630) had deduced from astronomical observations:

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="Kepler's three laws">

1. Each planet describes an **ellipse**, with the sun located at one of its foci.
2. The radius vector drawn from the sun to a planet sweeps **equal areas in equal times**.
3. The **squares of the periodic times** of the planets are proportional to the **cubes of the semimajor axes** of their orbits.

The first law is a particular case of Eq. (12.39); the second expresses the constant areal velocity; the third follows from Eqs. (12.45)–(12.47).

</RuleBox>

## Common Exam Pitfalls

<Callout variant="warning" title="Frequent mistakes in Newton's-second-law problems">

1. **Mixing up mass and weight.** In SI, the data are masses — compute $W = mg$. In U.S. units, the data are weights — compute $m = W/g$ in slugs. Never put a weight where $m$ belongs in $\sum F = ma$.
2. **Assuming the cord tension equals the hanging weight.** A block that accelerates is not in equilibrium: in Sample Problem 12.3, $T_2 = 1680\,\text{N}$, not $2940\,\text{N}$. Write $\sum F = ma$ for each body separately and add the kinematic constraint ($a_B = \tfrac12 a_A$).
3. **Using a relative acceleration in $\sum\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$.** The acceleration must be absolute. For a block on a moving wedge, use $\mathbf{a}_B = \mathbf{a}_A + \mathbf{a}_{B/A}$.
4. **Forgetting that $a_n$ changes the normal force.** On a curved track or at the bottom of a loop, $N \neq mg$; the friction force $\mu_k N$ therefore depends on $v^2/\rho$. Immediately after release from rest ($v = 0$), $a_n = 0$.
5. **Wrong direction for $a_n$ and the centrifugal term.** $a_n = v^2/\rho$ always points toward the center of curvature; on a banked curve that is **horizontal**, not along the incline. The inertia term $mv^2/\rho$ in dynamic equilibrium points **away** from the center.
6. **Radial–transverse slips.** $\sum F_r = m(\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2)$, $\sum F_\theta = m(r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta)$; a frictionless arm exerts only a transverse force, so $\sum F_r = 0$, not $\ddot r = 0$.
7. **Orbits: altitude vs radius.** Always use $r = R + \text{altitude}$ from the earth's **center** in $F = GMm/r^2$, $h = r_0v_0$ and $v_{\text{circ}}$; subtract $R$ only at the end. Use $GM = gR^2$ rather than $G$ and $M$ separately.

</Callout>
