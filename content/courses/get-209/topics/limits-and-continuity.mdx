## 1. Overview

In **James Stewart's *Calculus: Early Transcendentals* (7th Edition, Chapter 2)**, limits capture how a physical quantity behaves *arbitrarily close* to $x = a$, completely independent of whether the formula is even defined *at* $x = a$ itself. Mastering algebraic cancellation, radical conjugates, trigonometric limits, and the three-part continuity test unlocks the rest of GET 209.

---

## 2. Key Concepts

### Limit Laws & Replacement of Identical Functions (Stewart §2.3)

- **Direct Substitution Property:** If $f$ is a polynomial or rational function and $a$ is in the domain of $f$, then $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$.
- **Cancellation Principle:** If $f(x) = g(x)$ whenever $x \neq a$, then $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = \lim_{x \to a} g(x)$, provided the limit exists—even if $f(a)$ is undefined or $g(a)$ sits at a completely different height!

### Continuity & Types of Discontinuities (Stewart §2.5)

A function $f$ is **continuous at $x = a$** if and only if:

$$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$$

which requires simultaneously that (1) $f(a)$ is defined, (2) the two-sided limit $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ exists ($\lim_{x \to a^-} f = \lim_{x \to a^+} f$), and (3) both values match. Rational and trigonometric functions like $\tan x = \frac{\sin x}{\cos x}$ are continuous everywhere on their domains, exhibiting **infinite discontinuities** wherever the denominator vanishes ($\cos x = 0$ at $x = \pm\frac{\pi}{2}, \pm\frac{3\pi}{2}, \dots$).

    "Factor the numerator as a difference of squares: $\\frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1} = \\frac{(x - 1)(x + 1)}{x - 1}$.",
    "Since $x \\to 1$ implies $x \\neq 1$ ($x - 1 \\neq 0$), cancel $(x - 1)$ and substitute: $\\lim_{x \\to 1}(x + 1) = 1 + 1 = 2$."

## 4. Common Exam Pitfalls

<Callout variant="warning" title="Stewart's key cautions on limits">
- **Never apply the Quotient Law until you check $\lim_{x \to a} g(x) \neq 0$:** If the denominator approaches $0$ while the numerator approaches a **non-zero** constant, the limit does not exist (infinite discontinuity); if **both** approach $0$, simplify algebraically!
- **Radians only in calculus:** $\lim_{\theta \to 0} \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} = 1$ requires $\theta$ in **radians**.
</Callout>
