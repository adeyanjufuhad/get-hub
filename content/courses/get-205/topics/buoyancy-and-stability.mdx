## 1. Overview

Why does a solid steel bolt sink instantly while a 10,000-tonne steel cargo ship floats upright across the Atlantic? The answer lies in **buoyancy** (how much fluid weight is displaced) and **rotational stability** (whether a small tilt generates a restoring couple or an overturning couple).

In GET 205 exams, buoyancy questions almost always combine **vertical force equilibrium** ($F_B = W$) with **metacentric height** ($GM$) stability checks on barges, pontoons, or wooden cylinders.

---

## 2. Key Concepts

### Archimedes' Principle

Any body wholly or partly submerged in a static fluid experiences an upward **buoyant force** $F_B$ equal to the weight of the fluid it displaces:

$$F_B = \rho_f \, g \, V_{\text{sub}}$$

- $\rho_f$ is the density of the **surrounding fluid** ($\text{kg/m}^3$), *not* the solid body.
- $V_{\text{sub}}$ is the **submerged volume** ($\text{m}^3$).
- $F_B$ acts vertically upward through the **centre of buoyancy ($B$)**, which is the centroid of the displaced fluid volume.

For a **freely floating homogeneous body** of density $\rho_b$ and total volume $V$, vertical equilibrium ($F_B = W$) yields the classic submerged fraction ratio:

$$\frac{V_{\text{sub}}}{V} = \frac{\rho_b}{\rho_f} = \frac{SG_b}{SG_f}$$

### Stability of Submerged vs. Floating Bodies

- **Fully submerged body** (e.g., submarine, underwater sensor pod):
  - **Stable** if the centre of gravity $G$ lies **below** the centre of buoyancy $B$.
  - **Unstable** if $G$ lies above $B$.
- **Floating body** (e.g., pontoon, ship hull):
  - Even if $G$ is above $B$, the body can still be **stable** because tilting shifts the submerged wedge and moves $B$ laterally toward the dipped side!
  - The intersection of the new buoyant line of action with the body's original vertical centreline is the **metacentre ($M$)**.

The **metacentric radius** $BM$ depends on the second moment of area $I_{\min}$ of the **waterplane** (the horizontal cross-section sliced at the waterline) about its least-resistant tilting axis:

$$BM = \frac{I_{\min}}{V_{\text{sub}}}$$

And the **metacentric height** $GM$ is:

$$GM = BM - BG$$

where $BG = y_G - y_B$ is the vertical distance from $B$ up to $G$:
- **$GM > 0$** ($M$ above $G$): **Stable** — small tilts produce a restoring moment $M_R = W \cdot GM \sin\theta$.
- **$GM = 0$**: **Neutral** equilibrium.
- **$GM < 0$** ($M$ below $G$): **Unstable** — the body capsizes.

<Callout variant="tip" title="Which axis gives I_min for a rectangular barge?">
A rectangular pontoon of length $L$ and beam width $b$ ($b < L$) rolls most easily about its **longitudinal axis**, so always use $I_{\min} = \frac{L b^3}{12}$ (cube the *narrower* dimension!).
</Callout>

## 4. Common Exam Pitfalls

<Callout variant="warning" title="Watch out for these 3 traps">
1. **Using body density instead of fluid density in $F_B$:** Buoyancy always equals the weight of *displaced fluid*, $\rho_f g V_{\text{sub}}$.
2. **Cubing the wrong dimension in $I_{\min}$:** For a barge of width $b$ and length $L$, rolling stability uses $Lb^3/12$, never $bL^3/12$.
3. **Forgetting to halve the draft for $y_B$:** For a vertical-sided pontoon floating at draft $d$, the centre of buoyancy is at $y_B = d/2$ from the bottom—not $H/2$.
</Callout>
