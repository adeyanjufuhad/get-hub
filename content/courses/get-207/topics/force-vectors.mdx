## Overview

In **Beer & Johnston, Chapter 2 (*Statics of Particles*)**, we analyze the effect of forces acting on a **particle**—meaning any body whose size and shape do not significantly affect the solution, so all forces acting on it can be treated as concurrent at a single point of application. The chapter moves systematically from two-dimensional (**coplanar**) force systems (§2.2–2.11) to three-dimensional (**space**) force systems (§2.12–2.15), establishing both graphical/trigonometric tools and analytic unit-vector methods.

---

### 2.1 Introduction

Two fundamental tasks govern particle statics:
1. **Composition (Resultant):** Replacing two or more forces acting on a particle by a single equivalent force $\mathbf{R}$—the **resultant**—having the same mechanical effect on the particle.
2. **Equilibrium:** Determining unknown forces when the resultant of all forces acting on the particle is zero ($\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{0}$).

Throughout this chapter, every force acting on a given particle shares the same point of application and is completely specified by its **magnitude** and **direction**.

---

### 2.2 Force on a Particle. Resultant of Two Forces

A **force** represents the action of one body on another. It is characterized by its **point of application**, **magnitude** (measured in newtons $\text{N}$ or kilonewtons $\text{kN} = 1000\,\text{N}$ in SI units, and pounds $\text{lb}$ or kilopounds $\text{kip} = 1000\,\text{lb}$ in U.S. customary units), and **direction**. Direction comprises two parts:
- **Line of action:** The infinite straight line along which the force acts, defined by the angle it forms with a fixed reference axis.
- **Sense:** Indicated by an arrowhead; reversing the sense produces the directly opposite mechanical effect.

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="§2.2 Parallelogram Law for the Addition of Two Forces">

Two forces $\mathbf{P}$ and $\mathbf{Q}$ acting on a particle $A$ can be replaced by a single **resultant** force $\mathbf{R}$ obtained by constructing a parallelogram with $\mathbf{P}$ and $\mathbf{Q}$ as adjacent sides. The diagonal of the parallelogram passing through $A$ represents $\mathbf{R}$:

$$
\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{P} + \mathbf{Q}
$$

This law is established by experimental evidence; it cannot be proved mathematically.

</RuleBox>

### 2.3 Vectors

Physical quantities that possess **both magnitude and direction** and **obey the parallelogram law of addition** are represented mathematically as **vectors** (forces, displacements, velocities, accelerations, momenta). Quantities possessing magnitude only (volume, mass, energy) are **scalars**.

- **Notation:** In print, vectors are set in **boldface** ($\mathbf{P}$) and their magnitudes in *italics* ($P$); in handwriting, an over-arrow ($\vec{P}$) or underline ($\underline{P}$) is used.
- **Classification of Vectors:**
  - *Fixed (bound) vector:* Has a well-defined point of application (such as a force acting on a particle) and cannot be moved without altering the problem.
  - *Free vector:* May be moved freely in space (such as a couple vector in Ch. 3).
  - *Sliding vector:* May be slid along its line of action (such as a force acting on a rigid body in Ch. 3).
- **Equal and Negative Vectors:** Two vectors with the same magnitude and direction are **equal**, regardless of point of application. The **negative vector** $-\mathbf{P}$ has the same magnitude as $\mathbf{P}$ but opposite sense, so $\mathbf{P} + (-\mathbf{P}) = \mathbf{0}$.

<Callout variant="info" title="Why Finite Rotations Are Not Vectors">

Possessing magnitude and direction is **necessary but not sufficient** for a quantity to be a vector. Finite rotations of a rigid body (e.g., two $180^\circ$ rotations about perpendicular axes, Beer & Johnston Fig. 2.3) depend on the order of application and do not obey the parallelogram law; therefore finite rotations are **not** vectors.

</Callout>

### 2.4 Addition of Vectors

Because vectors add by the parallelogram law, vector addition is **commutative**:

$$
\mathbf{P} + \mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{Q} + \mathbf{P} \tag{2.1}
$$

Note carefully that the magnitude $|\mathbf{P} + \mathbf{Q}|$ is **not**, in general, equal to the scalar sum $P + Q$.

<RuleBox kind="working-rule" title="§2.4 Triangle Rule, Vector Subtraction, and Polygon Rule">

- **Triangle Rule:** Arrange $\mathbf{P}$ and $\mathbf{Q}$ in **tip-to-tail** fashion; the vector extending from the tail of $\mathbf{P}$ to the tip of $\mathbf{Q}$ equals $\mathbf{P} + \mathbf{Q}$ (half of the parallelogram).
- **Vector Subtraction:** Defined as adding the negative vector:
  $$
  \mathbf{P} - \mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{P} + (-\mathbf{Q}) \tag{2.2}
  $$
- **Associative Law for Three or More Vectors:**
  $$
  \mathbf{P} + \mathbf{Q} + \mathbf{S} = (\mathbf{P} + \mathbf{Q}) + \mathbf{S} = \mathbf{P} + (\mathbf{Q} + \mathbf{S}) \tag{2.4}
  $$
  Vector addition is both commutative and associative, so the order of summation is immaterial.
- **Polygon Rule (Coplanar Vectors):** Arrange all given coplanar vectors tip-to-tail in any order; the vector from the tail of the first to the tip of the last is the sum.
- **Scalar Multiplication:** The product $k\mathbf{P}$ of a scalar $k$ and vector $\mathbf{P}$ has magnitude $|k|P$, the same direction as $\mathbf{P}$ if $k > 0$, and the opposite direction if $k < 0$.

</RuleBox>

### 2.5 Resultant of Several Concurrent Forces

When several coplanar forces $\mathbf{P}, \mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{S}, \dots$ act at the same particle $A$ (i.e., they are **coplanar** and **concurrent**), their resultant $\mathbf{R}$ is obtained directly by applying the polygon rule in any convenient order.

---

### 2.6 Resolution of a Force into Components

Conversely, a single force $\mathbf{F}$ acting on a particle may be replaced by two or more **components** whose combined effect equals $\mathbf{F}$. While infinitely many component pairs exist, two practical cases dominate:

<RuleBox kind="working-rule" title="§2.6 Two Standard Cases of Force Resolution">

1. **One Component ($\mathbf{P}$) Is Known:** Apply the triangle rule by joining the tip of $\mathbf{P}$ to the tip of $\mathbf{F}$; the closing side gives the second component $\mathbf{Q}$ (Fig. 2.16).
2. **Line of Action of Each Component Is Known:** Apply the parallelogram law by drawing lines through the tip of $\mathbf{F}$ parallel to the two known lines of action (Fig. 2.17). Solve the resulting force triangle graphically or via the **law of sines**.

When solving oblique force triangles by trigonometry:
- Use the **law of cosines** ($R^2 = P^2 + Q^2 - 2PQ\cos B$) first when **two sides and the included angle** are known.
- Use the **law of sines** ($\dfrac{P}{\sin\alpha} = \dfrac{Q}{\sin\beta} = \dfrac{R}{\sin\gamma}$) first when **one side and all angles** are known.

</RuleBox>

### 2.7 Rectangular Components of a Force. Unit Vectors

In most engineering problems it is advantageous to resolve a force $\mathbf{F}$ into two **rectangular components** $\mathbf{F}_x$ and $\mathbf{F}_y$ along perpendicular $x$ and $y$ axes (either horizontal/vertical or tilted along an incline). Introducing unit vectors $\mathbf{i}$ and $\mathbf{j}$ of magnitude $1$ directed along the positive $x$ and $y$ axes:

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§2.7 Rectangular Components & Unit Vectors (2D)">

$$
\mathbf{F}_x = F_x\mathbf{i}, \qquad \mathbf{F}_y = F_y\mathbf{j} \tag{2.6}
$$

$$
\mathbf{F} = F_x\mathbf{i} + F_y\mathbf{j} \tag{2.7}
$$

Here $F_x$ and $F_y$ are the **scalar components** of $\mathbf{F}$ (positive when in the $+x$ or $+y$ sense, negative when opposite). If $\theta$ is measured counterclockwise from the positive $x$ axis to $\mathbf{F}$:

$$
F_x = F\cos\theta, \qquad F_y = F\sin\theta \tag{2.8}
$$

Conversely, given $F_x$ and $F_y$, the direction angle $\theta$ and magnitude $F$ are found from:

$$
\tan\theta = \frac{F_y}{F_x} \tag{2.9}
$$

$$
F = \sqrt{F_x^2 + F_y^2} \tag{2.10}
$$

</RuleBox>

### 2.8 Addition of Forces by Summing X and Y Components

When three or more coplanar forces act on a particle, a trigonometric solution from the force polygon is impractical. Instead, we resolve every force into rectangular components and add the scalar components algebraically. For $\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{P} + \mathbf{Q} + \mathbf{S}$:

$$
R_x\mathbf{i} + R_y\mathbf{j} = (P_x + Q_x + S_x)\mathbf{i} + (P_y + Q_y + S_y)\mathbf{j}
$$

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§2.8 Resultant by Summing Rectangular Components">

$$
R_x = \sum F_x, \qquad R_y = \sum F_y \tag{2.13}
$$

Then $\mathbf{R} = R_x\mathbf{i} + R_y\mathbf{j}$, with magnitude $R = \sqrt{R_x^2 + R_y^2}$ and direction angle $\theta$ given by $\tan\theta = R_y / R_x$ (placed in the proper quadrant according to the signs of $R_x$ and $R_y$).

</RuleBox>

### 2.9 Equilibrium of a Particle

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§2.9 Equilibrium of a Particle (2D)">

When the resultant of all forces acting on a particle is zero, the particle is in **equilibrium**:

$$
\mathbf{R} = \sum\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0} \tag{2.14}
$$

Resolving into rectangular components yields $(\sum F_x)\mathbf{i} + (\sum F_y)\mathbf{j} = \mathbf{0}$, so the necessary and sufficient algebraic conditions for equilibrium of a particle in a plane are:

$$
\sum F_x = 0, \qquad \sum F_y = 0 \tag{2.15}
$$

</RuleBox>

- **Two-force equilibrium:** Two forces are in equilibrium if and only if they have the same magnitude, the same line of action, and opposite sense.
- **Graphical meaning:** When arranged tip-to-tail, the forces acting on a particle in equilibrium form a **closed polygon** (for 3 forces, a **closed force triangle**).

---

### 2.10 Newton's First Law of Motion

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="§2.10 Newton's First Law of Motion">

If the resultant force acting on a particle is zero, the particle will remain at rest (if originally at rest) or will move with constant speed in a straight line (if originally in motion).

</RuleBox>

Both static rest and uniform rectilinear motion satisfy $\sum\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$ and are treated identically in particle statics.

### 2.12 Rectangular Components of a Force in Space

In three dimensions, a force $\mathbf{F}$ acting at origin $O$ is resolved into three mutually perpendicular components $\mathbf{F}_x, \mathbf{F}_y, \mathbf{F}_z$ along the $x, y, z$ axes (with right-handed unit vectors $\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k}$).

Two equivalent ways define $\mathbf{F}$ in space:
1. **Using elevation angle $\theta_y$ and azimuth angle $\phi$ (Fig. 2.30):** First project $\mathbf{F}$ onto the vertical $y$ axis and horizontal $xz$ plane:
   $$
   F_y = F\cos\theta_y, \qquad F_h = F\sin\theta_y \tag{2.16}
   $$
   Then resolve $F_h$ in the $xz$ plane:
   $$
   F_x = F_h\cos\phi = F\sin\theta_y\cos\phi, \qquad F_z = F_h\sin\phi = F\sin\theta_y\sin\phi \tag{2.17}
   $$
2. **Using coordinate angles $\theta_x, \theta_y, \theta_z$ (Fig. 2.31):** Measured from the **positive** $x, y, z$ axes ($0^\circ \le \theta_x, \theta_y, \theta_z \le 180^\circ$). An acute angle ($< 90^\circ$) gives a positive component; an obtuse angle ($> 90^\circ$) gives a negative component.

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§2.12 3D Rectangular Components & Direction Cosines">

$$
F = \sqrt{F_x^2 + F_y^2 + F_z^2} \tag{2.18}
$$

$$
F_x = F\cos\theta_x, \qquad F_y = F\cos\theta_y, \qquad F_z = F\cos\theta_z \tag{2.19}
$$

$$
\mathbf{F} = F_x\mathbf{i} + F_y\mathbf{j} + F_z\mathbf{k} = F(\cos\theta_x\,\mathbf{i} + \cos\theta_y\,\mathbf{j} + \cos\theta_z\,\mathbf{k}) = F\boldsymbol{\lambda} \tag{2.20–2.21}
$$

The unit vector $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ along the line of action of $\mathbf{F}$ has components equal to the **direction cosines** $\cos\theta_x, \cos\theta_y, \cos\theta_z$, which satisfy the fundamental identity:

$$
\cos^2\theta_x + \cos^2\theta_y + \cos^2\theta_z = 1 \tag{2.24}
$$

Given $F_x, F_y, F_z$, the direction angles are obtained from:

$$
\cos\theta_x = \frac{F_x}{F}, \qquad \cos\theta_y = \frac{F_y}{F}, \qquad \cos\theta_z = \frac{F_z}{F} \tag{2.25}
$$

</RuleBox>

### 2.13 Force Defined by Its Magnitude and Two Points on Its Line of Action

When the line of action of a force $\mathbf{F}$ passes from point $M(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ toward point $N(x_2, y_2, z_2)$ (same sense as $\mathbf{F}$), form the displacement vector $\overrightarrow{MN}$ and normalize it to obtain the unit vector $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$.

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§2.13 Unit Vector from Two Points M → N">

$$
\overrightarrow{MN} = d_x\mathbf{i} + d_y\mathbf{j} + d_z\mathbf{k}, \qquad d_x = x_2 - x_1,\; d_y = y_2 - y_1,\; d_z = z_2 - z_1 \tag{2.26}
$$

$$
d = |\overrightarrow{MN}| = \sqrt{d_x^2 + d_y^2 + d_z^2}
$$

$$
\boldsymbol{\lambda} = \frac{\overrightarrow{MN}}{MN} = \frac{1}{d}(d_x\mathbf{i} + d_y\mathbf{j} + d_z\mathbf{k}) \tag{2.27}
$$

$$
\mathbf{F} = F\boldsymbol{\lambda} = \frac{F}{d}(d_x\mathbf{i} + d_y\mathbf{j} + d_z\mathbf{k}) \implies F_x = \frac{Fd_x}{d},\; F_y = \frac{Fd_y}{d},\; F_z = \frac{Fd_z}{d} \tag{2.28–2.29}
$$

$$
\cos\theta_x = \frac{d_x}{d}, \qquad \cos\theta_y = \frac{d_y}{d}, \qquad \cos\theta_z = \frac{d_z}{d} \tag{2.30}
$$

</RuleBox>

### 2.14 Addition of Concurrent Forces in Space

For concurrent forces in 3D space, graphical and trigonometric methods are generally impractical. Instead, resolve every force into $x, y, z$ rectangular components and sum them algebraically:

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§2.14 Resultant of Concurrent Forces in Space">

$$
R_x = \sum F_x, \qquad R_y = \sum F_y, \qquad R_z = \sum F_z \tag{2.31}
$$

$$
R = \sqrt{R_x^2 + R_y^2 + R_z^2} \tag{2.32}
$$

$$
\cos\theta_x = \frac{R_x}{R}, \qquad \cos\theta_y = \frac{R_y}{R}, \qquad \cos\theta_z = \frac{R_z}{R} \tag{2.33}
$$

</RuleBox>

### 2.15 Equilibrium of a Particle in Space

A particle in 3D space is in equilibrium when the resultant of all forces acting on it is zero ($\sum\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}$), giving three independent scalar equilibrium equations:

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="§2.15 Equations of Equilibrium in Space (Max 3 Unknowns)">

$$
\sum F_x = 0, \qquad \sum F_y = 0, \qquad \sum F_z = 0 \tag{2.34}
$$

These three scalar equations can be solved for **at most three unknowns**—typically the magnitudes of three forces of known line of action, or the three rectangular components of a single unknown force.

</RuleBox>

## Common Exam Pitfalls

<Callout variant="warning" title="Five Frequent Errors in Chapter 2 Statics of Particles">

1. **Wrong sense of the displacement vector $\overrightarrow{MN}$ in 3D:** Always form $\overrightarrow{MN} = (x_2 - x_1)\mathbf{i} + (y_2 - y_1)\mathbf{j} + (z_2 - z_1)\mathbf{k}$ from the **point of application** toward the point the force pulls or pushes *toward*. Reversing $M$ and $N$ flips all three component signs!
2. **Confusing azimuth/elevation angles ($\theta_y, \phi$) with direction angles ($\theta_x, \theta_y, \theta_z$):** Only angles measured directly from the positive $x, y, z$ axes to the force vector itself satisfy $\cos^2\theta_x + \cos^2\theta_y + \cos^2\theta_z = 1$. If an angle lies in a tilted or horizontal plane (like $\phi$), project through $F_h = F\sin\theta_y$ first.
3. **Forgetting mass-to-weight conversion in SI:** A $75\\text{-kg}$ crate has weight $W = mg = 75(9.81) = 736\\,\\text{N}$, whereas a $3500\\text{-lb}$ automobile is already given as a force (weight).
4. **Assuming perpendicular construction lines for oblique components:** In §2.6, to resolve $\mathbf{F}$ along two non-perpendicular lines of action, draw lines through the tip of $\mathbf{F}$ **parallel** (not perpendicular!) to the two lines of action.
5. **Assuming different tensions on each side of a frictionless pulley or ring:** When a continuous cable passes over a frictionless pulley or through a frictionless ring (e.g., Probs. 2.53, 2.89, 2.121), the scalar tension $T$ is the **same** in both segments, though their unit vectors $\boldsymbol{\lambda}_1$ and $\boldsymbol{\lambda}_2$ differ.

</Callout>

## Summary of Formulas

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="Beer & Johnston 10e Ch. 2 — Complete Formula Reference">

- **Parallelogram & Triangle Laws (2D):** $\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{P} + \mathbf{Q}$; Law of cosines: $R^2 = P^2 + Q^2 - 2PQ\cos B$; Law of sines: $\dfrac{P}{\sin\alpha} = \dfrac{Q}{\sin\beta} = \dfrac{R}{\sin\gamma}$.
- **2D Rectangular Components & Resultant:**
  $$
  \mathbf{F} = F_x\mathbf{i} + F_y\mathbf{j}, \quad F_x = F\cos\theta,\; F_y = F\sin\theta, \quad F = \sqrt{F_x^2 + F_y^2}, \quad \tan\theta = \frac{F_y}{F_x}
  $$
  $$
  R_x = \sum F_x, \qquad R_y = \sum F_y
  $$
- **2D Particle Equilibrium:** $\sum F_x = 0,\; \sum F_y = 0$ (max 2 unknowns; or closed force triangle for 3 forces).
- **3D Rectangular Components & Direction Cosines:**
  $$
  \mathbf{F} = F_x\mathbf{i} + F_y\mathbf{j} + F_z\mathbf{k} = F(\cos\theta_x\,\mathbf{i} + \cos\theta_y\,\mathbf{j} + \cos\theta_z\,\mathbf{k}) = F\boldsymbol{\lambda}
  $$
  $$
  F = \sqrt{F_x^2 + F_y^2 + F_z^2}, \qquad \cos\theta_x = \frac{F_x}{F},\; \cos\theta_y = \frac{F_y}{F},\; \cos\theta_z = \frac{F_z}{F}
  $$
  $$
  \cos^2\theta_x + \cos^2\theta_y + \cos^2\theta_z = 1
  $$
- **Force from Two Points $M \to N$:**
  $$
  \boldsymbol{\lambda} = \frac{d_x\mathbf{i} + d_y\mathbf{j} + d_z\mathbf{k}}{d}, \quad d = \sqrt{d_x^2 + d_y^2 + d_z^2}, \quad F_x = \frac{Fd_x}{d},\; F_y = \frac{Fd_y}{d},\; F_z = \frac{Fd_z}{d}
  $$
- **3D Resultant & Particle Equilibrium:**
  $$
  R_x = \sum F_x,\; R_y = \sum F_y,\; R_z = \sum F_z \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sum F_x = 0,\; \sum F_y = 0,\; \sum F_z = 0
  $$

</RuleBox>
