## Overview

Engineering quantities almost never depend on a single variable alone: the area of a rectangle depends on both length and breadth, the pressure of an ideal gas depends on temperature and volume, and the deflection of a rectangular plate depends on two spatial coordinates $(x, y)$. Chapter 1 of H. K. Dass opens partial differentiation by establishing the foundation for functions of two variables: what $z = f(x, y)$ represents geometrically (§1.1), how a two-variable limit behaves across infinitely many approach paths (§1.2–1.3), when a function is continuous at a point or over a domain (§1.4–1.5), and how discontinuities are classified into five distinct kinds (§1.6). Every definition, working rule, solved example, and exercise set below follows Dass §1.1–1.6 in exact order.

### 1.1 Introduction

Consider the area of a rectangle: it is determined jointly by its length and its breadth, so area is a function of two variables. More generally, whenever a quantity $z$ takes one definite value for every ordered pair $(x, y)$ in a region, we say that $z$ is a **function of the two variables $x$ and $y$**, and write

$$z = f(x, y).$$

Here $x$ and $y$ are the **independent variables** and $z$ is the **dependent variable**. The same idea extends immediately to functions of three or more independent variables, such as $u = f(x, y, z)$.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§1.1 Function of two variables and its geometrical meaning">

A variable $z$ is called a **function of two variables** $x$ and $y$, written $z = f(x, y)$, if $z$ has one definite value for every pair of values $(x, y)$ in a domain (area) $A$ of the $xy$-plane.

- **Independent variables:** $x, y$
- **Dependent variable:** $z$
- **Geometrical interpretation:** To each point $(x, y)$ in the area $A$ of the $xy$-plane corresponds a height $z = f(x, y)$. The collection of all points $(x, y, z)$ traces out a **surface in three-dimensional space**.

</RuleBox>

### 1.2 Limit

For a single-variable function $f(x)$, the point $x$ can approach $a$ along the real line from only two directions (the left or the right). In the $xy$-plane, however, a moving point $(x, y)$ can approach $(a, b)$ along **infinitely many paths**—coordinate lines, straight lines of any slope, parabolas, spirals, or arbitrary curves. A two-variable limit exists only when every single path produces the exact same limiting value $l$.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§1.2 Limit of a function of two variables">

**Path-independence form:** The function $f(x, y)$ is said to tend to the limit $l$ as $x \to a$ and $y \to b$ if and only if the limit $l$ is independent of the path followed by the point $(x, y)$ as $x \to a$ and $y \to b$. Symbolically,

$$\lim_{\substack{x \to a \\ y \to b}} f(x, y) = l.$$

**$\varepsilon$–$\delta$ form:** The function $f(x, y)$ defined in a region $R$ is said to tend to the limit $l$ as $x \to a$ and $y \to b$ if and only if, corresponding to any positive number $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists another positive number $\delta > 0$ such that

$$|f(x, y) - l| < \varepsilon \qquad \text{whenever} \qquad 0 < (x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2 < \delta^2$$

for every point $(x, y)$ in $R$.

</RuleBox>

Notice the strict inequality $0 < (x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2 < \delta^2$: the condition applies at every point inside the open disk of radius $\delta$ centered at $(a, b)$, **excluding the center $(a, b)$ itself**. Thus the existence and value of the limit do not depend on whether $f(a, b)$ is defined or what value it takes.

### 1.3 Working Rule to Find the Limit

To test and evaluate a simultaneous limit $\lim_{\substack{x \to a \\ y \to b}} f(x, y)$ systematically, H. K. Dass gives the following working procedure, starting with the two iterated limits and (at the origin) progressing through linear and curved approach families.

<RuleBox kind="working-rule" title="§1.3 Working rule to find the limit">

**General procedure for $(x, y) \to (a, b)$:**

1. **Step 1:** Find the value of $f(x, y)$ along $x \to a$ first and then $y \to b$.
2. **Step 2:** Find the value of $f(x, y)$ along $y \to b$ first and then $x \to a$. If the values in Step 1 and Step 2 are different, the limit does not exist.
3. **Step 3:** When $a = 0$ and $b = 0$, evaluate the limit along straight lines $y = mx$ and higher-degree curves $y = mx^n$. If the resulting expression depends on $m$, the limit does not exist.

**Four-stage check at the origin $(0, 0)$ (Dass Note):**

- **(i)** Put $x = 0$ and then let $y \to 0$ in $f(x, y)$ to obtain $f_1$.
- **(ii)** Put $y = 0$ and then let $x \to 0$ in $f(x, y)$ to obtain $f_2$. If $f_1 \neq f_2$, the limit does not exist.
- **(iii)** If $f_1 = f_2$, put $y = mx$ and find the limit $f_3$. If $f_3$ depends on $m$ or $f_3 \neq f_1$, the limit does not exist.
- **(iv)** If $f_1 = f_2 = f_3$, put $y = mx^2$ and find the limit $f_4$. If $f_4$ depends on $m$ or $f_4 \neq f_1$, the limit does not exist.

</RuleBox>

<Callout variant="info" title="Showing non-existence vs. proving existence">

Path substitution ($x = 0$, $y = 0$, $y = mx$, $y = mx^2$, or another curve $y = g(x)$) is a **definitive test for non-existence**: finding even two paths that give different limits (or a family whose limit depends on the parameter $m$) immediately proves that the simultaneous limit does not exist.

When all tested paths agree on a candidate value $l$, confirming that the limit truly exists for *every* possible path in $0 < (x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2 < \delta^2$ is done either by direct substitution (when $f$ is a polynomial or a rational function with a non-zero denominator at $(a, b)$) or by showing $|f(x, y) - l| \to 0$ uniformly—for instance using polar coordinates $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$ as $r \to 0$.

</Callout>

### 1.4 Continuity

Just as in single-variable calculus, continuity of a surface $z = f(x, y)$ at a point $(a, b)$ means that the surface has no hole, jump, or break above $(a, b)$: the limiting height as $(x, y)$ approaches $(a, b)$ from every direction equals the actual function height $f(a, b)$.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§1.4 Continuity of a function of two variables">

A function $f(x, y)$ is said to be **continuous at a point $(a, b)$** if

$$\lim_{(x, y) \to (a, b)} f(x, y) = f(a, b).$$

A function is said to be **continuous in a domain** if it is continuous at every point of that domain.

</RuleBox>

### 1.5 Working Rule for Continuity at a Point (a, b)

To test whether a given function $f(x, y)$ is continuous at $(a, b)$, verify all three conditions in sequence. If any one condition fails, $f(x, y)$ is discontinuous at $(a, b)$.

<RuleBox kind="working-rule" title="§1.5 Working rule for continuity at a point (a, b)">

- **Step 1:** $f(a, b)$ must be well defined (finite).
- **Step 2:** The simultaneous limit $\displaystyle \lim_{(x, y) \to (a, b)} f(x, y)$ must exist (and be independent of the approach path).
- **Step 3:** The limit must equal the value of the function at the point:

$$\lim_{(x, y) \to (a, b)} f(x, y) = f(a, b).$$

</RuleBox>

### 1.6 Types of Discontinuity

When a function $f(x)$ fails to be continuous at a point $x = x_1$, the nature of the failure is classified by examining its **left-hand limit**

$$f(x_1 - 0) = \lim_{h \to 0^+} f(x_1 - h)$$

and its **right-hand limit**

$$f(x_1 + 0) = \lim_{h \to 0^+} f(x_1 + h),$$

together with the function value $f(x_1)$. H. K. Dass (§1.6; *Gujarat Univ. I sem. Jan. 2009*) classifies discontinuities into **five kinds**.

#### 1. Discontinuity of the First Kind (Jump Discontinuity)

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§1.6 (1) Discontinuity of the First Kind">

A function $f(x)$ is said to have a **discontinuity of the first kind** at $x = x_1$ if both the right-hand limit $f(x_1 + 0)$ and the left-hand limit $f(x_1 - 0)$ exist (and are finite), **but are not equal**:

$$f(x_1 - 0) \neq f(x_1 + 0).$$

</RuleBox>

For example, $f(x) = \dfrac{|x|}{x}$ at $x_1 = 0$ has $f(0 - 0) = -1$ and $f(0 + 0) = +1$; both one-sided limits exist finitely, yet they differ by a jump of $2$.

<DiagramFigure
  src="/diagrams/get-209/limits-and-continuity-first-kind.png"
  caption="H. K. Dass §1.6 — Discontinuity of the First Kind at x₁: both f(x₁ − 0) and f(x₁ + 0) exist finitely, but f(x₁ − 0) ≠ f(x₁ + 0)."
/>

#### 2. Discontinuity of the Second Kind

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§1.6 (2) Discontinuity of the Second Kind">

A function $f(x)$ is said to have a **discontinuity of the second kind** at $x = x_1$ if **neither** the right-hand limit $f(x_1 + 0)$ **nor** the left-hand limit $f(x_1 - 0)$ exists.

</RuleBox>

A standard example is $f(x) = \sin\!\left(\dfrac{1}{x - x_1}\right)$, which oscillates infinitely often between $-1$ and $+1$ on both sides of $x_1$, so neither $f(x_1 - 0)$ nor $f(x_1 + 0)$ exists.

<DiagramFigure
  src="/diagrams/get-209/limits-and-continuity-second-kind.png"
  caption="H. K. Dass §1.6 — Discontinuity of the Second Kind at x₁: neither f(x₁ − 0) nor f(x₁ + 0) exists."
/>

#### 3. Discontinuity of the Third Kind (Mixed Discontinuity)

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§1.6 (3) Discontinuity of the Third Kind (Mixed Discontinuity)">

A function $f(x)$ is said to have a **mixed discontinuity** (discontinuity of the third kind) at $x = x_1$ if **only one** of the two one-sided limits $f(x_1 + 0)$ and $f(x_1 - 0)$ exists and the other does not.

- **Third kind (i):** $f(x_1 - 0)$ exists, whereas $f(x_1 + 0)$ does not exist.
- **Third kind (ii):** $f(x_1 + 0)$ exists, whereas $f(x_1 - 0)$ does not exist.

</RuleBox>

<DiagramFigure
  src="/diagrams/get-209/limits-and-continuity-third-kind.png"
  caption="H. K. Dass §1.6 — Discontinuity of the Third Kind (Mixed): (i) one one-sided limit exists while the other does not; (ii) the reverse case."
/>

#### 4. Discontinuity of the Fourth Kind (Infinite Discontinuity)

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§1.6 (4) Discontinuity of the Fourth Kind (Infinite Discontinuity)">

A function $f(x)$ is said to have an **infinite discontinuity** (discontinuity of the fourth kind) at $x = x_1$ if **either one or both** of the right-hand limit $f(x_1 + 0)$ and the left-hand limit $f(x_1 - 0)$ is infinite.

Additionally, if both one-sided limits fail to exist and $f(x_1 \pm h)$ oscillates between bounds at least one of which is infinite as $h \to 0$, then $x = x_1$ is also a point of infinite discontinuity.

</RuleBox>

For instance, $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{(x - x_1)^2}$ has $f(x_1 - 0) = f(x_1 + 0) = +\infty$, while $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x - x_1}\sin\!\left(\dfrac{1}{x - x_1}\right)$ oscillates with unbounded amplitude as $x \to x_1$.

<DiagramFigure
  src="/diagrams/get-209/limits-and-continuity-fourth-kind.png"
  caption="H. K. Dass §1.6 — Discontinuity of the Fourth Kind (Infinite): one or both one-sided limits are infinite at x₁."
/>

#### 5. Discontinuity of the Fifth Kind (Removable Discontinuity)

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="§1.6 (5) Discontinuity of the Fifth Kind (Removable Discontinuity)">

A function $f(x)$ is said to have a **removable discontinuity** (discontinuity of the fifth kind) at $x = x_1$ if the right-hand limit $f(x_1 + 0)$ equals the left-hand limit $f(x_1 - 0)$ (both finite), **but this common value is not equal to $f(x_1)$** (or $f(x_1)$ is undefined):

$$f(x_1 - 0) = f(x_1 + 0) \neq f(x_1).$$

</RuleBox>

It is called *removable* because simply redefining $f(x_1)$ to equal the common limit $f(x_1 - 0) = f(x_1 + 0)$ removes the discontinuity and makes the function continuous at $x_1$.

  caption="H. K. Dass §1.6 — Discontinuity of the Fifth Kind (Removable): f(x₁ − 0) = f(x₁ + 0) exists finitely, but differs from f(x₁)."

#### Quick Comparison of the Five Kinds of Discontinuity

| Kind | Name in H. K. Dass | Condition on $f(x_1 - 0)$, $f(x_1 + 0)$, and $f(x_1)$ |
|---|---|---|
| **1st Kind** | First kind (jump) | Both $f(x_1 - 0)$ and $f(x_1 + 0)$ exist finitely, and $f(x_1 - 0) \neq f(x_1 + 0)$ |
| **2nd Kind** | Second kind | Neither $f(x_1 - 0)$ nor $f(x_1 + 0)$ exists |
| **3rd Kind** | Mixed discontinuity | Exactly one of $f(x_1 - 0)$, $f(x_1 + 0)$ exists and the other does not |
| **4th Kind** | Infinite discontinuity | At least one of $f(x_1 - 0)$, $f(x_1 + 0)$ is $\pm\infty$ (or oscillates unboundedly) |
| **5th Kind** | Removable discontinuity | $f(x_1 - 0) = f(x_1 + 0)$ (finite), but $f(x_1 - 0) = f(x_1 + 0) \neq f(x_1)$ |

## Common Exam Pitfalls

<Callout variant="warning" title="Frequent mistakes in §1.1–1.6 exam problems">

- **Stopping after straight lines $y = mx$:** As Example 1 ($f = \dfrac{x^2 y}{x^4 + y^2}$) and Exercise 1.2 Q3 ($f = \dfrac{x^3 y^3}{x^3 + y^3}$) show, a function can approach $0$ along every coordinate axis and every straight line $y = mx$, yet still have **no limit** along a parabola $y = mx^2$ or a curve hugging a zero of the denominator! Always match the powers of $x$ and $y$ in the denominator when picking test curves.
- **Confusing iterated limits with simultaneous limits:** Equality of $\lim_{x \to a}\lim_{y \to b} f(x, y)$ and $\lim_{y \to b}\lim_{x \to a} f(x, y)$ is *necessary* (when both exist) for the simultaneous limit to exist, but **not sufficient**.
- **Forgetting $|x|$ when taking square roots:** In Example 6, $\sqrt{x^2} = |x|$, not $x$. Writing $\frac{x}{|x|} = \pm 1$ immediately exposes the directional dependence even along $y = 0$.
- **Assuming continuity implies partial derivatives exist:** Exercise 1.2 Q8 proves the opposite—a function of two variables can be continuous at $(0, 0)$ even while $f_x(0, 0)$ and $f_y(0, 0)$ both fail to exist.
- **Mixing up the five discontinuity kinds:** Remember that the **first kind** requires *both* one-sided limits to exist finitely (but differ), the **second kind** means *neither* exists, the **third (mixed) kind** means *exactly one* exists, the **fourth kind** involves an *infinite* limit or unbounded oscillation, and the **fifth (removable) kind** means both limits exist and are *equal*, differing only from $f(x_1)$.

</Callout>

## Summary of Formulas

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="Chapter 1 (§1.1–1.6) Formula & Condition Summary">

- **Function of two variables (§1.1):** $z = f(x, y)$, representing a surface over a domain $A$ in the $xy$-plane.
- **$\varepsilon$–$\delta$ limit definition (§1.2):**

$$\lim_{\substack{x \to a \\ y \to b}} f(x, y) = l \iff \forall\,\varepsilon > 0,\;\exists\,\delta > 0 \text{ such that } 0 < (x - a)^2 + (y - b)^2 < \delta^2 \implies |f(x, y) - l| < \varepsilon.$$

- **Origin path-test sequence (§1.3):**
  1. $f_1$: set $x = 0$, let $y \to 0$;
  2. $f_2$: set $y = 0$, let $x \to 0$;
  3. $f_3$: set $y = mx$, let $x \to 0$;
  4. $f_4$: set $y = mx^2$ (or $y = mx^n$ / custom curve matching denominator zeroes), let $x \to 0$.
  If any two differ or depend on $m$, the limit does not exist.
- **Continuity at $(a, b)$ (§1.4–1.5):** $f(a, b)$ is defined, $\displaystyle \lim_{(x, y) \to (a, b)} f(x, y)$ exists, and

$$\lim_{(x, y) \to (a, b)} f(x, y) = f(a, b).$$

- **Five kinds of discontinuity at $x = x_1$ (§1.6):**
  1. **First kind:** $f(x_1 - 0)$ and $f(x_1 + 0)$ exist finitely, $f(x_1 - 0) \neq f(x_1 + 0)$.
  2. **Second kind:** neither $f(x_1 - 0)$ nor $f(x_1 + 0)$ exists.
  3. **Third kind (mixed):** exactly one of $f(x_1 - 0)$, $f(x_1 + 0)$ exists.
  4. **Fourth kind (infinite):** at least one of $f(x_1 - 0)$, $f(x_1 + 0)$ is infinite (or $f(x_1 \pm h)$ oscillates unboundedly).
  5. **Fifth kind (removable):** $f(x_1 - 0) = f(x_1 + 0)$ finitely, but $\neq f(x_1)$.

</RuleBox>
