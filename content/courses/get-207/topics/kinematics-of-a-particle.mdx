## Overview

Earlier topics dealt with **statics** — bodies at rest. This topic opens **dynamics**, the part of mechanics that deals with bodies in motion. Dynamics is split into two parts: **kinematics**, the study of the *geometry of motion* (how position, velocity, acceleration and time are related, without reference to the cause of the motion), and **kinetics**, the study of the relation between the forces acting on a body, its mass and its motion.

This chapter treats the kinematics of **particles**. A body may be treated as a particle whenever its size and rotation are irrelevant to the question asked — an aircraft over a 500-km route, a projectile, a car on a highway. The chapter has two halves:

- **Rectilinear motion**: the particle moves along a straight line. Position, velocity and acceleration are scalars with a sign. We learn to integrate the defining equations for the three common types of acceleration law, to use the uniform and uniformly accelerated formulas, to handle several particles (relative and dependent motion), and to read motion from graphs.
- **Curvilinear motion**: the particle moves along a curve. Position, velocity and acceleration become vectors, and we express them in **rectangular** components (projectiles), relative to a **translating frame**, in **tangential and normal** components, and in **radial and transverse** components.

All twelve Sample Problems of the chapter are worked in full below, followed by selected end-of-section problems with the standard answers.

## Rectilinear Motion of Particles

### 11.1 Introduction to Dynamics

Dynamics was developed much later than statics; Galileo (1564–1642) carried out the first significant experiments on uniformly accelerated bodies, and Newton (1642–1727) formulated the fundamental laws of motion. Kinematics is used to relate displacement, velocity, acceleration and time; kinetics is used to predict the motion caused by given forces or to find the forces needed to produce a given motion.

In this topic, bodies are treated as **particles** — their size is ignored and the motion of the whole body is represented by the motion of a single point (usually the mass centre). Rigid-body kinematics, where rotation matters, is covered in rigid-body kinematics.

---

### 11.2 Position, Velocity, and Acceleration

A particle moving along a straight line is in **rectilinear motion**. At any instant $t$ it occupies a position on the line. To define that position $P$ we choose a fixed origin $O$ on the line and a positive direction. The distance $x$ from $O$ to $P$, with the appropriate sign, is the **position coordinate** of the particle. A positive $x$ means $P$ is on the positive side of $O$; a negative $x$ means it is on the negative side. When $x$ is known for every value of $t$, the motion of the particle is known; this "timetable" may be given as an equation such as $x = 6t^2 - t^3$ or as an $x$–$t$ graph.

If the particle moves from $P$ (coordinate $x$) at time $t$ to $P'$ (coordinate $x + \Delta x$) at time $t + \Delta t$, the **average velocity** over the interval is $\Delta x/\Delta t$, and the **instantaneous velocity** $v$ is its limit as $\Delta t \to 0$.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="Velocity and acceleration in rectilinear motion">

$$
v = \frac{dx}{dt} \tag{11.1}
$$

$$
a = \frac{dv}{dt} \tag{11.2}
$$

$$
a = \frac{d^2x}{dt^2} \tag{11.3}
$$

Eliminating $dt$ between (11.1) and (11.2) gives the alternative form

$$
a = v\,\frac{dv}{dx} \tag{11.4}
$$

- $v$ is an algebraic number: $v > 0$ means $x$ is increasing (particle moving in the positive direction); $v < 0$ means it moves in the negative direction. The magnitude of $v$ is the **speed**.
- $a > 0$ means the velocity *increases* algebraically — the particle is speeding up if moving in the positive direction, or slowing down if moving in the negative direction. **Deceleration** is reserved for a decrease in speed, which happens whenever $a$ and $v$ have opposite signs.
- Units: $v$ in $\text{m/s}$ or $\text{ft/s}$ (also $\text{km/h}$, $\text{mi/h}$); $a$ in $\text{m/s}^2$ or $\text{ft/s}^2$.

</RuleBox>

the standard introductory illustration, $x = 6t^2 - t^3$, gives $v = 12t - 3t^2$ and $a = 12 - 6t$. The particle starts from $x = 0$ with $v = 0$ and $a = 12\,\text{m/s}^2$; it moves in the positive direction until $t = 4\,\text{s}$ (where $v = 0$ and $x = 32\,\text{m}$, the maximum), then reverses and moves in the negative direction with ever-increasing speed. Its velocity is maximum at $t = 2\,\text{s}$, when $a = 0$. The three motion curves — $x$–$t$, $v$–$t$ and $a$–$t$ — are related: the velocity at any instant is the **slope** of the $x$–$t$ curve, and the acceleration is the slope of the $v$–$t$ curve.

### 11.3 Determination of the Motion of a Particle

In practice the motion is rarely given as $x(t)$. Usually the *acceleration* is specified — by the conditions of the problem or by the forces acting — together with initial conditions $x_0$, $v_0$ at $t = 0$. The motion is then found by **two successive integrations**. Three common classes of acceleration law arise.

<RuleBox kind="working-rule" title="The three classes of motion">

**1. $a = f(t)$ — acceleration a given function of time.** From (11.2), $dv = f(t)\,dt$:

$$
\int_{v_0}^{v} dv = \int_0^t f(t)\,dt \;\Rightarrow\; v - v_0 = \int_0^t f(t)\,dt
$$

then from (11.1), $dx = v\,dt$:

$$
x - x_0 = \int_0^t v\,dt
$$

**2. $a = f(x)$ — acceleration a given function of position.** Use (11.4) and separate variables, $v\,dv = f(x)\,dx$:

$$
\tfrac12 v^2 - \tfrac12 v_0^2 = \int_{x_0}^{x} f(x)\,dx
$$

which gives $v$ in terms of $x$; then $dt = dx/v$ gives $t$ in terms of $x$.

**3. $a = f(v)$ — acceleration a given function of velocity.** Either use (11.2), $dt = \dfrac{dv}{f(v)}$, giving $t - 0 = \displaystyle\int_{v_0}^{v} \frac{dv}{f(v)}$; or use (11.4), $dx = \dfrac{v\,dv}{f(v)}$, giving $x - x_0 = \displaystyle\int_{v_0}^{v}\frac{v\,dv}{f(v)}$. Either relation, combined with (11.1), yields the motion.

</RuleBox>

Definite integrals are used so that the initial conditions enter automatically and no separate constant of integration needs to be found.

### 11.4 Uniform Rectilinear Motion

In **uniform rectilinear motion** the acceleration is zero for every $t$; the velocity is therefore constant, $dx/dt = v = \text{constant}$. Integrating from $(0, x_0)$ to $(t, x)$:

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="Uniform rectilinear motion (a = 0, v = constant)">

$$
x = x_0 + vt \tag{11.5}
$$

This equation may be used **only** when the velocity is known to be constant.

</RuleBox>

---

### 11.5 Uniformly Accelerated Rectilinear Motion

In **uniformly accelerated rectilinear motion** the acceleration $a$ is constant. Integrating $dv/dt = a$ from $(0, v_0)$ gives (11.6); substituting into $dx/dt = v$ and integrating again gives (11.7); and integrating $v\,dv/dx = a$ from $(x_0, v_0)$ gives (11.8).

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="Uniformly accelerated rectilinear motion (a = constant)">

$$
v = v_0 + at \tag{11.6}
$$

$$
x = x_0 + v_0 t + \tfrac12 a t^2 \tag{11.7}
$$

$$
v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a(x - x_0) \tag{11.8}
$$

Before using them, define the origin $O$ and the positive direction; the signs of $a$, $v_0$ and $x_0$ follow from that choice. Equation (11.6) relates $v$ and $t$ (use it when $x$ is neither given nor wanted); (11.7) relates $x$ and $t$; (11.8) relates $v$ and $x$. An important application is **free fall**: neglecting air resistance, a body near the earth's surface has the constant acceleration $g = 9.81\,\text{m/s}^2 = 32.2\,\text{ft/s}^2$ directed downward.

</RuleBox>

### 11.6 Motion of Several Particles

When several particles move independently along the **same line**, a separate equation of motion is written for each. Whenever possible, **time should be recorded from the same initial instant for all particles and displacements measured from the same origin and in the same direction**.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="Relative motion of two particles on the same line">

With $x_A$ and $x_B$ measured from the same origin, the **relative position coordinate** of $B$ with respect to $A$ is

$$
x_{B/A} = x_B - x_A \quad\text{or}\quad x_B = x_A + x_{B/A} \tag{11.9}
$$

Differentiating once and twice:

$$
v_{B/A} = v_B - v_A \quad\text{or}\quad v_B = v_A + v_{B/A} \tag{11.10}
$$

$$
a_{B/A} = a_B - a_A \quad\text{or}\quad a_B = a_A + a_{B/A} \tag{11.11}
$$

A positive $x_{B/A}$ means $B$ is on the positive side of $A$; a positive $v_{B/A}$ means $B$, as seen from $A$, moves in the positive direction. Note the memory aid: the product of subscripts $A$ and $B/A$ on the right equals the subscript $B$ on the left.

</RuleBox>

<RuleBox kind="working-rule" title="Dependent motions (cables and pulleys)">

When the position of one particle depends on the positions of others — typically blocks connected by an inextensible cable over pulleys — write the **constant-length condition** in terms of position coordinates measured from fixed points. For the single block-and-pulley system of the standard the diagram,

$$
x_A + 2x_B = \text{constant} \qquad (\text{one degree of freedom})
$$

and for the three blocks of the diagram,

$$
2x_A + 2x_B + x_C = \text{constant} \qquad (\text{two degrees of freedom})
$$

Because such a relation is **linear**, the same relation holds between the velocities and between the accelerations:

$$
2v_A + 2v_B + v_C = 0, \qquad 2a_A + 2a_B + a_C = 0
$$

The number of coordinates that can be chosen arbitrarily is the number of **degrees of freedom** of the system.

</RuleBox>

### 11.7 Graphical Solution of Rectilinear-Motion Problems

The defining equations $v = dx/dt$ and $a = dv/dt$ have a direct geometric meaning: the **velocity equals the slope of the $x$–$t$ curve** and the **acceleration equals the slope of the $v$–$t$ curve**. Integrating the same formulas from $t_1$ to $t_2$:

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="Area properties of the motion curves">

$$
x_2 - x_1 = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} v\,dt \qquad\text{and}\qquad v_2 - v_1 = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} a\,dt \tag{11.12}
$$

- The **area under the $v$–$t$ curve** between $t_1$ and $t_2$ equals the change in $x$ over that interval.
- The **area under the $a$–$t$ curve** between $t_1$ and $t_2$ equals the change in $v$ over that interval.
- Areas below the $t$ axis are negative.

</RuleBox>

These properties let us construct the $v$–$t$ curve from a known $a$–$t$ curve (and the $x$–$t$ curve from the $v$–$t$ curve), step by step. They are especially useful when the acceleration is piecewise constant, so that the curves are made of straight segments and parabolic arcs. When the $a$–$t$ curve is a horizontal segment, the $v$–$t$ curve is a straight segment and the $x$–$t$ curve is a parabolic arc; when $a = 0$, $v$ is constant and the $x$–$t$ curve is a straight line.

### 11.8 Other Graphical Methods

The **moment-area method** gives the position directly from the $a$–$t$ curve. Writing the area under the $v$–$t$ curve from $0$ to $t_1$ as a rectangle $v_0 t_1$ plus horizontal strips of area $(t_1 - t)\,dv$, and substituting $dv = a\,dt$:

$$
x_1 - x_0 = v_0 t_1 + \int_0^{t_1} (t_1 - t)\,a\,dt
$$

The integral is the **first moment of the area under the $a$–$t$ curve about the line $t = t_1$**. If $\bar t$ is the abscissa of the centroid $C$ of that area:

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="Moment-area method">

$$
x_1 = x_0 + v_0 t_1 + (\text{area under } a\text{–}t \text{ curve})(t_1 - \bar t) \tag{11.13}
$$

For a composite area, multiply each component area by the distance from its centroid to the line $t = t_1$; areas above the $t$ axis are positive and areas below are negative.

</RuleBox>

A second method uses the **$v$–$x$ curve**: if $AC$ is the normal to the $v$–$x$ curve at a point $A$, the subnormal $BC$ (measured along the $x$ axis) equals the acceleration, $BC = a$, provided the same scale is used for $v$ and $x$ — because $a = v\,dv/dx$ and the slope of $AC$ is $-dx/dv$.

## Curvilinear Motion of Particles

### 11.9 Position Vector, Velocity, and Acceleration

When a particle moves along a curve other than a straight line it is in **curvilinear motion**. Its position $P$ at time $t$ is defined by the **position vector** $\mathbf{r}$ joining a fixed origin $O$ to $P$. If the particle moves to $P'$, defined by $\mathbf{r}'$, at time $t + \Delta t$, the vector $\Delta\mathbf{r}$ joining $P$ and $P'$ represents the change in position — direction as well as magnitude.

<RuleBox kind="definition" title="Velocity and acceleration in curvilinear motion">

$$
\mathbf{v} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0}\frac{\Delta \mathbf{r}}{\Delta t} \tag{11.14}
$$

$$
\mathbf{v} = \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt} \tag{11.15}
$$

The velocity $\mathbf{v}$ is a vector **tangent to the path**. Its magnitude, the **speed**, is the rate of change of the arc length $s$:

$$
v = \frac{ds}{dt} \tag{11.16}
$$

$$
\mathbf{a} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0}\frac{\Delta \mathbf{v}}{\Delta t} \tag{11.17}
$$

$$
\mathbf{a} = \frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt} \tag{11.18}
$$

The acceleration is **not**, in general, tangent to the path. Plotting the velocity vectors from a common origin $O'$, their tips describe the **hodograph** of the motion; the acceleration is tangent to the hodograph.

</RuleBox>

---

### 11.10 Derivatives of Vector Functions

A vector $\mathbf{P}(u)$ that depends on a scalar variable $u$ has the derivative

$$
\frac{d\mathbf{P}}{du} = \lim_{\Delta u \to 0}\frac{\mathbf{P}(u + \Delta u) - \mathbf{P}(u)}{\Delta u} \tag{11.19}
$$

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="Rules for differentiating vector functions">

$$
\frac{d(\mathbf{P} + \mathbf{Q})}{du} = \frac{d\mathbf{P}}{du} + \frac{d\mathbf{Q}}{du} \tag{11.20}
$$

$$
\frac{d(f\mathbf{P})}{du} = \frac{df}{du}\,\mathbf{P} + f\,\frac{d\mathbf{P}}{du} \tag{11.21}
$$

$$
\frac{d(\mathbf{P}\cdot\mathbf{Q})}{du} = \frac{d\mathbf{P}}{du}\cdot\mathbf{Q} + \mathbf{P}\cdot\frac{d\mathbf{Q}}{du} \tag{11.22}
$$

$$
\frac{d(\mathbf{P}\times\mathbf{Q})}{du} = \frac{d\mathbf{P}}{du}\times\mathbf{Q} + \mathbf{P}\times\frac{d\mathbf{Q}}{du} \tag{11.23}
$$

(In the vector product the order of the factors must be kept.) With $\mathbf{P} = P_x\mathbf{i} + P_y\mathbf{j} + P_z\mathbf{k}$ (11.24) in a fixed frame, the unit vectors are constant and

$$
\frac{d\mathbf{P}}{du} = \frac{dP_x}{du}\mathbf{i} + \frac{dP_y}{du}\mathbf{j} + \frac{dP_z}{du}\mathbf{k} \tag{11.25}
$$

$$
\dot{\mathbf{P}} = \dot P_x\mathbf{i} + \dot P_y\mathbf{j} + \dot P_z\mathbf{k} \tag{11.25'}
$$

</RuleBox>

The rate of change of a vector as observed from a **fixed** frame and from a **rotating** frame are, in general, different; Eq. (11.25') applies to a frame whose axes do not rotate.

### 11.11 Rectangular Components of Velocity and Acceleration

With $\mathbf{r} = x\mathbf{i} + y\mathbf{j} + z\mathbf{k}$ (11.26) and fixed unit vectors:

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="Rectangular components">

$$
\mathbf{v} = \dot x\,\mathbf{i} + \dot y\,\mathbf{j} + \dot z\,\mathbf{k} \tag{11.27}
$$

$$
\mathbf{a} = \ddot x\,\mathbf{i} + \ddot y\,\mathbf{j} + \ddot z\,\mathbf{k} \tag{11.28}
$$

$$
v_x = \dot x \quad v_y = \dot y \quad v_z = \dot z \tag{11.29}
$$

$$
a_x = \ddot x \quad a_y = \ddot y \quad a_z = \ddot z \tag{11.30}
$$

</RuleBox>

The great advantage of rectangular components is that **the motion in each coordinate direction can be studied separately** whenever $a_x$ depends only on $t$, $x$ and/or $v_x$, and similarly for $a_y$ and $a_z$.

**Motion of a projectile.** Neglecting air resistance, the only force on a projectile is its weight, so $a_x = \ddot x = 0$, $a_y = \ddot y = -g$, $a_z = \ddot z = 0$. With the origin at the launch point and initial velocity components $(v_x)_0$, $(v_y)_0$, $(v_z)_0 = 0$:

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="Projectile motion (y vertical, positive upward)">

$$
v_x = (v_x)_0 \qquad v_y = (v_y)_0 - gt \qquad v_z = 0
$$

$$
x = (v_x)_0\,t \qquad y = (v_y)_0\,t - \tfrac12 g t^2 \qquad z = 0
$$

The horizontal motion is **uniform** and the vertical motion is **uniformly accelerated**; the motion takes place in a vertical plane and the trajectory is a **parabola**. The two motions can be treated as two independent rectilinear motions linked only by the common time $t$.

</RuleBox>

### 11.12 Motion Relative to a Frame in Translation

Sometimes it is convenient to describe the motion of a particle $B$ relative to a frame $Ax'y'z'$ attached to another moving particle $A$, whose axes remain **parallel** to those of the fixed frame $Oxyz$ (a frame **in translation**).

<RuleBox kind="theorem" title="Relative motion — frame in translation">

$$
\mathbf{r}_B = \mathbf{r}_A + \mathbf{r}_{B/A} \tag{11.31}
$$

$$
\dot{\mathbf{r}}_B = \dot{\mathbf{r}}_A + \dot{\mathbf{r}}_{B/A} \tag{11.32}
$$

$$
\mathbf{v}_B = \mathbf{v}_A + \mathbf{v}_{B/A} \tag{11.33}
$$

$$
\mathbf{a}_B = \mathbf{a}_A + \mathbf{a}_{B/A} \tag{11.34}
$$

$\mathbf{v}_{B/A}$ and $\mathbf{a}_{B/A}$ are the velocity and acceleration of $B$ **relative to** the frame $Ax'y'z'$ (or, briefly, relative to $A$). The motion of $B$ with respect to the fixed frame is the **absolute motion**. These equations hold **only** if the moving frame does not rotate. Since the vector addition can be done graphically, each equation can be solved with a vector triangle.

</RuleBox>

### 11.13 Tangential and Normal Components

The velocity is tangent to the path, but the acceleration usually is not. It is often convenient to resolve the acceleration along the tangent and the normal to the path. For **plane motion**, attach to the particle the unit vector $\mathbf{e}_t$ tangent to the path (in the direction of motion) and the unit vector $\mathbf{e}_n$ along the normal, **directed toward the center of curvature** of the path. As the particle moves from $P$ to $P'$ the tangent rotates through $\Delta\theta$, and one finds

$$
\mathbf{e}_n = \frac{d\mathbf{e}_t}{d\theta} \tag{11.35}
$$

Writing $\mathbf{v} = v\,\mathbf{e}_t$ (11.36) and differentiating, $\mathbf{a} = \dfrac{dv}{dt}\mathbf{e}_t + v\dfrac{d\mathbf{e}_t}{dt}$ (11.37). With $ds = \rho\,d\theta$ and $ds/dt = v$, $\dfrac{d\mathbf{e}_t}{dt} = \dfrac{v}{\rho}\mathbf{e}_n$ (11.38).

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="Tangential and normal components of acceleration">

$$
\mathbf{a} = \frac{dv}{dt}\,\mathbf{e}_t + \frac{v^2}{\rho}\,\mathbf{e}_n \tag{11.39}
$$

$$
a_t = \frac{dv}{dt} \qquad a_n = \frac{v^2}{\rho} \tag{11.40}
$$

- $a_t$ measures the rate of change of the **speed**; $a_n$ measures the rate of change of the **direction** of the velocity.
- $a_n$ is always directed toward the center of curvature; $a_t$ points in the direction of motion if the speed increases and opposite to it if the speed decreases.
- If the speed is constant, $a_t = 0$ and the acceleration is purely normal. At a point of inflection ($\rho = \infty$) or in rectilinear motion, $a_n = 0$.
- When $a_t$ is constant, the uniformly-accelerated formulas apply along the path with $a$ replaced by $a_t$.

</RuleBox>

The fact that $a_n$ does not vanish while the speed is constant explains why high-speed highway curves and railway tracks are designed with gradually increasing curvature (transition curves) and why a passenger feels a lateral force on a curve. For a particle moving along a **space curve**, the plane containing $\mathbf{e}_t$ and $\mathbf{e}_n$ is the **osculating plane**; the third unit vector $\mathbf{e}_b = \mathbf{e}_t \times \mathbf{e}_n$ defines the **binormal**, and since the acceleration lies in the osculating plane, Eq. (11.39) still holds with no binormal component.

### 11.14 Radial and Transverse Components

In some plane-motion problems the position of $P$ is defined by the polar coordinates $r$ and $\theta$. It is then convenient to resolve velocity and acceleration along the unit vector $\mathbf{e}_r$ (directed along $OP$, away from $O$) and the unit vector $\mathbf{e}_\theta$ (obtained by rotating $\mathbf{e}_r$ through $90^\circ$ counterclockwise). These are the **radial** and **transverse** components. Unlike $\mathbf{i}$ and $\mathbf{j}$, the unit vectors $\mathbf{e}_r$ and $\mathbf{e}_\theta$ change direction as $P$ moves:

$$
\frac{d\mathbf{e}_r}{d\theta} = \mathbf{e}_\theta \qquad \frac{d\mathbf{e}_\theta}{d\theta} = -\mathbf{e}_r \tag{11.41}
$$

$$
\dot{\mathbf{e}}_r = \dot\theta\,\mathbf{e}_\theta \qquad \dot{\mathbf{e}}_\theta = -\dot\theta\,\mathbf{e}_r \tag{11.42}
$$

Differentiating $\mathbf{r} = r\,\mathbf{e}_r$ twice using these results:

<RuleBox kind="formula" title="Radial and transverse components">

$$
\mathbf{v} = \dot r\,\mathbf{e}_r + r\dot\theta\,\mathbf{e}_\theta \tag{11.43}
$$

$$
\mathbf{a} = (\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2)\,\mathbf{e}_r + (r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta)\,\mathbf{e}_\theta \tag{11.44}
$$

$$
v_r = \dot r \qquad v_\theta = r\dot\theta \tag{11.45}
$$

$$
a_r = \ddot r - r\dot\theta^2 \qquad a_\theta = r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta \tag{11.46}
$$

**Circular motion** about $O$ ($r$ constant, $\dot r = \ddot r = 0$):

$$
\mathbf{v} = r\dot\theta\,\mathbf{e}_\theta \qquad \mathbf{a} = -r\dot\theta^2\,\mathbf{e}_r + r\ddot\theta\,\mathbf{e}_\theta \tag{11.47}
$$

$a_r$ is **not** equal to $\ddot r$, and $a_\theta$ is **not** equal to $r\ddot\theta$. Positive $\dot\theta$ means counterclockwise rotation.

</RuleBox>

**Extension to three dimensions — cylindrical coordinates.** With $\mathbf{r} = R\,\mathbf{e}_R + z\,\mathbf{k}$ (11.48):

$$
\mathbf{v} = \dot R\,\mathbf{e}_R + R\dot\theta\,\mathbf{e}_\theta + \dot z\,\mathbf{k} \tag{11.49}
$$

$$
\mathbf{a} = (\ddot R - R\dot\theta^2)\,\mathbf{e}_R + (R\ddot\theta + 2\dot R\dot\theta)\,\mathbf{e}_\theta + \ddot z\,\mathbf{k} \tag{11.50}
$$

## Common Exam Pitfalls

<Callout variant="warning" title="Frequent mistakes in particle kinematics">

1. **Distance traveled is not displacement.** Whenever $v$ changes sign inside the interval (Sample Problem 11.1(d)), find the instant where $v = 0$, compute each leg separately and **add the magnitudes**. Using $x_6 - x_4$ alone would give $2\,\text{ft}$ instead of $18\,\text{ft}$.
2. **Using the constant-acceleration formulas when $a$ is not constant.** Equations (11.6)–(11.8) hold only for constant $a$. If $a = f(t)$, $f(x)$ or $f(v)$, integrate — and choose $a = v\,dv/dx$ when position, not time, is involved.
3. **Inconsistent signs and origins.** Fix one origin and one positive direction for **all** particles and measure time from the same instant. The sign of $g$ follows from your axis: $a = -9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ when $y$ is positive upward.
4. **Rejecting the wrong root.** Quadratics in $t$ give a negative root that precedes the start of the motion — discard it, but keep both roots when both are physical (two firing angles in Sample Problem 11.8).
5. **Dependent motion: forgetting the factor 2.** Each cable segment that lengthens with a coordinate contributes that coordinate once; a pulley supported by two segments contributes $2x_D$. The same linear relation then applies to velocities and accelerations.
6. **Treating $a_n$ as zero on a curve at constant speed.** At constant speed $a_t = 0$, but $a_n = v^2/\rho \neq 0$. Conversely, $a_t$ alone does not give the total acceleration.
7. **Radial–transverse confusion.** $a_r = \ddot r - r\dot\theta^2$ and $a_\theta = r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta$; the acceleration **relative to the arm** is only $\ddot r$ (Sample Problem 11.12(c)). Always convert $\theta$ to radians before differentiating.

</Callout>
